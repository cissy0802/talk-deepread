A story doesn't wound you by being false. It wounds you by being the only one

Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie · "The danger of a single story"

At seven, on a university campus in eastern Nigeria, a girl began writing stories in pencil with crayon illustrations, which her poor mother was obligated to read. Her characters were all white and blue-eyed. They played in the snow, ate apples, and talked a great deal about the weather, about how lovely it was that the sun had come out.

She had never left Nigeria. It does not snow there; they ate mangoes; and they never discussed the weather, because there was no need to. Her characters also drank a great deal of ginger beer, because the children in the British books drank ginger beer. "Never mind that I had no idea what ginger beer was." The room laughed. For years afterwards, she said, she carried a desperate desire to taste it.

In July 2009, a thirty-one-year-old Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie opened nineteen minutes on the TEDGlobal stage in Oxford with that joke. She offered no theory. She told three stories, each turning the screw one notch further. Because the only books she could find were foreign, she became convinced that books by their very nature had to be about foreigners. Because all she had ever heard about the family of Fide, the houseboy, was how poor they were, she was startled speechless by a beautifully patterned basket his brother had made — it had never occurred to her that anybody in that family could make something. And at nineteen in America, a roommate asked to hear her "tribal music," and was disappointed when she produced a tape of Mariah Carey. "She had felt sorry for me even before she saw me." Then she turned the knife on herself: walking through Guadalajara watching Mexicans go to work, roll tortillas, laugh, she was overwhelmed with shame — she had been so immersed in American coverage that Mexicans had become one thing in her mind too.

That is the real architecture of this talk: it is not an indictment, it is a confession. And its sharpest move is that it never claims the negative stories are false. She says plainly that Africa is full of catastrophe — the rapes in Congo, five thousand people applying for one job in Nigeria, her close friend Okoloma dead in a plane crash "because our fire trucks did not have water." Her point is a different one:

The single story creates stereotypes, and the problem with stereotypes is not that they are untrue, but that they are incomplete.

And what decides whose story counts is power. She reaches for an Igbo word, nkali, which she says loosely translates to "to be greater than another": "Power is the ability not just to tell the story of another person, but to make it the definitive story of that person."

So what became of it? The talk has been played 42.8 million times and has found its way onto every slide from provincial school curricula to corporate diversity training — and then it was flattened into a single story of itself. What survives is "diverse narratives are good." What gets dropped is the hardest part: that the catastrophe stories are true, and that she was a perpetrator too. The sharper irony arrived in Paris in January 2018, when a French journalist asked the author of "The Danger of a Single Story," on stage, whether there are bookshops in Nigeria. And after 2017 the machine that flattens people into one thing turned on her: in 2023, a teacher argued on the National Council of Teachers of English blog that the talk should no longer be shown in classrooms, because of what its author is.

The moment

Ginger beer. The funniest and the deadliest thing in the whole talk is ginger beer.

She started writing at about seven, in pencil, with crayon illustrations "that my poor mother was obligated to read." What she wrote was exactly what she read:

All my characters were white and blue-eyed, they played in the snow, they ate apples, (Laughter) and they talked a lot about the weather, how lovely it was that the sun had come out.— Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, TEDGlobal 2009

Laughter. She waits for it to fall away, then lands the second blow: all of this despite the fact that she lived in Nigeria and had never been outside it. No snow. They ate mangoes. They never talked about the weather, "because there was no need to." Her characters also drank a great deal of ginger beer — because the children in the British books drank ginger beer. "Never mind that I had no idea what ginger beer was."

The room laughs. And then comes the line that actually chills: for many years afterwards, she carried "a desperate desire" to taste ginger beer. The child had not only copied someone else's world, she had grown homesick for it. That is the entire talk, delivered first as a joke.

The speaker, and the year

Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, born 1977, grew up on the Nsukka campus of the University of Nigeria. In her own words from the stage: "My father was a professor. My mother was an administrator." Underneath those two sentences sit harder specifics: her father, James Nwoye Adichie, was the university's professor of statistics; her mother, Grace Ifeoma, was its first female registrar. And the house the family lived in had previously been occupied by Chinua Achebe. Read that against the previous section: the girl whose imagination had been colonised by British children's books was rescued by Achebe's books — and she was living in Achebe's house. She does not mention this on stage; she says only that because of writers like Chinua Achebe and Camara Laye, she went through "a mental shift in my perception of literature." It is the private foundation under everything that follows.

On 23 July 2009, aged thirty-one, she gave this talk at TEDGlobal 2009 in the Oxford Playhouse, at a conference themed "The Substance of Things Not Seen." She was not a newcomer: Purple Hibiscus (2003) and Half of a Yellow Sun (2006) were out, the latter having won the 2007 Orange Prize, and she had taken a MacArthur "genius" grant in 2008. But she stood there as a novelist, not yet the global public intellectual — "We should all be feminists" was still three years away.

What matters more is the air of that year. In 2009 the default Western frame for Africa was still the hopeless continent; The Economist cover that swung the tone all the way round, "Africa Rising," would not appear until 2011, two years after this talk. She walked on while the pendulum was still pinned to one side — facing not a consensus that was loosening, but one that was setting hard.

The talk, in full

Her first words are "I'm a storyteller," followed immediately by notice that what she will give are "a few personal stories." She is not going to argue; she is going to testify, and the first exhibit is that ginger-beer childhood. The conclusion she draws from it is not "Western children's books are poison" but something more precise: because all she had read were books in which the characters were foreign, she had become convinced that books "by their very nature had to have foreigners in them and had to be about things with which I could not personally identify." The first casualty of a single story was her understanding of what a book is. What saved her was Achebe and Camara Laye. And she adds a scrupulous aside: she loved those American and British books; what went wrong was their "unintended consequence."

The second story moves her from victim to perpetrator. The year she turned eight the family took on a houseboy, Fide; the only thing her mother told her was that his family was very poor, and she used it to make her finish her dinner: "People like Fide's family have nothing." Then one Saturday they visited his village, and his mother showed them a beautifully patterned basket of dyed raffia that his brother had made.

It had not occurred to me that anybody in his family could actually make something. … Their poverty was my single story of them.— Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, TEDGlobal 2009

The third story moves her back to victim: at nineteen in America, a roommate asks where she learned to speak English so well, asks to hear her "tribal music," and gets a Mariah Carey tape (laughter). What she seizes is not the comedy but the structure beneath it — "She had felt sorry for me even before she saw me." In that single story there was no possibility of Africans being similar to her in any way, no possibility of feelings more complex than pity, no possibility of a connection as human equals.

Here she cuts from private history to history. Where does this single story of Africa come from? From Western literature, she says, and reads a passage from a sixteenth-century London merchant, John Lok, sailing to west Africa: Africans with no houses, and indeed no heads, their mouths and eyes in their breasts. (That passage is worth checking on its own; see the next section.) It marks the beginning of a tradition — sub-Saharan Africa as a place "of negatives, of difference, of darkness," peopled by Kipling's "half devil, half child." The tradition runs all the way to her: a professor told her that her novel was not "authentically African," because her characters drove cars and were not starving.

Then comes the best laugh in the talk. A student told her it was such a shame that Nigerian men were physical abusers like the father in her novel. She replied that she had just read a novel called American Psycho (laughter) and that it was such a shame that young Americans were serial murderers. (Laughter, applause.) And then she immediately dismantles her own joke, in the most honest moment of the evening:

This is not because I am a better person than that student, but because of America's cultural and economic power, I had many stories of America.— Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, TEDGlobal 2009

Then she puts the knife into herself: Mexico. On her first day in Guadalajara, watching people go to work, roll tortillas in the marketplace, smoke, laugh, "I remember first feeling slight surprise. And then, I was overwhelmed with shame." She had been immersed in the immigration coverage until Mexicans were one thing in her mind. Out of which comes the recipe: "So that is how to create a single story, show a people as one thing, as only one thing, over and over again, and that is what they become."

The last layer is power — the Igbo word nkali. Power is not just the ability to tell another person's story, but the ability to make it the definitive story of that person. She quotes the Palestinian poet Mourid Barghouti: if you want to dispossess a people, the simplest way is to tell their story and to start with "secondly."

And what is most often missed is that the talk refuses to deny the negative stories. "Of course, Africa is a continent full of catastrophes" — the horrific rapes in Congo, five thousand applicants for one vacancy; and her own ledger is open too: grandfathers who died in refugee camps, her close friend Okoloma dead in a plane crash "because our fire trucks did not have water." She does not want those erased. She wants the core judgement: the problem with a stereotype is not that it lies, but that it is one. So the ending is not an indictment but a long list of "what if my roommate knew" — her publisher, Nollywood, her wonderfully ambitious hair braider, and the woman working as a messenger at a Lagos TV station who read her novel and came to tell her how to write the sequel — landing at last on Alice Walker's "a kind of paradise was regained."

Under the iceberg

The passage in those nineteen minutes that looks most like hard evidence is also the one that holds up worst: John Lok.

First, the year is wrong. She says Lok "sailed to west Africa in 1561." But the quotation comes from Richard Hakluyt's The Principal Navigations, whose heading reads, in plain type: "The second voyage to Guinea … in the yere 1554. The Captaine whereof was M. John Lok." 1554, not 1561. What makes this interesting is that 1561 is not invented: the same collection does contain two other Lok documents dated Anno 1561 — sailing instructions delivered to him, and a letter he wrote to the merchants' company. She has the right man and the wrong voyage.

Second, and more importantly: that sentence is almost certainly not Lok's eyewitness report. Hakluyt's original spelling runs "people without heads, called Blemmyae, hauing their eyes and mouth in their brest"; the 1824 modernised edition has "people without heads, called Blemines, having their eyes and mouths in their breast." Adichie drops "called Blemmyae" when she reads it. That phrase is the key to the whole sentence — the Blemmyae are a famous monster of Greco-Roman geography. Open the surrounding context and it is plainer still: the line sits in a chain of citations to classical authorities, with Gemma Frisius on "Libia interior" as "large and desolate, containing many horrible wildernesses, replenished with various kinds of monstrous beasts and serpents," and the Troglodytes who "dwell in caves and dens, instead of houses, and feed upon the flesh of serpents, as is reported by Pliny and Diodorus Siculus."

In other words: Europeans were copying Africa out of Pliny before they got there. A sixteenth-century Englishman writing about Guinea was writing first-century Roman stock. The correction does not weaken her argument; it pushes it back another fifteen hundred years — she calls this "the beginning of a tradition," and the text suggests it is not even a beginning but a transcription. The cost is that her framing on stage ("he writes") is firmer than the text will bear. Incidentally, the phrase she quotes as "beasts who have no houses" does not correspond to any single sentence in the edition I could read; the nearest is "a beastly living people, without God, law, religion or government."

The second iceberg is where the argument comes from. On stage she credits "a balance of stories" explicitly to Achebe — it is the title of the third of the three essays in his Home and Exile (2000), drawn from his 1998 McMillan-Stewart Lectures at Harvard. The earlier source text is Achebe's 1975 Chancellor's Lecture at Amherst, An Image of Africa, where he argues that there is a desire — "one might indeed say the need" — in Western psychology to set Africa up as a foil to Europe, "a place of negations." Put that beside Adichie's phrase from the stage, "a place of negatives, of difference, of darkness," and you are looking at very nearly the same words. This talk is a teacher's thesis, compressed by his student into eighteen minutes, moved into the first person, and fitted with a ginger-beer punchline. She names her source. The chain of transmission remembered only the person at the podium.

One more detail she hedged and her readers unhedged: of nkali she says it "loosely translates to" — and in the countless retellings since, the "loosely" has vanished, and nkali gets quoted as a precise term.

What became of it

Nobody has overturned the descriptive core: a stereotype's defect really is incompleteness rather than falsehood, and the right to narrate really does follow power. What got flattened is the remedy — "hear a few more stories" was taken up as a mechanism for changing beliefs, and the best field experiment we have says beliefs are exactly what it does not change — and the talk itself: a warning against reducing people to one thing was first reduced to a diversity-training slide, and finally used to reduce its author.

First the scale. At the time of writing, TED's counter reads 42,865,398 plays. It is in school curricula, in freshman orientation, in corporate diversity and inclusion training. A nineteen-minute literary talk has become a general-purpose teaching aid — at the cost of being sanded smooth. The version on stage is far more careful than its users: she says the catastrophes are real, she says stereotypes are "not untrue," she spends a whole passage entering herself as defendant, and she warns that she could mock that student only because America's power had given her many stories of America — not because she was the better person. What usually survives on the slide is "diverse narratives are good."

And the causal chain everyone assumes — more stories → recognition of equal humanity → less prejudice — is precisely the testable part, and it is not what the test found. The most solid field experiment on the question is Elizabeth Levy Paluck's Rwanda study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2009, vol. 96, no. 3): 12 communities, 480 adults, communities matched in pairs and randomly assigned, running a full year, with research assistants bringing a portable stereo to each village every month to play four 20-minute episodes. The treatment group heard Musekeweya (New Dawn), a reconciliation soap opera; the control group heard Urunana, a health soap opera. The result: listeners' perceptions of social norms and their behaviour shifted on intermarriage, open dissent, trust, empathy, cooperation and trauma healing. But the sentence in the abstract reads:

However, the radio program did little to change listeners' personal beliefs.— Elizabeth Levy Paluck, J. Pers. Soc. Psychol. 96(3), 2009

One item even moved slightly the wrong way (not significantly): reconciliation listeners were less likely to believe that intermarriage contributes to peace. Paluck's reading is that stories change what people take their neighbours to expect, not what they privately believe — and norms drive behaviour more powerfully than beliefs do. So stories do work; they just do not come in through the door the talk implies. In fairness: Adichie says stories can empower and can repair dignity, not that they will. It is the users, not her, who read "can" as "must."

Then the most precise irony of all. Paris, the Night of Ideas, 25 January 2018: the French journalist Caroline Broué asked Adichie, on stage, whether there are bookshops in Nigeria. A murmur of disapproval went through the room. Her reply: "I think it reflects very poorly on the French people that you have to ask me that kind of question. I really do. Because I mean surely it's 2018…" Broué later explained that she had meant it ironically, to point up how Nigeria is discussed in French media only through Boko Haram and violence; Adichie afterwards acknowledged on Facebook that irony had been intended but "wasn't recognized." Either way — nine years after a talk that climaxes with a Lagos TV messenger who read her novel and came to tell her how to write the sequel, its author was asked to her face whether her country has bookshops.

Finally, the machine turned on her. In a 2017 interview she said that "trans women are trans women"; in 2021 she posted a long essay, "It Is Obscene," attacking cancel culture and naming former students; and since then, for a sizeable share of readers, she has been one thing. In April 2023 the National Council of Teachers of English published a blog post by the teacher Danelle Adeniji arguing that the talk should no longer be used in classrooms, on the grounds that its author's views conflict with her core value of "creating and upholding pro-Black, queer community spaces."

The rights and wrongs of that dispute are outside this page's range. What is worth recording is the shape: the woman who stood on that stage and said "show a people as one thing, as only one thing, over and over again, and that is what they become" is now, to a great many people, one thing. Her talk is still correct. It simply failed to protect anyone — including her.
