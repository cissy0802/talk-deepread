Oxford Inaugural Lecture · 1958

Forced to be free in the name of your "real self": Berlin's two liberties, and the line he was really defending

Isaiah Berlin · Two Concepts of Liberty

Oxford, 31 October 1958. In the text Isaiah Berlin sent to the publisher, after "Mr Vice-Chancellor," the first sentence is a thought experiment: if men had never disagreed about the ends of life, if our ancestors had stayed undisturbed in the Garden of Eden, the subject of the chair he had just taken up could hardly have been conceived. When ends are agreed, only means are left, and those are questions for engineers and doctors. Politics exists because people want different things.

He then recalled Heine's warning that ideas raised in the stillness of a professor's study could destroy a civilisation. If professors have that power, Berlin said, perhaps only other professors can disarm them.

The idea he set out to disarm was one everyone praises: freedom. The word has more than two hundred senses; he took two. The first answers "how large is the area in which I am left alone by others?" He called it negative liberty: nobody blocks the way, the doors are open. The second answers "who governs me?" He called it positive liberty: I want to be my own master, a subject and not an object.

They sound like two ways of saying the same thing. Berlin argued that they grew into enemies, and that the turn happened inside a metaphor. Being your own master implies a master and a mastered within you: a rational, "real" self above, desires and impulses below. One step further and the real self can be identified with something larger than the person: a tribe, a race, a church, a state. At that point whoever holds power can say: you resist only because you have not yet recognised what you truly want; in coercing you I am exercising the freedom of your true self. Berlin called this a "monstrous impersonation."

He did not say positive liberty was bad in itself. He dug one layer deeper. What sends people to the altar is an older belief: that all good things are ultimately compatible, that somewhere there is a final solution, so that any sacrifice of freedom that brings it closer is worth it. His reply was plain. Experience shows no such harmony: equality squeezes liberty, justice collides with mercy. Because we must choose between them, the freedom to choose matters.

The audience that day probably heard only half of it. The version read from the lectern was about half the length of the written text, and the text itself went through several drafts. One change matters a great deal. In the draft sent to the publisher, the ideal Berlin calls "truer and more humane" is negative liberty. In the text that circulates today, the same sentence begins with "pluralism, with the measure of 'negative' liberty that it entails." The line he was defending had moved from liberty to pluralism.

The verdict on its fate: partly held. In 1967 the American philosopher Gerald MacCallum argued that there is really only one concept of freedom, filled in differently. In 2001 the Cambridge historian Quentin Skinner argued that Berlin had missed a third: not living under anyone's arbitrary power, even if that power is never used. Later scholars also found serious and plausible objections to Berlin's history of Rousseau and the German philosophers. But nobody has overturned the core warning: be careful when someone tells you he knows what you really want better than you do, and is ready to use force on that basis.

The moment

In section III, Berlin asks the reader to imagine a wound.

I have a wound in my leg. There are two methods of freeing myself from pain. One is to heal the wound. But if the cure is too difficult or uncertain, there is another method. I can get rid of the wound by cutting off my leg.— Isaiah Berlin, Two Concepts of Liberty, 1958

This is his portrait of ascetics, Stoics and "Buddhist sages": if a desire cannot be met, cut out the desire; stop caring about your property and your family, and the tyrant can no longer touch you. Berlin grants that there is something noble here, then gives it a cruel name: "a sublime, but unmistakable, form of the doctrine of sour grapes." Retreat into the inner citadel, shrink the frontier again and again, and the logical end is suicide. The passage is short, but it is the whole lecture in miniature. What Berlin feared most was not open enemies of freedom. It was the road that starts in freedom's name, makes sense at every step, and ends at its opposite.

The speaker, and the year

Berlin was born in Riga in 1909. At eight he was living in Petrograd and watched both revolutions of 1917; in 1921 the family emigrated to Britain. During the war he wrote weekly reports on American politics for the British government in New York and Washington. In 1945–46 he spent four months in the Soviet Union and met the persecuted poets Anna Akhmatova and Boris Pasternak, which hardened his opposition to Communism. He wrote by dictating to a machine; his secretary typed it up, and he rewrote whole passages in small, spidery handwriting between the lines, in the margins and on the backs of pages.

He was elected to the Chichele chair in 1957 and knighted the same year; this was the inaugural lecture. It was the height of the Cold War, and the text opens by describing the world as divided by an open war between "two civilisations and two systems of ideas." New states were appearing across Asia and Africa, and section VI addresses them directly. In Oxford, ordinary-language philosophy reigned and political theory was a backwater; a visitor from Mars, Berlin joked, might think its universities still lived in Eden. The material was not new: the editor Henry Hardy traces the lecture directly to Berlin's early-1950s manuscript Political Ideas in the Romantic Age.

Glossary

  • Negative liberty: the size of the area in which others leave me alone. The question is "what may I do without being stopped?"
  • Positive liberty: whether I am my own master. The question is "who governs me, who decides?"
  • Monism: the belief that all true values are ultimately compatible and every question has one right answer.
  • Value pluralism: genuine values are many, they can conflict, and there is no common scale on which to rank them.

The talk, in full

Berlin starts from obedience. Why should I obey anyone else? If I disobey, may I be coerced? By whom, and in the name of what? To coerce a man is to deprive him of freedom. So what is freedom?

Section I is negative liberty: the less others interfere with me, the freer I am. The key word is "others." Being unable to jump ten feet, to read because I am blind, or to understand "the darker pages of Hegel" is inability, not unfreedom. Uncle Tom was a slave because a man named Simon Legree coerced him. Negative liberty needs a frontier between private life and public authority. Where it runs can be haggled over, because "freedom for the pike is death for the minnows." Berlin adds that this ideal is modern, and that it has no logical connection with democracy: a liberal-minded despot could leave his subjects a wide area of personal freedom. "Who governs me?" and "how far does government interfere with me?" are different questions.

Section II is positive liberty: I wish to be a subject, not an object, deciding and not decided for. Berlin treats this as a real and deep wish. The trouble lies in the momentum of the metaphor of self-mastery. Master and slave are both inside me: a rational, "real" self above, desires and impulses below. Then the real self is enlarged into a tribe, a race, a church, a state, and the road is open:

Once I take this view, I am in a position to ignore the actual wishes of men or societies, to bully, oppress, torture them in the name of their ‘real’ selves— Isaiah Berlin, Two Concepts of Liberty, 1958

He draws the distinction finely. Saying "this is good for you, so I will coerce you against your wishes" at least admits that it is coercion. Saying "this is good for you, so you have really chosen it, and you are not being coerced" equates what X would choose if he were someone else with what X actually seeks. That, he says, is the "monstrous impersonation" at the heart of all political theories of self-realisation.

The next two sections trace two historical routes to self-direction. One is the amputation from the opening, running from the Stoics to Kant's autonomy. The other is liberation through understanding: once the schoolboy grasps the proof, the theorem is no longer a foreign obstacle; once the musician has absorbed the score, playing it is no longer obedience. Rationalists said social rules work the same way. Hegel and Marx changed the content of the laws but kept the faith.

Section V is named after Sarastro's temple, from the wise high priest of Mozart's Magic Flute. The reasoning runs: every question has one true answer, two truths cannot conflict, so there must be an order that fits everyone's rational ends together. Those who cannot see it must first be educated; Fichte wanted education to work so that "you will later recognise the reasons for what I am doing now." Comte asked why, if we do not allow free thinking in chemistry, we should allow it in morals. Berlin's conclusion:

This is the argument used by every dictator, inquisitor and bully who seeks some moral, or even aesthetic, justification for his conduct.— Isaiah Berlin, Two Concepts of Liberty, 1958

He lays out the premises of this road one by one, then asks whether Hume might be right and Socrates mistaken: whether virtue is not knowledge, and whether not one of these premises can be shown to be true.

Section VI is the most surprising. Many people, he says, want neither kind of liberty but recognition. Some would rather be roughly governed by members of their own group than well treated by a just, gentle administrator from outside. Berlin takes this longing seriously but refuses to call it liberty; it is closer to solidarity and fraternity. Section VII returns to institutions and borrows from Benjamin Constant: the problem is not who holds power but how much power any hands hold. "It is not against the arm that one must rail, but against the weapon. Some weights are too heavy for the human hand." Democracy can crush individuals too, and "the triumph of despotism is to force the slaves to declare themselves free." The two liberties part company in one line:

The former want to curb authority as such. The latter want it placed in their own hands.— Isaiah Berlin, Two Concepts of Liberty, 1958

The last section, "The One and the Many," gets to the root. Individuals are sacrificed on the altars of justice, progress, national destiny, even liberty itself, because of the belief that somewhere there is "a final solution," a phrase that after the Nazis could carry only one echo. Experience gives no such guarantee, Berlin says. The ends of men are many and not all compatible, so conflict and tragedy cannot be removed, and we have to choose between values that are equally ultimate. That is what gives the freedom to choose its weight. He quotes Kant: "Out of the crooked timber of humanity no straight thing was ever made." The draft ends:

To demand unity and certainty is perhaps a deep and incurable metaphysical need; but to allow it to guide one’s practice is a symptom of an equally deep, and far more dangerous, moral and political immaturity.— Isaiah Berlin, Two Concepts of Liberty, 1958 (Draft E)

Below the iceberg

There is no single text. The editor Henry Hardy lists versions A through K, from the original dictation through several rewrites and the text sent to the publisher, to the versions in Four Essays on Liberty (1969) and Liberty (2002). The one read from the lectern was F, "only half as long as the text that appears in Liberty." The dictation of Draft A still survives as a recording. Almost everyone who quotes this lecture is quoting words its audience never heard.

The changes are evidence. Draft B opens with a complaint that the studies of the chair "have fallen upon evil days in this country"; by Draft E this has become Eden and the visitor from Mars. In B the pages of Hegel Berlin cannot follow are the "more esoteric" ones; in E they are the "darker" ones. The pike and the minnows sit at the very end of B, attached to a reminder from the economic historian R. H. Tawney; E moves the line up to section I, to show why the frontier must be haggled over. In a 1962 television interview the version that came out was "freedom for the pike means death to the carp."

The most important change is at the end. Draft E says: "The ‘negative’ liberty that they strive to realise seems to me a truer and more humane ideal." In the text in circulation today the sentence begins "Pluralism, with the measure of 'negative' liberty that it entails," and the reason is rewritten: human goals are many, not all of them commensurable, and in perpetual rivalry with one another. In effect Berlin conceded that his foundation was value pluralism, not negative liberty. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entry on Berlin reads the lecture accordingly: less negative against positive liberty than individualism, empiricism and pluralism against collectivism, metaphysical rationalism and monism.

The scales were uneven from the start. The same entry notes that Berlin defined negative liberty clearly and briefly but gave positive liberty two different basic definitions, the ability to pursue goals and autonomy, and "these are not the same." He later regretted not saying more about the evils negative liberty had been used to justify, such as exploitation under laissez-faire.

Small flaws. Both drafts credit Cephalus in Plato's Republic with a remark about old age freeing him from passion, when Cephalus is actually quoting Sophocles; later versions corrected it. "The triumph of despotism is to force the slaves to declare themselves free" was put in quotation marks by Berlin, yet Hardy never found a source and guesses it may be a garbled line of Goethe. Berlin's hard evidence sits in a footnote: Bukharin wrote in 1920 that proletarian coercion, "from executions to forced labour," is "the method of moulding communist humanity out of the human material of the capitalist period." The phrase "human material," Berlin says, vividly conveys the attitude.

He was not positive liberty's enemy. Section VII says the satisfaction each kind of liberty seeks is an ultimate value with an equal right to rank among the deepest interests of mankind. The SEP stresses that his target was positive liberty in its perverted forms, and the monism and collective idea of the self that perverted it.

What happened next

Partly held · as of 2026-11

The warning against coercion in the name of a "true self", and the value pluralism behind it, remain basic reference points in political philosophy. But the two-concept map itself has been redrawn twice (there is only one; there is a missing third), and Berlin's history of Rousseau and German idealism has drawn serious objections.

1967: only one liberty. In the Philosophical Review, the American legal philosopher Gerald MacCallum argued that every claim about freedom has the same three-part form: an agent is free from some constraint to do or become something. The two camps differ only in how they fill the three slots; the "true self," for example, shrinks or enlarges the agent. I have not read the original; this follows the SEP entry on positive and negative liberty.

1969: Berlin replies. When the lecture was collected in Four Essays on Liberty, Berlin's introduction answered his critics. According to Skinner, his reply to MacCallum was that some pleas for freedom are simply pleas to be liberated, with no "to do" slot: a two-part structure. Skinner calls that reply confused.

2001–2002: a third liberty. Skinner gave the first Isaiah Berlin Lecture at the British Academy in November 2001, and the text appeared in the London Review of Books the next spring. He begins by defending Berlin: MacCallum's formula cannot contain the self-realisation freedom of T. H. Green and Bernard Bosanquet, so Berlin really did isolate two incommensurable concepts. Then he names the blind spot. Equating freedom with non-interference was a position Hobbes stated explicitly in order to defeat a rival theory, an older one drawn from Roman law and from Livy and Tacitus: if your rights depend on someone else's goodwill, you live in servitude even if he never lifts a finger. England's Parliamentarians used it, and so did the American Declaration of Independence. Skinner points out that in section VI Berlin had already asked whether this could be called liberty "in some third sense," and answered no. The philosopher Philip Pettit had developed this line in 1997 into republican freedom as non-domination.

Today. The SEP entry on Berlin says his account of Rousseau and Kant's followers faces "serious and plausible objections" on both historical and conceptual grounds, and that he is often misread as the sworn enemy of positive liberty. The SEP entry on positive and negative liberty (revised June 2026) says Berlin's distinction remains an important point of reference, and whether non-domination can really be separated from negative liberty "remains to be seen." The map keeps being redrawn, but the danger zone Berlin marked is still on it.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · anattā (non-self): the perversion of positive liberty depends on positing a self "truer" than your present wishes. Buddhism denies any such permanent self, which knocks the stage out from under the impersonation. Where it differs: Buddhist liberation means extinguishing craving, which in Berlin's scheme belongs to the sour-grapes "inner citadel."
  • Psychology · self-determination theory: Edward Deci and Richard Ryan list autonomy as one of three basic psychological needs, and for them autonomy is not independence: following someone else's request counts as autonomous if you genuinely endorse it. That is close to Berlin's "to understand is to be free" version of positive liberty. Where it differs: the theory measures what people actually endorse, while Berlin feared rulers who declare what you would endorse if you were rational. One asks you; the other answers for you.
  • Distributed systems · single point of failure: reliable systems never put all authority in one node, and assume any node may fail or turn malicious. That is Constant's intuition that "some weights are too heavy for the human hand": don't bet on who holds power, limit how much power anyone holds. Where it differs: a system has a clear objective function; Berlin's political world does not.

Ten takeaways

  1. Negative liberty asks how large the area of non-interference is; positive liberty asks who governs me and whether I am my own master.
  2. Inability is not unfreedom: only interference by other people makes you unfree, so being unable to jump high or to see is a matter of capacity.
  3. Negative liberty has no logical link to democracy: an enlightened despot can leave wide private freedom, and a democracy can crush individuals.
  4. The metaphor of self-mastery splits a person into a "real self" and lower desires, then inflates the real self into a nation or a state.
  5. Passing off what you would choose if you were rational as what you actually chose is the core mechanism of coercion in liberty's name.
  6. Cutting out desires to avoid being hurt and retreating into an inner citadel is a sublime form of sour grapes, not an enlargement of liberty.
  7. The belief in a final answer that harmonises all values is the intellectual root of sacrificing individuals to ideals.
  8. Human ends are many and often conflict, and because we must choose between ultimate values, the freedom to choose carries weight.
  9. Berlin revised the lecture repeatedly, and the ideal its ending defends changed from negative liberty to pluralism with the negative liberty it entails.
  10. Quentin Skinner argued for a third liberty: if your rights depend on someone else's goodwill, you are in servitude even if he never interferes.
