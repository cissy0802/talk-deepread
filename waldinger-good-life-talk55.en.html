75 years of data say "relationships matter most" — what the data actually hold up is a correlation

Robert Waldinger · "What makes a good life? Lessons from the longest study on happiness"

Every two years, someone calls the same group of men and asks whether they'd mind filling out one more questionnaire. Half of them were chosen as boys from Boston's poorest neighborhoods, many from homes without hot and cold running water. They often ask back: why do you keep wanting to study me? My life just isn't that interesting. The other half went to Harvard. Robert Waldinger, onstage, delivers the punchline: the Harvard men never ask that question. The room laughs.

Behind the joke is a rare study, the Harvard Study of Adult Development. Since 1938 it has followed 724 men from adolescence into old age: questionnaires, interviews, medical records, blood draws, brain scans. Waldinger is its fourth director. Its value is that it doesn't rely on memory: everything was written down before anyone knew how the stories would end.

So his question is simple. If you were investing now in your future best self, where would you put your time and energy? He opens with a survey: over 80 percent of young adults said a major life goal was getting rich, half said becoming famous. Then he gives the 75-year answer:

Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period.— Robert Waldinger, TEDxBeaconStreet, 2015

He splits it into three lessons. Loneliness is toxic: better-connected people are happier, healthier and live longer. Quality beats quantity: you can be lonely in a crowd and lonely in a marriage, and a high-conflict, low-affection marriage may be worse for your health than divorce. And good relationships protect the brain: people in their 80s who feel they can count on their partner keep their memories sharper longer. The line people remember: looking back at the men at 50, what predicted their health at 80 was not their cholesterol but how satisfied they were in their relationships.

He closes with Mark Twain: life is too short for bickering and calling to account; there is only time for loving.

The twelve-and-a-half-minute talk has been played more than 50 million times on TED's site. What the stage didn't show is the shape of the evidence. The papers behind the three lessons have small, all-white samples and are all correlational. The memory finding held only for women. And the study itself found in 1998 that once drinking, smoking and depression at age 50 were controlled for, the link between social support and health shrank considerably.

The verdict: partly holds. The correlation has been confirmed again and again: a 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies and over 300,000 people found about 50 percent higher odds of survival for those with stronger social relationships, on a par with well-known mortality risks. The causal version is shakier: a 2024 genetic analysis of UK Biobank data found that loneliness's links to most diseases were largely explained by income, health habits and depression; for 20 of the 26 diseases tested, the link didn't look causal. Loneliness may be more a signal than a cause. The advice remains harmless and quite possibly helpful. The "Period" is what the data can't give him.

The moment

Waldinger is describing something dull: every two years, the research staff phone these old men to ask if they can send yet another questionnaire. Then he adds:

Many of the inner city Boston men ask us, "Why do you keep wanting to study me? My life just isn't that interesting." The Harvard men never ask that question.— Robert Waldinger, TEDxBeaconStreet, 2015

Laughter. The joke quietly reveals the study's foundations: the two groups never lived in the same world. One felt its lives weren't worth recording; the other took it for granted. A moment later he lands another: about a decade ago the researchers finally asked the men's wives to join, and many of the women said, "You know, it's about time." Both laughs point to the same thing: whose data this is, and who's missing.

The speaker, and the year

Waldinger is a psychiatrist and psychoanalyst at Harvard Medical School, and an ordained Zen priest. He is the study's fourth director; the best known of his predecessors, George Vaillant, ran it for over three decades and wrote several books on aging well from its data. Onstage Waldinger is like a clinician in his consulting room: even pace, one concrete picture after another.

By November 2015 the study had turned 75. About 60 of the original 724 men were still alive, most in their 90s. Waldinger's team had recently published papers on marriage and late-life health: one in 2010 on pain and mood, one in 2015 on attachment and memory. TEDxBeaconStreet is an independent local TEDx event in the Boston area. TED later featured the talk; as of this writing its page shows more than 53 million views.

By then, "loneliness is bad for your health" was no longer news; a large 2010 meta-analysis had already tied social relationships to mortality. Waldinger wasn't bringing a new finding. He was bringing a rare witness: a study that had watched lives for 75 years.

  • Longitudinal study: following the same people over many years, rather than taking a single snapshot.
  • Correlation vs causation: two things showing up together doesn't mean one causes the other.
  • Meta-analysis: pooling many studies' results statistically to estimate an overall effect.
  • Mendelian randomization: using inborn genetic differences as a "natural randomization" to test whether a factor is really a cause.

The talk, in full

Waldinger opens with an investment question: if you were going to invest now in your future best self, where would you put your time and your energy? He cites a survey of millennials: over 80 percent named getting rich as a major life goal, another 50 percent becoming famous (laughter). Add the constant advice to lean in to work, and we're told this is the recipe for a good life.

The trouble is evidence. Most of what we know about human life comes from asking people to remember the past, and memory is sometimes downright creative. What if we could watch entire lives as they unfold?

"We did that." Since 1938, two groups of men. One started as Harvard sophomores, finished college during World War II, and mostly went off to war. The other was boys from Boston's poorest neighborhoods, chosen precisely because their families were among the most troubled and disadvantaged; many lived in tenements without hot and cold running water. They grew up to be factory workers, lawyers, bricklayers, doctors, one President of the United States; some developed alcoholism, some climbed from the bottom to the top, some went the other way. Studies like this almost never survive a decade, he says; this one survived on luck and the persistence of several generations of researchers.

After the two jokes he asks: what have we learned from tens of thousands of pages? Not wealth, fame or working harder, but that single line: good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period. He breaks it into three lessons.

Lesson one: social connections are good for us, and, in his words, "loneliness kills." People who are more isolated than they want to be see their health decline earlier in midlife, their brain functioning decline sooner, and they live shorter lives. He adds a number: at any given time, more than one in five Americans will report that they're lonely.

Lesson two: it's not the number of friends, it's the quality of close relationships. You can be lonely in a crowd and lonely in a marriage. High-conflict marriages without much affection are very bad for our health, "perhaps worse than getting divorced." Warm relationships are protective. Then comes the talk's most powerful look back:

And when we gathered together everything we knew about them at age 50, it wasn't their middle age cholesterol levels that predicted how they were going to grow old. It was how satisfied they were in their relationships.— Robert Waldinger, TEDxBeaconStreet, 2015

The people most satisfied in their relationships at 50 were the healthiest at 80. He adds that the most happily partnered men and women reported, in their 80s, that on days with more physical pain their mood stayed just as happy, while for people in unhappy relationships physical pain was magnified by emotional pain.

Lesson three: good relationships protect not just our bodies but our brains. People in their 80s in securely attached relationships, who feel they can really count on the other person in times of need, keep their memories sharper longer. And the relationships needn't be smooth: some couples bicker day in and day out, but as long as they can count on each other when the going gets tough, the arguments don't take a toll on memory.

Then the turn. This wisdom is "as old as the hills," so why is it so hard to get and so easy to ignore? Because we want a quick fix, and tending relationships is messy, unglamorous and lifelong. He offers one detail from the study:

The people in our 75-year study who were the happiest in retirement were the people who had actively worked to replace workmates with new playmates.— Robert Waldinger, TEDxBeaconStreet, 2015

He circles back to the opening: these men, too, believed as young adults that fame and wealth were the ticket, but the ones who fared best "leaned in to relationships," a deliberate echo of "lean in to work." The advice is plain: replace screen time with people time, reach out to the family member you haven't spoken to in years, because family feuds take a terrible toll on the people who hold the grudges. He closes with Mark Twain, and then:

The good life is built with good relationships.— Robert Waldinger, TEDxBeaconStreet, 2015

Below the waterline

The opening survey wasn't about the respondents themselves. Waldinger says it asked millennials what their most important life goals were. This appears to be the Pew Research Center's 2007 "Generation Next" survey: among 18- to 25-year-olds, 64 percent said getting rich was the most important goal for their generation and another 17 percent said it was the second most important, which adds up to 81 percent; for fame it was 10 plus 41, or 51 percent. Pew notes explicitly that respondents were asked about their generation, not about themselves. The numbers are right; the target shifted, from "I think my generation wants to be rich" to "I want to be rich."

724 men, all white. The 724 is the sum of two groups: 268 Harvard undergraduates recruited in 1939–1942 and 456 inner-city Boston boys born between 1925 and 1932. Wives were brought in only in the 2000s; women and non-white people were never part of the original design.

The pain finding comes from 47 couples over 8 nights. Waldinger and Marc Schulz's 2010 paper in Psychology and Aging used the Harvard group only: 47 couples, husbands averaging 82.9 years old, all white. Researchers phoned them on eight consecutive evenings and asked about the past 24 hours: pain (one 0–10 item), physical limitation, and happiness (one 7-point item). Among people low in marital satisfaction, painful days were clearly less happy; among those high in satisfaction, the slope between pain and happiness was not statistically significant. So "just as happy" is more precisely "no detectable decline in this small sample." The authors themselves note that direction can't be established, that mood may shape how people report pain, and that the participants were elite survivors of the World War II generation. The inner-city group wasn't in this study.

The memory finding held only for women. The 2015 paper by Waldinger and colleagues in Clinical Psychological Science studied 81 couples (51 from the Harvard group, 30 from the inner-city group), husbands averaging 80.8 and wives 75.7. Attachment security was rated from a 45–60 minute semi-structured interview, and memory was tested two and a half years later. Greater security predicted less depression and higher life satisfaction later in both sexes. But for memory: women r = .41, significant; men r = −.12, not significant. The finding that security buffers the toll of conflict on memory was likewise seen only in women. Onstage, "those people's memories stay sharper longer" turns a result found in the wives into a claim about everyone. The paper itself calls for replication and acknowledges possible reverse causation.

The cholesterol line: we couldn't match it to a source. Vaillant and Kenneth Mukamal's 2001 paper "Successful aging" in the American Journal of Psychiatry is the most systematic "age 50 predicts age 80" analysis of these men: 237 from the Harvard group and 332 from the inner-city group. Of the variables measured before 50, six were beyond personal control (such as parental social class, ancestral longevity and major depression) and seven were at least partly controllable (alcohol abuse, smoking, marital stability, exercise, body mass index, coping style and education). The abstract concludes that once the seven controllable variables were accounted for, depression was the only uncontrollable predictor still at work. In that abstract, relationships appear only as "marital stability," alongside not smoking and not drinking, not as the factor that trumps all others, and cholesterol isn't mentioned. The "relationship satisfaction beats cholesterol" comparison may come from another analysis by the team; we didn't find the corresponding paper on this pass, so we can only report that it was said onstage and that we couldn't check the underlying data.

The study had already spotted the confounding. In 1998 Vaillant and colleagues published a paper in Psychological Medicine whose title is a question: "Are social supports in late midlife a cause or a result of successful physical ageing?" Using 50 years of data on 223 men, they found that social support from 50 to 70 was strongly correlated with physical health at 70, but social support was itself strongly predicted by alcohol abuse, smoking and signs of major depression at 50. Controlling for those three, the association was "very much attenuated." Their conclusion: social supports surely matter, but much of the link between poor support and mortality may run through alcoholism, smoking and prior psychopathology. And a counterintuitive detail: in this sample, friends seemed more important for sustained physical health than closeness to spouse and children. The paper predates the TED talk by 17 years and goes unmentioned onstage.

Between the "three lessons" and the papers there is a consistent gap. It isn't fabrication: nearly every line onstage has a shadow in some paper. The qualifiers just fell off, one by one, on the way to the stage.

What happened next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-11

The correlation, that socially connected people are healthier and live longer, has been confirmed repeatedly in large independent samples. The causal version, that relationships themselves produce health, gets mostly negative or uncertain answers from genetic evidence. Several of the talk's specific claims are broader than the papers behind them.

The correlation is solid. Five years before the talk, Julianne Holt-Lunstad of Brigham Young University and colleagues published a meta-analysis in PLOS Medicine: 148 studies, 308,849 people, with about 50 percent greater odds of survival for those with stronger social relationships (OR = 1.50), consistent across age, sex and cause of death. The link was strongest for complex measures of social integration (OR = 1.91) and weakest, and not significant, for simply living alone. In 2015 the same group published a meta-analysis focused on loneliness and isolation: in studies that controlled for confounders, social isolation, loneliness and living alone corresponded to roughly 29, 26 and 32 percent higher mortality risk, with no difference between subjective loneliness and objective isolation. That fits Waldinger's "more isolated than they want to be."

Then it became the language of public policy. In May 2023 US Surgeon General Vivek Murthy issued an advisory on an "epidemic" of loneliness; according to the Associated Press, he said its health risks are as deadly as smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day. The comparison traces back to Holt-Lunstad's 2010 meta-analysis. Critics note that it comes from lining up effect sizes across different meta-analyses, not from a direct measurement. The same year Waldinger and Schulz published The Good Life, turning the study into a book.

On causation, the newest evidence pulls back. In 2024 Yannis Yan Liang, Jihui Zhang and colleagues published a study in Nature Human Behaviour whose title is its conclusion: "Observational and genetic evidence disagree on the association between loneliness and risk of multiple diseases." Using UK Biobank data with a median follow-up of 12.2 years, they looked at 56 diseases. Observationally, loneliness was linked to 30 of them. But among the 26 with genetic data for Mendelian randomization, after correcting for multiple testing, 20 associations were judged non-causal, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity and most neurological diseases; only six were potentially causal. Socioeconomic factors, health behaviors, baseline depression and comorbidities largely explained the observed links. The authors suggest that for most diseases loneliness may be a surrogate marker rather than a cause. That points the same way as what Vaillant's team saw in its own data in 1998. Mendelian randomization has its own limits, and different teams haven't fully agreed, so this isn't the last word; but it's enough to show that "good relationships, therefore better health" can't be treated as established fact.

The study has since enrolled wives and more than 2,000 children, but its core 724 remain white men of one era, and that can't be fixed after the fact. We didn't find a formal written response from Waldinger to the causation critique on this pass, so we don't paraphrase his position.

Today's consensus, roughly: social connection is a reliable marker of health, quite possibly also one of its causes, but how much of it is cause remains contested. Waldinger's prescription has almost no side effects, which is part of why it travels so well.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · sangha and clinging: Buddhism counts the sangha, the community of practitioners, as one of the Three Jewels; practice depends on mutual support, which matches "relationships as refuge." But Buddhism also treats clinging (upādāna) as a root of suffering, warning precisely against holding on to people. The "secure attachment" Waldinger praises comes from John Bowlby's attachment theory and means being able to rely on someone, not refusing to let go. Both prize relationships, yet score "attachment" in opposite directions.
  • Neuroscience · Coan's hand-holding study: In 2006 James Coan and colleagues put 16 married women in an fMRI scanner under threat of electric shock; holding their husband's hand dampened threat-related brain activity, more so the higher the marital quality. Alike in "relationships buffer pain"; unlike in that it measures seconds of lab response, not decades of health.
  • Philosophy · Aristotle on friendship: Books VIII and IX of the Nicomachean Ethics sort friendship (philia) into three kinds, based on usefulness, pleasure or virtue, of which only the last endures, and say no one would choose to live without friends. Alike in putting relationships at the center of the good life; unlike in that for Aristotle friendship is part of the good life itself and needs no health payoff to justify it.

Ten takeaways

  1. Since 1938 the Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed 724 men: 268 Harvard undergraduates and 456 boys from Boston's poorest neighborhoods.
  2. A longitudinal study records lives before the outcome is known, sidestepping the way memory rewrites the past.
  3. Waldinger sums up 75 years in three lessons: loneliness harms, quality of relationships beats quantity, and good relationships protect the brain.
  4. A high-conflict, low-affection marriage may be worse for health than divorce; what matters is not having a partner but how good the relationship is.
  5. The Pew survey cited at the start asked young adults about their generation's goals, and the 81 and 51 percent figures combine first and second choices.
  6. "Just as happy on painful days" comes from 47 Harvard-group couples' phone diaries over 8 nights, and means no detectable drop, not proof of no change.
  7. Secure attachment predicted better memory two and a half years later only among the wives, not among the husbands.
  8. In 1998 the study itself found that controlling for drinking, smoking and depression at 50 greatly weakened the link between social support and health.
  9. A meta-analysis of 148 studies found about 50 percent higher survival odds with strong social ties, but 2024 Mendelian randomization found loneliness's links to most diseases look non-causal.
  10. Aristotle treated friendship as part of the good life itself, needing no longevity payoff to prove its worth.
