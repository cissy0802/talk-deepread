Saanen Public Talk · Switzerland · 1981

You are not an individual, you are the rest of mankind: an 86-year-old says so in a Swiss mountain village

J. Krishnamurti · "What is the nature of our consciousness?" (first Saanen talk, 12 July 1981)

July 1981, Saanen, a village in the Swiss Alps. An 86-year-old Indian man tells his audience something that reads very differently today. Computers, he says, are programmed by experts; they gather experience, learn from it, accumulate knowledge, and "gradually they are going to outstrip all our thinking in accuracy and with greater speed." Then he leaves himself one exception:

"Of course they cannot compose as Beethoven, or as Keats, but they will outstrip our thinking."— J. Krishnamurti, Saanen, 1981

But he hasn't come to talk about computers. The computer is a mirror. If a machine can be programmed, what about a person? People are programmed to be Catholic or Protestant, Italian or British; programmed to believe, to follow rituals, to be nationalistic and go to war. His question: on what grounds does a programmed brain think of itself as "me"?

The man is Jiddu Krishnamurti. As a boy he was "discovered" on an Indian beach by the Theosophical Society and groomed to be the coming World Teacher. In 1929 he dissolved the order built around him and declared that "truth is a pathless land." For the next half century he told audiences not to follow anyone, including the man on the stage. He never said "I." He called himself "the speaker."

The core of this talk is one sentence, which he restates several ways:

"Human consciousness is one whole; it is not your consciousness or mine."— J. Krishnamurti, Saanen, 1981

The argument is plain to the point of stubbornness. You are anxious, lonely, confused, and so is someone ten thousand miles away. The content of consciousness (fear, pleasure, sorrow, the dread of death) is shared by all of humanity, so "the individual" is an illusion, a belief we were programmed with. Even thought isn't yours. Experience becomes knowledge, knowledge is stored as memory, memory produces thought, thought produces action, action brings new experience, and round it goes. Knowledge is always limited, so thought is limited, and once something limited says "I am a Hindu" or "I am a Christian," conflict follows.

The way out? He won't give a method. Don't ask how to stop thought, he says; just look, without the filter of any organization, religion or nationality. To actually realize that you are the rest of mankind, not as a verbal acceptance but as "the feeling in your guts," brings tremendous energy and a tremendous love for human beings. And, as always, he adds: please do not accept what the speaker is saying.

What happened next? Still unresolved. The aside about computers has largely come true. Stanford's 2024 AI Index reports that AI has surpassed humans on several benchmarks, including some in image classification and English understanding, while still trailing on complex reasoning and planning. The central claim is harder to judge. In 2012 the psychologist Sam McFarland published an "Identification With All Humanity" scale. Across ten studies, people who count "all humans everywhere" as their own group cared more about human rights and were readier to give to international relief. The love Krishnamurti predicted shows up in the correlations. But in 2022, two preregistered experiments (675 people in total) tried to "activate" that identity with written materials. The effect was small, and it changed nothing about people's pandemic behavior. So the feeling exists and does go with kindness, but no one knows how to move it from the lips into the gut, which is exactly the step Krishnamurti called the hard one. As for "the individual is an illusion," no experiment has yet been able to test it.

Talk What is the nature of our consciousness? (first Saanen public talk) · Venue Saanen summer gathering, Switzerland · 12 July 1981 · Length edited text of about 3,100 words · Full text The Network of Thought, ch. 1 (Krishnamurti Foundation Trust)

The moment

A few minutes in, he is still talking about the need to "think together" when he swerves into a subject a 1981 audience probably didn't expect: computers.

"We have been ‘programmed’ biologically, physically and also ‘programmed’ mentally, intellectually." Computers, he goes on, are programmed by experts, gather experience, learn, accumulate knowledge, and "gradually they are going to outstrip all our thinking in accuracy and with greater speed." Then comes the exception:

"Of course they cannot compose as Beethoven, or as Keats, but they will outstrip our thinking."— J. Krishnamurti, Saanen, 1981

Then he turns the question on the audience. If robots and computers can do almost everything a human being can do, "then what is man?" Programmed to be Catholic or British, programmed to believe, to be nationalistic, to go to war. That is the fuse for the whole talk. What he wants to take apart is not the computer but the "I."

The speaker, and the year

Jiddu Krishnamurti was born in 1895 in Madanapalle, a small town in south India. He was a sickly child, often taken to be intellectually slow, and beaten by his teachers. In 1909 Charles Leadbeater, a self-described clairvoyant of the Theosophical Society, noticed the boy on the beach at Adyar and said he had "the most wonderful aura he had ever seen." Leadbeater decided he was the vehicle for the coming World Teacher, and the Society founded the Order of the Star in the East around him. In 1929, aged 34, he dissolved the Order, gave back a castle in the Netherlands and 5,000 acres of land, and said the sentence his foundations treat as the core of everything he taught:

"I maintain that truth is a pathless land, and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever, by any religion, by any sect."— J. Krishnamurti, 1929 (as given in Mary Lutyens's biography)

For the next fifty-odd years he toured between India, England, California and Switzerland. Saanen is a village in the mountains of south-western Switzerland. He spoke there every summer from 1961: first in a town hall that held 350 people, then from the next year in a domed tent that held 900. Aldous Huxley and Yehudi Menuhin were among those who came to hear him.

In 1981 he was 86. That summer he gave seven talks in Saanen, and this was the first. Together with two Amsterdam talks they became the book The Network of Thought. Why computers and "programming" that year? Between April and June of the previous year he had held ten conversations with the physicist David Bohm (later published as The Ending of Time), which kept returning to whether thought is limited and whether humanity has gone wrong. In 1981 he was raising computers elsewhere too, in a small group discussion in Ojai and in a talk in Bombay. He opened this talk by asking the regulars for patience:

"please be tolerant if the speaker repeats himself, for repetition has a certain value."— J. Krishnamurti, Saanen, 1981

Glossary

  • Theosophical Society: an esoteric movement founded in New York in 1875 that blended Eastern and Western religion and believed hidden "Masters" guide humanity's spiritual evolution.
  • Content of consciousness: Krishnamurti's term for what fills the mind, such as fears, pleasures, beliefs, memories and national identity. For him, consciousness is its content.
  • Choiceless awareness: simply watching what happens in the mind without judging it, suppressing it or steering it.

The talk, all the way through

He begins with prejudice. Prejudice, ideals and beliefs all limit our capacity to "think together," and the world, he says, is "rapidly disintegrating, degenerating." His question: after millions of years of evolution, why has man become so violent, callous and destructive?

The first step is a deliberately flat fact. Thought is the common factor of all mankind. There is no Eastern or Western thought: the surgeon, the carpenter, the labourer in the field and the great poet all use the same capacity, and we are all caught in this "network of thought." Then comes the computer passage, and then he pins down where the crisis lies. Politicians can't solve it because they are programmed to think in a particular way, and neither can scientists or the business world. The turning point "is in our consciousness." He asks the audience to be serious about this. The atomic bomb is proliferating, and "some lunatic will turn on" one.

That leads into the heart of the talk. Volumes have been written on consciousness, East and West, but one thing is simple:

"The significance is that your consciousness is the consciousness of all humanity because you suffer, you are anxious, you are lonely, insecure, confused, exactly like others, though they live ten thousand miles away."— J. Krishnamurti, Saanen, 1981

He adds a guard straight away. This is not a verbal statement, and if you take it as an idea you will miss the point: "The realization of it, the feeling of it – the feeling in your guts – is totally different from the mere verbal acceptance." Realizing that you are the rest of mankind brings tremendous energy and breaks "the narrow circle of me and you, we and they." Then a second guard: "Please do not accept what the speaker is saying; if you do it will have no meaning."

Then he peels back the layers. Near the surface is group consciousness. The carpenter has one, the surgeon has one, the housewife has one, and your country's tradition and religious culture become part of you too. Deeper down are fear, pleasure, envy, loneliness, depression, great sorrow, what he calls love, and the everlasting fear of death, all shared by everyone. That brings him to his sharpest line:

"This is very hard to accept because we have been programmed, as is the computer, to think we are individuals."— J. Krishnamurti, Saanen, 1981

How do you face this? By looking, and you can only look if you are not programmed: "It is only if you do not belong to any organization, to any group, to any particular religion or nationality, that you can really observe." A brain crowded with book knowledge and experience is blocked as well. Along the way he takes a swipe at "one guru opposed to another idiotic guru."

The last stretch turns to thought itself. "Thought is responsible for the beauty of a great cathedral, but thought is also responsible for all the nonsense that takes place inside the cathedral." It made the poems and the technology, and it made the bombs and the hostage-taking. Then he gives a model that is almost coldly mechanical:

"Experience, knowledge, memory, thought, action; from that action learn more and repeat. This is how we are programmed."— J. Krishnamurti, Saanen, 1981

Knowledge is always limited because it belongs to time: "I do not know Russian but I will learn it." It may take six months, a year, a lifetime. So thought is limited, the action it generates is limited, and limitation breeds conflict. I say I am a Hindu, you say you are a Christian, and the conflict sits between us. The root of conflict is thought. He ends without a method: "Please do not ask, ‘How am I to stop thought?’ – that is not the point. The point is to understand the nature of thought, to look at it."

Below the waterline

What you are reading is not a word-for-word transcript. The circulating text comes from The Network of Thought, which describes its contents as "authentic reports" of the talks, meaning edited versions made from the recordings. It runs to about 3,100 words, and most of the repetitions and pauses of speech have been edited out. Every quote on this page comes from that edited text (the Krishnamurti Foundation's version and an independent reprint match word for word), not from the audio. The krishnamurti.org page listed in our reading list was behind a bot check during this run and could not be read.

"Thought is limited" was worked out a year earlier, with a physicist. The first Krishnamurti–Bohm conversation, on 1 April 1980, opens like this:

"How shall we start? I would like to ask if humanity has taken a wrong turn."— J. Krishnamurti and David Bohm, The Ending of Time, ch. 1, 1980

Bohm answers, "Well it must have done so, a long time ago, I think," and mentions something he had read about man going wrong five or six thousand years ago, when he learned to plunder and take slaves. Krishnamurti won't take the historical explanation and pulls it inward: "mankind has always tried to become something." Put the two side by side and you can see what Saanen left out. On stage he only says that thought is limited and so breeds conflict. In the dialogue the chain is longer: wanting psychologically to become something requires psychological time, and time is conflict.

Bohm was this philosophy's most serious translator, and its most troubled one. According to his biographer F. David Peat, the two met in 1961, and Bohm counted Einstein and Krishnamurti as the two most important encounters of his life. Peat recalls Bohm saying the most significant thing Krishnamurti ever told him was "Begin with the unknown." But Peat also describes Bohm's growing unease at how the people around Krishnamurti treated him as a guru. Here was a man who said not to listen to gurus, including himself, surrounded by followers. Bohm also wondered why, if people were supposedly free of conditioning, the Krishnamurti schools had so much conflict. The friendship went through a major crisis in 1984 and lasted until Krishnamurti's death in 1986.

Where this sits in his thought. In 1929 he rejected outer authority, the order and the guru. Over the next half century he rejected inner authority as well: experience and knowledge. The Saanen talk takes the last step and removes the "I" who observes. The Foundation's summary of the seven talks is blunt. The central illusion is that a separate psychological agent stands behind each person's thoughts, desires and fears, "but there is no separate agency, there is no thinker."

The stage version claims more than the argument supports. "Everyone is anxious, lonely and afraid" is an observation that the contents of consciousness are similar. "So you are not an individual" is a metaphysical conclusion about whose consciousness it is. There is a big step between the two: two people feeling fear does not make it one fear. Krishnamurti doesn't argue for that step. He hands it to "the feeling in your guts," and declares in advance that agreeing with it in words is useless. That makes the claim hard to refute, and just as hard to test.

What happened next

STILL UNRESOLVED · as of 2026-10

The passing prediction about computers has largely come true. The idea that identifying with all humanity goes with care for people shows up in psychology's correlational data, but experiments have had little success producing that identification with words. The central claim, that the individual is an illusion and you are all of humanity, still has no testable form.

The computers: largely right. Stanford HAI's 2024 AI Index reports that AI has surpassed human performance on several benchmarks, including some in image classification, visual reasoning and English understanding, while trailing on competition-level mathematics, visual commonsense reasoning and planning. "They will outstrip our thinking in accuracy and with greater speed" pointed the right way. As for the Beethoven-and-Keats exception, I found no research that settles it, so this page doesn't try to.

Identifying with all humanity: measurable, but no one can manufacture it. The closest line of psychology research comes from Sam McFarland. In 2012 he and colleagues published the Identification With All Humanity Scale (IWAH) in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. It asks how much you feel a part of, feel love toward and have concern for people in your community, your compatriots, and all humans everywhere. Across ten studies, this identification turned out to be more than an absence of bias or a disposition toward empathy. Higher scores predicted more concern for global human rights and more willingness to donate to international relief. McFarland and colleagues' 2019 review in Political Psychology found it related negatively to ethnocentrism, authoritarianism and self-centeredness and positively to empathy. When Krishnamurti says such a feeling "means that you have tremendous love for human beings," the correlations are on his side.

Correlation, though, is not causation. In 2022, David Sparkman and colleagues at the University of Wisconsin–Eau Claire ran two preregistered experiments (675 participants in total). They tried to activate people's human identity and see whether it raised their willingness to protect others from COVID-19. The manipulation had a small effect on people's psychological bond with all humanity (Cohen's d 0.21–0.27) and none on their concern for all humanity. It had no causal effect on health-related intentions or on actual helping behavior. The paper also notes that another team ran 11 experiments using education, persuasion, subliminal priming and more, and found it hard to raise identification at this most inclusive level. In a 2023 Frontiers in Psychology collection spanning 14 countries, one study found that this identification fell noticeably among participants in Spain and Chile over two months of strict lockdown.

These results leave Krishnamurti in an odd spot. He said from the start that verbally accepting "you are the rest of mankind" is meaningless and that it has to be felt in the gut. Activating an identity with written materials is precisely the kind of acceptance he said doesn't work. So the experiments don't refute him. They do show that nobody has found a reliable way to produce the shift he described, let alone measure it.

What it was turned into. Late in life, Bohm turned "thinking together" and "watching the movement of thought" into a group method now called Bohm Dialogue: no agenda, no decisions, everyone suspending their assumptions. The management writer Peter Senge recommended it in The Fifth Discipline (1990). The Saanen plea that "we must be able to think together" ended up in corporate "learning organization" workshops.

Another part of the story is more personal. In 1991, Radha Rajagopal Sloss, the daughter of his old associates the Rajagopals, published Lives in the Shadow with J. Krishnamurti. It describes a long affair between him and her mother, running from the early 1930s to the late 1950s. Followers were dismayed, and rebuttals followed. In the London Review of Books, Patricia Beer was fair: he betrayed a friend, but "he was not, in this instance, a hypocrite," because his later teaching no longer demanded chastity. What matters more for this page is the other half. A man who spent his life saying "follow no one" left behind followers who found it hard to accept that he was human, which is exactly what had troubled Bohm.
