Yale / Coursera · 2018 · short course 2/3

The mind judges by reference points, gets used to everything, and doesn't know it will: the mechanisms held, several studies were told fuller than the papers

Laurie Santos · The Science of Well-Being (2/3: four annoying features of the mind)

Laurie Santos has just finished a long list of tricks for outwitting the mind's biases in her living room, and announces that the things that really make people happy will have to wait until next week. A student asks: are you holding them back because you're interrupting our happiness? She laughs: "Yeah, exactly." One of the strategies she has just taught is interruption: don't consume a good thing in one go; pause and come back, and the pleasure climbs again. The student caught her using it on them.

These are the second and third lectures of Yale's happiness course (last time: most of what we want won't make us as happy as we think). Now she explains why, with four "annoying features" of the mind: our strongest intuitions are often wrong; we don't judge in absolutes, only against reference points; we get used to everything; and we don't realize we will. Silver medalists look glummer than bronze medalists because silver is thinking about the gold they nearly won, and bronze about the medal they nearly missed. Lottery winners a year on are no happier than people who didn't win. People who are rejected, dumped, or told they are HIV-positive feel far less bad than they predicted, and the next time, they mispredict all over again.

The third lecture brings remedies. Spend on experiences, not stuff: a new car "will stay around to disappoint you," a trip "has the good sense to go away." Savor good moments; imagine they never happened; treat today as your last; keep a gratitude list, write a gratitude letter. Reset your reference points: revisit how life used to be, delete social media from your phone (she did, and reports feeling "a lot happier"), split good things into several helpings.

Check the studies one by one against the originals and the direction mostly holds, but two were told backwards or too broadly. She says potato chips eaten next to sardines are liked nearly twice as much; the paper is titled "Why affective forecasters overestimate comparative value," and it found that only people predicting thought the comparison would matter; people actually eating liked the chips just as much next to chocolate as next to sardines. She says experiences beat possessions at every income level; the original says the lowest-income respondents were evenly split, and a 2018 study found that lower-income people get as much happiness from things as from experiences.

So the verdict is partly held. Reference points, hedonic adaptation and the impact bias are still mainstream, and the Olympic medal finding replicated on photos from 2000–2016. But adaptation is not universal: a Swedish study of more than 3,000 lottery players found that big prizes raised life satisfaction with no sign of fading over a decade or more; German and British panel data show that after disability, happiness rarely returns to baseline. The remedies do work, only modestly: gratitude exercises beat comparison activities by about 0.17 standard deviations and do no better than other active exercises; randomly deactivating Facebook for four weeks raised well-being by 0.09 SD, a long way from "beats a $100,000 job."

The moment

The third lecture is nearly over. Santos has laid out a long run of ways to make yourself happier and says the things that will really make people happy are coming next week. She asks students to commit publicly to a habit for the week; the first one she calls on asks a question first:

Um, is the reason why you're waiting till next week to talk about these things is because you're interrupting our happiness?— a student, The Science of Well-Being, video 18

"Yeah, exactly," Santos says. Interruption was the strategy she had just taught: don't take good things in one unbroken stretch; pause, come back, and the pleasure rises again. She adds a second reason (next week covers why endings matter, and what comes last sticks best) and then owns it: "But yeah, I'm I'm stringing you guys along." No slide in these two lectures makes the point better. The mind's quirks don't live only in the lab; the lecturer was using one, and the audience had learned to spot it.

The speaker, and the year

Same living room, same lecturer. As the previous issue described, Laurie Santos studies decision-making in monkeys and is head of Yale's Silliman residential college; this course was a pilot recorded at her home with a couple of dozen of her students, and later became the most popular course in Yale's history and a free Coursera course.

This issue covers the pilot's second and third lectures, each with homework: last week, measure your happiness; this week, take a character-strengths test; next week, commit on camera to the habit you'll practice. The details place it around 2017: Snapchat stories are the thing, the social media she says she uses are Twitter and Facebook, and she cites the 2014 book Excellent Sheep, a critique of elite-college students, worrying that this generation is too afraid of bad outcomes to take risks.

Almost the whole skeleton of these two lectures comes from one research partnership: Dan Gilbert at Harvard and Tim Wilson at the University of Virginia. "Miswanting" is their term; so are "impact bias," "focalism" and "immune neglect." She quotes Gilbert's 2006 bestseller Stumbling on Happiness several times. Santos's role is to turn twenty years of lab results into a map and pair each bias with something you can do on an ordinary day.

Glossary

  • Miswanting: Gilbert and Wilson's term for being wrong about how much you'll like something in the future, and so wanting the wrong things.
  • Reference point: the standard you compare against when judging something; your own past, other people, or anything salient nearby.
  • Social comparison: judging yourself against other people; upward toward those better off, downward toward those worse off.
  • Hedonic adaptation: the fading of emotional reactions to good and bad changes as the new state becomes normal.
  • Impact bias: overestimating how intense and how lasting the emotional impact of a future event will be. Gilbert gives two causes: focalism and immune neglect.
  • Effect size d / standard deviation (SD): how many standard deviations apart two group averages are. About 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium.

The talk, in full

Lecture two opens with homework. Last week everyone measured their happiness, and most found they were less happy than they thought. Then she puts last lecture's list back up: good job, lots of money, awesome stuff, true love, perfect body, perfect grades. Last time's conclusion was that these don't make us as happy as we expect; this time the question is why. She gives the phenomenon a name, miswanting: the brain hands us a signal that says "I want this," and the signal is often wrong. She sorts the reasons into four "annoying features" of the mind, introducing each with a visual illusion, because biases are easiest to see in vision.

Annoying feature one: our strongest intuitions are often wrong. These are last lecture's two identical tables, now given a number.

Annoying feature two: we don't think in absolutes; we judge against reference points. She shows the Ebbinghaus illusion: two identical orange circles, one ringed by small blue circles and one by large ones, and everyone says the right one is smaller. A reference point is a "salient, but often completely irrelevant" standard we are constantly comparing against.

Then the Olympics. She shows Michael Phelps on the podium in 2008: he is beaming, the silver medalist looks miserable, and the bronze medalist is smiling even more than Phelps. The study she cites is by Medvec, Madey and Gilovich, who had raters independently score medalists' faces from Olympic footage. Both when they learned their place and on the podium, silver medalists looked less happy than bronze. The explanation is the reference point: silver is fixed on the gold one second away, bronze on how close they came to no medal at all. What sets your mood is not what you got but what you think you could have got.

Few of us win Olympic medals, so she brings reference points back to last lecture's list. The first is our own past: people earning $30,000 say they'd need $50,000 to be happy, people earning $100,000 say $250,000. Citing two economists, she says that for every dollar actual income rises, "required income" rises $1.40, so we always feel underpaid. The second, and worse, is other people. She shows a cartoon of an employee negotiating: "If you can't see your way to giving me a pay raise, how about giving Parkinson a pay cut?"

The evidence piles up. Clark and Oswald surveyed 5,000 British workers: holding absolute income constant, the more your colleagues earn, the less you like your job. Solnick and Hemenway asked Harvard School of Public Health students whether they'd rather earn $50,000 while others earn $25,000, or $100,000 while others earn $250,000; she says over half chose the first, half the money so as not to be behind. Reference points aren't picky either: O'Guinn and Shrum found that the more TV people watch, the more they overestimate others' wealth and the poorer they feel by comparison. When a Dutch postcode lottery gave winners a new car along with the cash, next-door neighbors became almost twice as likely to buy a new car the following year.

Grades, which Yale students care about most, are no exception. She describes a classroom experiment in which a professor offered everyone 0.5 extra points on the final grade. Nobody would go down, and about half would tip into a higher letter grade. Yet about 41% of students voted against it, and rejections ran about 10% higher in the top half of the class: they would forgo their own bonus rather than watch others climb.

Finally, the reference point unique to this generation: social media. She cites Vogel and colleagues: Facebook use correlates negatively with self-esteem, at about twice the size of the income-happiness correlation (about 0.1). A follow-up experiment with fake feeds ruled out reverse causation: comparing upward lowered self-esteem sharply; comparing downward gave no matching boost. That leads to her strongest claim of the lecture:

So, if you right now were to structure your life and do one thing that you think would make you happy, it wouldn't be picking a job that earned you $100,000 because the magnitude of that effect is less than if you just decided to stay off social media because it's not actually helping you.— Laurie Santos, video 10

Her summary of the section is blunt: "And our mind just sucks at picking reference points." It takes in whatever comes along, with no filter.

Annoying feature three: we get used to stuff. In vision this is perceptual adaptation: stare at a color-reversed image long enough and an afterimage appears when it goes away. Happiness works the same way; this is hedonic adaptation. She shows a cat facing a pile of hot dogs and says we all believe the seventh hot dog will be as good as the first. Three pieces of evidence: Di Tella and colleagues tracked Germans' incomes for nearly twenty years, and as salaries rose, the happiness line stayed flat; in Brickman and colleagues' famous lottery study, winners a year on rated their happiness about 4 out of 6 against 3.82 for controls, statistically no different; and marital satisfaction is back to baseline about two years in. Then she asks the students: you screamed on admission day, but this morning, did anyone wake up amazed to be a Yale student? The room laughs.

Annoying feature four: we don't realize we'll get used to stuff. She considers this the most insidious. Gilbert and colleagues call it the impact bias: we overestimate both the intensity and the duration of an event's emotional impact. The evidence: Harvard freshmen predicted that being housed in a "good" or a "bad" residential house would make a big difference a year later, and a year later it made almost none; junior faculty predicted that being denied tenure would be devastating, and it wasn't; Paul Eastwick and colleagues found breakups hurt less than people predicted; Sieff and colleagues found that people expected an HIV-positive result to bring near-maximal distress, and the reality was far less bad, with the gap narrowing further a year on. She stresses that we overestimate bad events more than good ones.

Does experience teach us? Ayton and colleagues found a bad event that can repeat: the driving test. Teenagers who failed predicted every time that failing would feel terrible, every time it didn't, and next time they predicted the same.

We don't get better at impact bias as we get more experience with it, which is kind of sad.— Laurie Santos, video 12

Why? She gives Gilbert's two reasons. One is focalism: when predicting, we fixate on the one event and forget everything else in life. Picture testing HIV-positive tomorrow: you think "everything in my life is going to stop," but the you of a year from now is going to classes, hanging out, thinking about summer jobs, eating cupcakes in the dining hall. The other is immune neglect: we have a "psychological immune system" (friends, ice cream, puppy pictures, rationalizing, finding reasons and meaning), and when we forecast, we leave it out entirely. This is what she worries about most for her students' generation:

And this is why I think this annoying feature is one of the most insidious ones because it means you guys don't know how powerful and how resilient you are.— Laurie Santos, video 12

So take the risky class and apply for the long-shot job; failing will feel far less bad than you think. Lecture two ends on a cliffhanger: how do we switch these features off? Next week.

In the Q&A a student challenges the TV studies: maybe heavy TV watchers spend more just because they have more leisure time and buy potato chips to watch with? Santos agrees these are correlational data: "Those kinds of studies haven't been done yet." She predicts that experiments would find the same effects. Another student asks how to handle social comparison; she says comparison is a useful yardstick in principle, but social media's yardstick is fake, because everyone posts only "the super awesome part of the bell curve."

Lecture three turns to remedies, stressing "intentionally": none of these happen on their own, and at first they'll feel forced and a bit kitschy. She works backwards, starting with hedonic adaptation.

First, spend on experiences, not stuff. Stuff is static and sticks around, which makes it easiest to adapt to. She quotes Gilbert twice: the worst thing about the new car is that it lasts, "It will stay around to disappoint you," whereas a trip to Europe ends: "It has the good sense to go away and leave you with nothing but your wonderful memory." The evidence comes from Van Boven and Gilovich: thinking back on a purchase over $100 made to be happy, experiential purchases brought more happiness at the time and in retrospect, and felt like money better spent. She says the pattern holds at every income level, growing stronger as income rises. Kumar and colleagues add a layer: waiting for a ski trip feels like excitement, waiting for a package feels like impatience. Yet people don't believe it: forecasting in advance, they expect experiences to be about half as good as material purchases, while their actual ratings two and four weeks later say the opposite. Also, people who talk about experiences come across as more likeable, and experiences are harder to compare, so Howell and Hill found they provoke less envy.

Second, thwart adaptation to what you already have. Start with savoring: stepping outside an experience to appreciate it. Citing Jose and colleagues, she says sharing with others, thinking how lucky you are, and being absorbed in the present strengthen savoring; thinking about when it will end, deciding it wasn't as good as hoped, or telling yourself you don't deserve it weaken it. Replaying memories helps too: she describes Lyubomirsky and colleagues having students spend eight minutes, three days a week, replaying a happy memory like rewinding a videotape, with more positive emotion four weeks later.

Then negative visualization: imagining the good thing never happened. Her example is It's a Wonderful Life, where Jimmy Stewart sees the world as it would be had he never been born. Koo and colleagues had couples spend fifteen minutes writing about how they might never have met; controls wrote about how they did meet. The first group reported more relationship satisfaction and more happiness overall, by about a whole point, she says. Related is treating this day as your last: Kurtz had graduating seniors write about college life, telling one group they had only about 1,200 hours left and the other "a quarter of a year." Well-being rose in the first group and actually fell in the second.

Last comes what she calls the most powerful technique: gratitude. Emmons and McCullough had people list five things they were grateful for each week, against controls listing five hassles or five neutral events. After a few weeks the gratitude group rated their lives as a whole about half a point higher, felt more optimistic about the coming week, reported fewer physical symptoms, and exercised almost an hour more per week. In Seligman's "gratitude visit," you write a letter to someone you never properly thanked and deliver it in person; she says the boost was immediate and still visible three months later. At work, Grant and Gino found that university fundraisers who got one sentence of thanks in person from a supervisor made 50% more voluntary calls.

Third, reset your reference points. She first adds a study she didn't get to last week: eating potato chips with a fine chocolate on the table, or with a can of unappetizing sardines. She says people were almost twice as likely to enjoy the chips next to the sardines and enjoyed them less next to the chocolate. Then the techniques: concretely re-experience your old life (go back to the bad job you had before Google, or spend eight minutes replaying it); concretely observe the life you envy, which often turns out to have your problems too; against social comparison, use the "stop" technique from cognitive behavioral therapy (say "stop" out loud when you catch yourself comparing) and fill your attention with gratitude, because:

Gratitude is a kind of killer of envy.— Laurie Santos, video 17

Beyond that, curate what you let in, or simply delete social media. Since she was giving this lecture on a Sunday, she had deleted Twitter and Facebook from her phone the week before: "I didn't delete my accounts to be fair." A week off them, she self-reports feeling a lot happier.

Then two counterintuitive ones. Interrupt consumption: Nelson and colleagues had people listen to a favorite song; with a break inserted, enjoyment jumped back up when the song returned. A sitcom with commercials was liked more than the same sitcom without them, even though everyone predicted the ads would spoil it. So split good things up; and do the reverse with bad things, getting them over in one go, since every return to them hurts afresh. Finally, increase variety: the same ice cream is dull by the fifth day; space things out and mix them up and the reference point resets. That is one more reason experiences beat stuff: stuff stays the same, while experiences keep changing.

She closes by squaring the books: last lecture said the things on your list won't make you happy; now you know why, and with these strategies they can make you happy again. Only feature one remains: beyond making the things we want good again, what are the things we don't even want that really would make us happy? That's next week. In the Q&A, someone asks whether visiting grandma is really as good as a trip to French Polynesia. She answers that the mind tracks change, not the size of the change:

And so this is kind of the crazy thing about positive and negatives and and one of the things that comes from not recognizing absolutes is that what our mind notices is change.— Laurie Santos, video 18

Beneath the surface

The potato-chip study was told backwards. It is Morewedge, Gilbert, Myrseth, Kassam and Wilson's 2010 paper in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, titled "Consuming experience: Why affective forecasters overestimate comparative value." Each experiment used 30 to 98 Harvard students; half only predicted, half actually ate Godiva chocolate, Lay's potato chips and Beach Cliff sardines. Forecasters expected the chocolate or the sardines to change how the chips tasted; experiencers liked the chips equally in both conditions (F < 1). The authors' explanation: comparing takes cognitive resources, and while you're actually eating, the food itself takes them up. That is the reverse of Santos's version, and it is in fact the best illustration of her feature four (we mispredict our own feelings), used instead to prove feature two.

"At every income level" is not what the paper says. Van Boven and Gilovich's 2003 national survey, run by Harris Interactive by phone, covered 1,263 people: 57% said experiences made them happier, 34% said possessions. But the paper states that income was positively associated with favoring experiences, "so much so that respondents with the lowest levels of income were equally likely to indicate that material or experiential purchases made them happier"; the least educated even leaned slightly toward possessions. The authors' own explanation is that people with little discretionary income must first meet basic needs. In 2018 Lee, Hall and Wood nailed this down in Psychological Science: lower-class participants showed no experiential advantage, getting equal or more happiness from material goods, and imagining a halved monthly income made the advantage disappear. Santos told Yale students their median income would be around $76,000; for them the advice holds; for everyone, it needs a discount.

Adaptation to lotteries and income is messier than the slide's curves. Brickman's 1978 study had just 22 Illinois lottery winners and 22 controls, with an average prize of $1.18 million. Di Tella and colleagues' German panel covered 7,812 people from 1984 to 2000 and found that about 65% of the happiness from an income rise is lost over the next four years: not a line flat from start to finish. They also found that happiness from status does not adapt, and if anything grows over time. For the "$1.40" figure Santos gave no source, and I could not find the original analysis; I report it only as her claim.

The strength of the evidence varies a lot. Santos mixes correlational studies and experiments, and in the Q&A she admitted the TV studies are all correlational. The Facebook comparison needs care: she set a "Facebook and self-esteem" correlation against an "income and happiness" correlation; the outcomes differ, so "twice as big" can't be turned into "staying off social media beats a $100,000 salary." The original Olympic study analyzed footage from the 1992 Barcelona Games; the 2008 Phelps photo was only her illustration.

Most of what she describes is misprediction, not experience itself. Look closely and the studies in these lectures that measured both forecasts and outcomes (Koo's never-met couples, Nelson's commercials, the experiential-purchase forecasts) all found the same thing: people mispredict how they will feel. That fits twenty years of Gilbert and Wilson's work. Their 1998 paper "Immune neglect" used six studies (a breakup, tenure denial, an electoral defeat, negative personality feedback, an account of a child's death, rejection by an employer) to show that people overestimate how long bad events will hurt and can't tell which situations will trigger their psychological immune system. Santos's four annoying features are the teaching version of that research program.

What happened next

Partly held · as of 2026-10

Reference points, hedonic adaptation and the impact bias remain mainstream, and the Olympic medal finding replicated. But adaptation is incomplete: big lottery wins and disability leave lasting marks, and the size of the impact bias depends on how you ask. The remedies work, with effects much smaller than the lectures suggest.

2007: adaptation has limits. Richard Lucas used two national panels from Germany and Britain (about 40,000 and 27,000 people) to follow 679 and 272 people before and after the onset of a disability, for around seven years on average. Disability brought drops of 0.40 to 1.27 standard deviations, followed by "little adaptation over time." His review the same year added unemployment, divorce and widowhood to the list of lasting changes. Hedonic adaptation is real, but it is not a thermostat that returns every shock to baseline.

2012–2013: the impact bias taken apart. Levine, Lench, Kaplan and Safer's "Accuracy and artifact," in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, used an election, exam grades and a meta-analysis to show that when asked how they will feel about the event itself, people predict quite accurately; overestimation shows up mainly in designs that ask about general mood and later don't mention the event. Wilson and Gilbert replied in 2013 that the impact bias is alive and well; Levine and colleagues replied in turn under the title "Like Schrödinger's cat, the impact bias is both dead and alive." I have read only the citations of the two replies and don't restate their arguments. The safe reading today: people do overestimate, but part of the size reported in early studies came from how the question was asked.

2020: the lottery study redone. Lindqvist, Östling and Cesarini's Swedish lottery study in the Review of Economic Studies surveyed 3,362 players 5 to 22 years after their win, under an analysis plan archived before they saw the data. Large-prize winners showed sustained increases in life satisfaction with "no evidence of dissipating with time," about 0.037 SD per $100,000 after tax; effects on happiness and mental health were much smaller and not statistically significant. Rescaling Brickman 1978 to the same units, they got an estimate of 0.014 with a standard error of 0.025: almost the same as their own happiness estimate, only far less precise. So "lottery winners adapt within a year" roughly holds for day-to-day mood, and fails for how people rate their lives.

2021: the Olympic medals held. Hedgcock, Luangrath and Webster gathered podium photos from the 2000–2016 Games and ran a conceptual replication with automated facial coding. The results supported silver looking less happy than bronze, and also supported a second explanation, proposed by McGraw, Mellers and Tetlock in 2005: silver medalists had higher expectations going in, so their disappointment comes from missed expectations, not only from "almost gold."

2016 on: the size of the remedies. Davis and colleagues' 2016 meta-analysis of gratitude interventions found that, against measurement-only controls, they raised psychological well-being by d = 0.31 (just five studies); against comparison activities, d = 0.17; against psychologically active comparison exercises, d = −0.03, no advantage. The gratitude effect Santos describes is real, but most "psychological exercises" deliver a similar lift; gratitude isn't special.

2020: the Facebook deactivation experiment. Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow, in the American Economic Review, randomly assigned some of 2,844 American users to deactivate Facebook for four weeks. Their subjective well-being index rose 0.09 SD; on a seven-point happiness scale they scored 0.12 above controls (control mean 4.47). The authors put this at about 25–40 percent of the effect of standard psychological interventions. It is the cleanest test of Santos's advice: right direction, small, nowhere near "beats a $100,000 salary."

Crossovers

  • Stoicism · premeditatio malorum: the English label "negative visualization" was used by William Irvine's 2009 A Guide to the Good Life for this Stoic practice; Epictetus advises saying, as you kiss your child, that it may die tomorrow. Same structure: imagine losing it so the thing in front of you is seen again. Different aim: Santos wants to pull happiness out of adaptation; the Stoics wanted not to be broken when the loss came, with joy as a by-product.
  • Neuroscience · Schultz's reward prediction error: Wolfram Schultz found that monkeys' dopamine neurons fire for unexpected rewards, stop firing for fully predicted ones, and dip below baseline when an expected reward fails to come. It looks like a cellular version of "the mind notices change." The difference: it is a learning signal, not subjective pleasure itself.
  • Electronics · AC coupling: an AC-coupled amplifier uses a capacitor to block the steady DC component and amplify only changes. Hedonic adaptation is like such a capacitor on our emotions; unlike it, the capacitor is fixed, while Lucas's data show some shocks move the DC baseline itself.
  • Evolutionary biology · relative fitness: natural selection counts how many more offspring you leave than your rivals, not the absolute number. A brain that tracks relative standing makes evolutionary sense. Unlike then, today's comparisons include Beyoncé on a screen, who competes with you for nothing, and the brain compares anyway.
  • Mindfulness · the raisin exercise: in Jon Kabat-Zinn's Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, an early session often has people spend several minutes slowly eating a single raisin. Like savoring, it pins attention to present experience; unlike it, mindfulness doesn't select for the pleasant, and unpleasant feelings are to be seen clearly too, while savoring amplifies only the good part.

Ten takeaways

  1. "Miswanting" is Gilbert and Wilson's term: the brain tells us we want something but often misjudges how much we will later like it.
  2. The mind judges against reference points, not absolutes; Olympic silver medalists fix on gold and so often look less happy than bronze medalists who fix on no medal.
  3. The worst reference point is other people: the more your colleagues earn, the less you like your job, even when your own income is unchanged.
  4. Hedonic adaptation turns a new job, a new car or a new marriage into "normal," and the pleasure they bring fades over time.
  5. The impact bias means overestimating how strongly and how long events will affect us, driven by focalism and neglect of our psychological immune system.
  6. Teenagers who repeatedly fail their driving test overestimate the pain every time, so the impact bias does not fade with experience.
  7. Experiences resist adaptation and comparison better than things, but the experiential advantage appears mainly among people with money to spare and vanishes at low incomes.
  8. In Morewedge and colleagues' potato-chip experiment, only forecasters thought nearby chocolate or sardines would change the taste; people actually eating were unaffected.
  9. A Swedish lottery study found big prizes raise life satisfaction for over a decade without fading; "winners quickly adapt" applies only to day-to-day mood.
  10. Gratitude exercises and quitting Facebook do help, but by only about 0.1 to 0.3 standard deviations, and gratitude does no better than other psychological exercises.
