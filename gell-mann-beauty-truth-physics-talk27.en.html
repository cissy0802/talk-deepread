"Seven experiments, all wrong": he picked the right theory by its beauty, then said that beauty has nothing to do with us

Murray Gell-Mann · Beauty, truth and ... physics?

March 2007, the TED stage. A theoretical physicist nearing eighty is describing a theory of the weak force that he and a few colleagues put forward in 1957. He pauses and repeats the number:

And it was in disagreement with seven -- seven, count them, seven experiments. Experiments were all wrong.— Murray Gell-Mann, TED2007

They published without waiting for the experiments to be fixed, "because we figured it was so beautiful, it's gotta be right!" The experiments were in fact redone, and the results came over to the theory's side.

The speaker is Murray Gell-Mann. He gave the quark its name and won the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physics for classifying the elementary particles. His 16-minute talk asks one question: in fundamental physics, why does beauty work so well as a way of choosing the right theory?

First he says what beauty means. If some mathematical notation lets you write a theory down briefly, without much complication, that is what physicists call beauty or elegance. Then he gives his explanation. Physicists dig into ever smaller scales, like peeling an onion, and each new skin turns out to need almost the same mathematics as the one before. Newton's gravity and Coulomb's electric force both fall off as the inverse square of distance. Rotational symmetry, and then the symmetry of special relativity, make Maxwell's equations shorter and shorter. The new symmetry Yang and Mills found by generalizing Maxwell's equations ended up describing the strong and weak forces. Each new skin uses mathematics we already have, so it can be written briefly, so it looks beautiful.

From this he draws a hard conclusion: the beauty has nothing to do with human beings. Aliens with seven tentacles, fourteen compound eyes and a pretzel-shaped brain would find the same laws. That the skins of the onion resemble each other is a property of nature, not a preference of the human mind.

What the audience did not hear is that the "too beautiful to be wrong" story is far more cautious on paper. The 1958 paper by Feynman and Gell-Mann states outright that the clash with a helium-6 experiment is "the most serious disagreement" and that "further check on this is obviously very desirable." In a review from the same period, Gell-Mann and a co-author even called the idea behind the theory its "last stand." The confidence on stage came afterwards.

What happened next? Partly stands. All of his examples hold up, and Yang–Mills theory became the backbone of the Standard Model. What he left out is that beauty is a rule of thumb that only works while the skins keep resembling each other. After the talk, the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) found the Higgs boson but none of the supersymmetric particles that many physicists thought were too beautiful not to exist. In 2018 the theoretical physicist Sabine Hossenfelder wrote a whole book arguing that beauty had led physics astray. Earlier, the philosopher of science James McAllister had offered a rival explanation: what physicists find beautiful is learned from the theories that worked before. That fits neatly with Gell-Mann's own phrase, "because they use mathematics we already have," and contradicts his claim that beauty has nothing to do with us.

The moment

A few minutes in, Gell-Mann turns to 1957. "Three or four of us" had proposed a theory of the weak force, partial but beautiful. Then he holds up the number:

And it was in disagreement with seven -- seven, count them, seven experiments. Experiments were all wrong.— Murray Gell-Mann, TED2007
And we published before knowing that, because we figured it was so beautiful, it's gotta be right! The experiments had to be wrong, and they were.— Murray Gell-Mann, TED2007

He adds a bigger name straight away. When Einstein heard that a man called D. C. Miller (Dayton Miller) had an experiment that seemed to disagree with special relativity, Gell-Mann says, he answered, "Aw, that'll go away." The audience laughed.

Theory fights experiment, theory wins, and it wins on beauty. Physicists love this kind of story, and it is also the most dangerous kind. This page puts it back into the original papers.

The speaker, and the year

Gell-Mann was one of the most important theoretical physicists of the second half of the twentieth century. He brought order to the zoo of elementary particles, named the quark, and won the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physics. He taught at Caltech for decades. In 1984 he helped found the Santa Fe Institute to study complex systems, and he took linguistics, archaeology and evolutionary biology as seriously as physics. At the Nobel banquet he said something very much in character: the beauty of the basic laws of nature is "allied to the litheness of a merganser diving in a pure Swedish lake."

By the time he took the TED stage in 2007 he was nearly eighty, and physics was waiting. CERN's Large Hadron Collider was about to switch on, and many theorists expected it to see beautiful new physics such as supersymmetry. The screen showed portraits of his "colleagues," Newton and Einstein, and his first line was a joke about them. Halfway through he greeted the Einstein portrait: "Hi, Al!"

So this was more than an old man's memories. On the eve of the experiment that would audit the bet on beauty, one of the most successful people ever to make that bet was defending it.

Glossary

  • Weak force: one of the four fundamental forces, responsible for some kinds of radioactive decay, such as beta decay.
  • V-A theory: the 1957 theory of the weak force that allows only one form of interaction, "vector minus axial vector." It is the beautiful theory Gell-Mann describes.
  • Yang–Mills theory: a generalization of Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism with a new kind of symmetry. In today's Standard Model, both the strong and weak forces are written in this form.
  • Supersymmetry (SUSY): the hypothesis that every known particle has a "partner" particle. It was widely favored for its mathematical elegance, and none of the partners has been found.

The talk, all the way through

Gell-Mann starts small. His field asks what is left if you chop matter up very, very fine. We know a lot about four forces, and more may be hiding at very small distances. Then he asks the talk's only question: in this field, beauty has turned out to be a very successful criterion for choosing the right theory, "And why on earth could that be so?"

V-A and its seven experiments are the first piece of evidence, and Einstein and Miller are the second. Then he takes on a deeper question: does this beauty have anything to do with human beings? He gives the answer away early: no. He asks us to picture intelligent beings in another galaxy with seven tentacles, fourteen funny-looking compound eyes and a brain shaped like a pretzel:

Would they really have different laws? There are lots of people who believe that, and I think it is utter baloney.— Murray Gell-Mann, TED2007

The laws are "really there," and we close in on them a little at a time. Then comes his definition, the line from the talk most worth keeping:

And when the mathematics is very simple -- when in terms of some mathematical notation, you can write the theory in a very brief space, without a lot of complication -- that's essentially what we mean by beauty or elegance.— Murray Gell-Mann, TED2007

He stops to correct a popular phrase: the final unified theory should not be called a "theory of everything." Because it is quantum mechanical, it predicts only probabilities. The history of the universe is set by the fundamental law plus "this incredibly long series of accidents," and much of the information around us comes from those accidents.

Then comes the central image, the onion. As physicists look from low energies to high and from large distances to small, each skin has something in common with its neighbors and needs very similar mathematics. Newton called nature "conformable to herself." The simplest example: Newton's gravity weakens with the square of the distance, and Coulomb in France found the same law for electric charges. A richer example is symmetry. Written out with x, y and z components, Maxwell's equations are a long list. Rotational symmetry in vector notation makes them shorter, and the symmetry of special relativity makes them shorter still. He puts the three versions on screen and tells the audience they don't need to know what they mean: "you can just look at the form." Laughter.

The crucial step is Chen Ning Yang ("called by us 'Frank' Yang") and Bob Mills, who fifty years earlier had generalized Maxwell's equations with a whole new symmetry. On its own, their work did nothing for particle physics. "But then some of us generalized it further. And then it did!" It gave a beautiful description of both the strong force and the weak force. From this comes his explanation:

And the kind of mathematics that we had for the previous skin is almost the same as what we need for the next skin. And that's why the equations look so simple. Because they use mathematics we already have.— Murray Gell-Mann, TED2007

The turn comes with Newton. The force that pulls the apple down is the same force that keeps the moon in orbit. That was a great unification in its day, and who would now call it "a mere conceit of the human mind"? It is a property of gravity. In the same way, the resemblance between the skins of the onion is a property of the fundamental law. These principles are not extra metaphysical postulates; they are "emergent properties." Then he pushes the word as far as it goes. Life emerges from physics and chemistry plus a great many accidents, and the human mind emerges from neurobiology plus accidents. Readers of his book The Quark and the Jaguar keep asking whether there isn't "something more." His last line:

You don't need something more to explain something more.— Murray Gell-Mann, TED2007

Below the waterline

The original version of "seven experiments, all wrong." On stage the story goes like this: we didn't know about the conflict when we published, we bet on beauty, and the experiments later turned out to be wrong. The paper tells it differently. Feynman and Gell-Mann's "Theory of the Fermi Interaction" was received on 16 September 1957 and published in Physical Review 109 on 1 January 1958. Its abstract already says the theory disagrees with the measured electron–neutrino angular correlation in helium-6. The body is more specific (as quoted in Hadjiivanov's historical survey):

Limiting ourselves to those that are well established, we find that the most serious disagreement with our theory is the recoil experiment in He6 of Rustad and Ruby indicating that the T interaction is more likely than the A. Further check on this is obviously very desirable.— Feynman & Gell-Mann, Physical Review 109, 1958

So they knew about the conflict when they published, and they said so in print. Their tone was "please measure this again," not "the experiments are wrong." Gell-Mann's other writing from that time is even more striking. A review he published with Rosenfeld in December 1957 called the "V, A hypothesis" the "'last stand'" of a universal Fermi interaction. To accept it, they wrote, "we must first of all disregard much of the evidence" on electron–neutrino correlation in beta decay, especially Rustad and Ruby's helium-6 result: "This is already a very serious objection." The confidence he showed on stage does not appear in what he wrote at the time.

The count differs too. On stage it was seven. Sudarshan and Marshak at Rochester, who proposed the same theory independently, counted "one published and several unpublished experiments" in their 1957 conference paper. They concluded that "All of these experiments should be redone," and said that if "any of the above four experiments stands," the hypothesis would have to be abandoned. They bet on the theory too, but they spelled out the conditions of the bet.

Beauty wasn't the only motive, either. Sudarshan and Marshak described the vector–axial mixture as "the only universal four-fermion interaction which is possible," and only after that as one that "possesses many elegant features." Uniqueness and universality came first, elegance second. Late in life Feynman told the historian Jagdish Mehra that the law "wasn't as beautiful a law as Dirac's or Maxwell's," but it was the only time in his career he had known a law of nature that no one else knew.

The ending was as Gell-Mann said. The neutrino's left-handedness was confirmed by the end of 1957, and over the next couple of years the data said to contradict V-A were carefully re-examined and corrected. Sheldon Glashow put it this way in 2009: "The experiments were redone with results that now confirmed their hypothesis." The bet paid off, but it was a bet whose risks had been stated openly, with a list of what needed checking.

Einstein's remark. Gell-Mann gives no source for "Aw, that'll go away," and I could not find one, so it stands only as his anecdote. Miller's "ether drift" is a famous anomaly, and it did go away. In 2006 Thomas Roberts reanalyzed Miller's raw data and concluded that Miller's 1933 claim to have measured the Earth's "absolute motion" was not statistically significant: flaws in the analysis had produced a false signal that happened to look like what Miller expected.

The Newton lines. "Conformable to herself" checks out. Query 31 of the Opticks reads "For Nature is very consonant and conformable to her self." The longer passage about not wanting to be "accounted an extravagant freak" I could not find in the printed Opticks, and Gell-Mann does not say which text it comes from, so this page leaves it unjudged.

"Some of us." The key step that turned Yang–Mills into a theory of the strong force was the 1973 paper by Fritzsch, Gell-Mann and Leutwyler. It built a Yang–Mills gauge model from colored quarks and color-octet gluons, which became quantum chromodynamics (QCD). The weak-force half was mainly the work of Glashow, Steven Weinberg and Abdus Salam. On stage, "some of us" folded two separate histories, made by different people, into one.

What happened next

PARTLY STANDS · as of 2026-10

His historical cases all hold up. The general claim that beauty is a reliable guide to the right theory, and has nothing to do with us, has been challenged by name in the LHC era: the most favored "beautiful" new physics did not show up, and there is a rival, human-centered account of where the sense of beauty comes from that explains the record just as well.

2007–2012: waiting for the audit. When Gell-Mann gave the talk, many expected the LHC to see supersymmetry. The LHC found the Higgs boson, the last missing piece of the Standard Model. Beyond that, as a 2018 book review in Nature put it, the most natural version of supersymmetry, the one that needs no fine-tuning, has been ruled out by LHC data, and "everyone agrees that supersymmetry is in trouble."

2018: Hossenfelder. Sabine Hossenfelder, then a theoretical physicist at the Frankfurt Institute for Advanced Studies, published Lost in Math: How Beauty Leads Physics Astray. Her opening question could almost be addressed to Gell-Mann: "Why should the laws of nature care about what I find beautiful?" Her argument is that experimental data have become harder to get, so theorists rely more and more on mathematical arguments and personal taste to judge theories. She calls supersymmetry a "superstimulus" for physicists because it combines everything they value: symmetry, naturalness and unification. She does not ask physicists to give up mathematics. She proposes ground rules instead: make sure there is a real conflict between theory and data, state your aesthetic assumptions openly, and let empirical evidence decide which mathematics fits.

The other side has specific arguments too. Anil Ananthaswamy, reviewing the book in Nature, notes that theorizing without data has paid off before, and his example is Gell-Mann himself: in the early 1960s he used symmetry to bring order to the particles and predict quarks, and the mathematics turned out to be right. The philosopher of physics Jeremy Butterfield's long 2019 review mostly praises the book but opens with his main disagreements, which center on supersymmetry, naturalness and the multiverse. Supersymmetry researchers have not given up: a 2025 review by Baer and colleagues argues that the LHC search "has only just begun to explore the interesting regimes of parameter space."

A rival account: beauty is learned. More than twenty years before Hossenfelder, the philosopher of science James McAllister proposed "aesthetic induction" in Beauty and Revolution in Science (1996). A scientific community's preference for an aesthetic feature is proportional to how well past theories with that feature have worked. Scientists first produce theories that succeed empirically, then learn to admire some of their features. (I have not read the book itself; this summary follows Jesús Zamora Bonilla's 2015 account.)

Read that next to Gell-Mann's own words. His mechanism is that the new skin "looks beautiful because we already know how to write it in a lovely, concise way." "We already know" is a statement about people. The skins of the onion may well resemble each other by nature, but between "they resemble each other" and "so they look beautiful" sits the physicist's familiar mathematical toolbox. His explanation also predicts when the rule of thumb will fail: as soon as the next skin stops resembling the last, the familiar mathematics can no longer write it briefly, and beauty will point the wrong way. What happened to supersymmetry at the LHC can be read as a test of that sentence.

Gell-Mann died in Santa Fe on 24 May 2019, aged 89. I found no public reply from him to Hossenfelder. Today, roughly: nobody denies that beauty once worked. Why it worked, and whether it will keep working at the next skin, is still waiting for a verdict from the LHC and the colliders after it.
