TEDxBoston · 2012

God doesn't employ accountants: the father of disruption turns his theories on his own life

Clayton Christensen · How Will You Measure Your Life?

Clayton Christensen asks the audience a question that sounds like a joke. At Lucent, who decided the company should go out and get killed? And on what date did they decide it?

People laugh. Lucent, along with Nortel, was one of the giants of telecom equipment, and it built circuit-switching gear. Then a small company called Cisco built the router. It was good enough to carry data but not good enough to carry voice, so Cisco started at the bottom of the market with data and kept moving up until it killed Lucent and Nortel. The answer, of course, is that nobody made that decision. Everyone inside did their own part right, and together they added up to a disaster.

That is his theory of disruptive innovation, made famous by The Innovator's Dilemma in 1997. In 2012, at sixty, he took it to TEDxBoston, and he wasn't there to talk about companies. He was there to talk about people. On the last day of his Harvard Business School course, he stopped asking students to analyze one more company and told them to look in the mirror. Using this term's theories, can you explain why your life is the way it is? If you keep doing what you're doing now, can you predict where it will go?

His own answer came from class reunions. He graduated from HBS in 1979, and his class meets every five years. At the fifth, everyone was happy. By the tenth, fifteenth, twentieth and twenty-fifth, more and more of them came back unhappy and divorced, their children being raised across the country by remarried ex-spouses and estranged from them. He guarantees that not one of his classmates graduated planning to get divorced or to raise children who hated them, yet a large share of them carried out exactly that strategy.

He says the mechanism is the same one that killed Lucent. When an ambitious person has a spare half hour or a little extra energy, they instinctively give it to whatever shows results soonest: a closed sale, a shipped product, a promotion, a paycheck. Family pays off twenty years later, and in the meantime the kids misbehave every day. Companies put money into projects that hit the income statement tomorrow, and people put time into work that shows results tomorrow. Each makes a sensible choice in every present moment, and each ends up somewhere it never planned to go.

The second idea is more personal. Christensen was once sure he would be chosen to lead a large institution, and they chose someone else. So he wondered how he would know his life had been worth living if it wasn't that kind of job. His conclusion was that "God doesn't employ accountants or statisticians." Finite minds have to add things up into reports, and that adding up is where hierarchy comes from. It's why we measure people by rank and money. An infinite mind has no need to aggregate. At the end of his life, he imagines, God won't ask how high he climbed on the org chart. God will ask about the individual people he helped.

So what happened to these ideas? The theory behind "it's the same mechanism" took two heavy hits. In 2014 the historian Jill Lepore went through the cases in his books for The New Yorker and called them handpicked. In 2015 Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh asked 79 industry experts about the 77 cases he had cited, and only 9% fit all four elements of the theory. Even Lucent's real ending was messier than the one he told on stage: in 2004 the SEC charged that it had improperly booked about $1.1 billion of revenue in fiscal 2000. The life half holds up better. The Harvard Study of Adult Development followed 724 men for more than seventy years and found that good relationships keep people happier and healthier. A 2015 set of four experiments found that small ethical lapses really do slide step by step into bigger ones. The verdict is partly holds. The advice stands, but the proof that it's the same mechanism that topples companies is weaker than it sounded on stage.

The moment

Partway through the story of how Cisco brought down Lucent, Christensen stops and asks an absurd question on everyone's behalf:

"I wonder who decided at Lucent that they should go out and get killed?" (Laughter) "And when was the date on which they decided they would get killed?"— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

The laugh makes sense, because the answer is obvious: there was no such person and no such day. But the question is there for a reason. A few minutes later he puts the very same question to his business-school classmates. Which of you decided to get divorced, and on what date? Which of you decided your children should hate you? The whole talk rests on those two questions being one question.

The speaker, and the year

Christensen stood six feet eight inches tall. He was a devout member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, a Rhodes scholar and a 1979 Harvard MBA, and he joined the HBS faculty in 1992. His doctoral work on the disk-drive industry grew into the theory of disruptive innovation. On that theory, good incumbent companies don't fall because they are lazy or stupid. They fall because they listen too well to their best customers, so they dismiss the cheaper, cruder products climbing up from the bottom of the market. The Innovator's Dilemma (1997) made "disruption" the common language of business. After his death, The Harvard Crimson's obituary noted that years into his fame he still drove a beat-up Honda Accord to work, and that when he met newcomers at church he handed them his business card with his home phone number.

The content of this talk wasn't new in 2012. A former student remembers him using the last lecture of a 1998 course to remind the class of what they owed to others. In 2010 the HBS graduating class asked him to speak, but not about using his theories in their careers. They wanted to know how to use them in their personal lives. That summer Harvard Business Review turned the talk into an article titled How Will You Measure Your Life?, which he wrote the year after a cancer diagnosis. In 2012 he published a book of the same name with James Allworth and Karen Dillon and gave this talk at TEDxBoston. On stage he says he is "unfortunately 60 years old now," his five children have all left home, and "life has become boring." He is speaking to a room of entrepreneurs and ambitious people.

Glossary

  • Disruptive innovation: an entrant takes the bottom of a market with a cheaper, cruder product that incumbents don't think is worth fighting for, then improves and moves up until it is too late for them to respond.
  • Jobs-to-be-done: people buy things because a job comes up in their lives and they "hire" a product to do it. The thing to study is the job, not the buyer's characteristics.
  • Resource allocation process: where a company actually puts its money and people. Christensen argues that the real strategy is whatever comes out of this process, not whatever is written in the strategy document.
  • RONA, IRR: return on net assets and internal rate of return, two common profitability measures. Both are ratios, so you can raise them by growing the numerator or by shrinking the denominator.

The talk, in full

He opens with a structure: the world is nested. Nations contain industries, which contain corporations, then business units, then teams, then people, and people contain brains. Then he gives "theory" a strict definition: a statement of causality, an account of what causes what and why.

He lays out three of his theories. The first is disruption: successful companies don't fall because they stop working hard. They fall because someone comes in at the bottom of the market and moves up, and the incumbents' own pursuit of profit is what makes success so hard to keep. The second, the "preservation of modularity," gets one sentence: it explains why the euro doesn't work and why SAP implementations are so hard. The third is jobs-to-be-done, and he demonstrates it on himself. He's Clay, he's 60, he lives in the suburbs, his five children have moved out and life has gotten boring, and none of those characteristics causes him to buy The New York Times. Characteristics may correlate with buying, but they don't cause anything. What causes a purchase is a job that comes up in your life.

the insight there is that the customer is the wrong unit of analysis, it's the job that we need to understand.— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

The setup can sound like a list of his greatest hits, but it is there to set a rule for the rest of the talk: what follows is about causes, not life lessons. He and his colleagues found, "inadvertently," that these causal statements hold at every level of the nested system. They explain why Japan "was so successful and then died," why America finds it hard to regain its momentum, and so on all the way down to teams. So can you keep going down, all the way to a single person?

That brings him to his course. All term, students use the theories as lenses on companies, economies and industries: can they explain why things are happening as they are, and which actions would lead to which results? On the last day he stops asking them to look at one more company and has them look in the mirror instead:

"Can you explain why your life is the way it is today because of these theories?", and "Can you predict what will happen in your life if you continue to do what you are now doing?"— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

He calls the students' last-day answers remarkable: they explain themselves with causal theories, then name what they need to change so that their lives turn out the way they hoped. He offers two of these ideas.

The first: successful companies and successful people fail by the same mechanism. He starts with the companies. A few years earlier, Lucent and Nortel were the giants of telecom and made circuit-switching equipment. Cisco was "not very consequential." Its router wasn't good enough for voice, so it went to the bottom of the market with data, moved up, and killed Lucent and Nortel. Why didn't the giants respond? From where they stood, the router was worse on every dimension, so they kept making better and better circuit switches.

Then comes the absurd question: who decided Lucent should get killed, and when? Nobody decided.

In fact, what happened is all the individual people in a very successful organization did everything right, but because they did all of these things independently and what made sense in those circumstances, when it summed up, it summed up to disaster.— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

Why does it add up to disaster? Everyone is maximizing profitability, and the usual way of calculating it favors investments that pay off tomorrow, which reach the bottom line and feel much more tangible than investments that pay off ten years out.

Then he cuts to his classmates. They graduated from HBS in 1979 and hold a reunion every five years. At the fifth reunion everyone was happy, and he slips in a joke: most of his classmates had married people much better looking than his classmates. (Laughter.) Careers were going well too. But by the tenth, fifteenth, twentieth and twenty-fifth reunions, "oh my gosh," friends were coming back unhappy with their lives. Many were divorced, and their ex-spouses had remarried and were raising his classmates' children on the other side of the country, estranged from their parents.

I guarantee that none of my classmates ever planned when they graduated from the business school to go out, and get divorced, and have children who hate their guts— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

Yet a large share of them carried out a strategy they never planned, and that is management language. Your strategy isn't what you write down. It is what you actually put resources into. The cause, he says, is "the very same mechanism," the pursuit of achievement:

when you have an extra ounce of energy or 30 minutes of time, instinctively and unconsciously, you'll allocate it to whatever activities in your life give you the most immediate evidence of achievement.— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

Careers happen to supply the most tangible, immediate achievements: you close a sale, ship a product, finish a presentation, close a deal, get promoted, get paid. Investments in family take a very long time to pay off. Day after day the children misbehave, and only twenty years later can you put your hands on your hips and say you raised great children. Investing in a spouse or children brings no achievement you can see today. So people who mean to have a happy life, and he calls families the deepest source of happiness, spend their time, energy and talent in a way that carries out a strategy they would never choose.

He ties the two halves together in one line. Successful companies fail because they invest in whatever gives the most immediate, tangible evidence of achievement, and their horizons are short because they are run by people like you and me. "We then apply that very same thinking process in our personal lives with sad results."

The second: how to measure a life. This one starts with a private disappointment. Years earlier, driving to work early one morning on Huron Avenue in Cambridge, he had a feeling that something important was about to happen to Clay Christensen. He would be handed a business opportunity far more consequential than being "a plain, old professor." A couple of weeks later, the person in a certain position announced he was leaving, and Christensen put two and two together: he would be the replacement. The day came, and they chose someone else.

He admits he doesn't know where the feeling came from ("Did the people kind of lose guts? Or... I don't know"). Then he asks a harder question. If they won't make him the leader of a large institution, how will he know whether his life has been worth living? He thought about it for a long time and reached "the strangest conclusion":

I reached the strangest conclusion: that God doesn't employ accountants or statisticians.— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

The argument has three steps. First, finite minds can only understand the world by aggregating. In a company you can't track every invoice, so you roll them up into receivables, payables and revenue. You can't track every cost, so you roll costs into categories, subtract, get a number, and compare it with last year's number. If it's better, you say you're doing better. Second, aggregation has a side effect, which is hierarchy. People who preside over more people seem more important than people who preside over fewer, so we come to feel that those who climb higher will be judged to have lived better lives. Or we measure ourselves by how much money we make. All of this, he says, comes from having limited minds and having to aggregate.

The third step is a short management lesson that looks like a detour: the choice of measure is a big deal. If you measure profitability by return on net assets, which is a ratio, you can raise it by innovating and growing the numerator, or you can outsource everything and shrink the denominator. The ratio doesn't care which. If you follow your finance professors and judge innovation by internal rate of return, you can raise that too, simply by investing only in short-term projects. "And it's just the long way of saying, be careful in how you measure profitability in your company." The point of the detour is that the yardstick you choose ends up shaping how you live.

Then he comes back to God. God's mind is infinite, so God doesn't have to aggregate above the level of individual people to understand the world perfectly. When he realized that, he says, "Oh, my goodness!"

When I have my interview with God at the end of my life, he's not going to show me how high I went into anybody's org-chart, or how much money I left behind in the bank when I died— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

Instead, God will say: Clay, I put you in that circumstance, so let's talk about the individual people you helped become better people, whether because you worked with them, because they were family, or because you just met them and they needed your help. He gives the room this as his second takeaway. It really is important to succeed at what you are succeeding at, but that won't be the measure of your life.

God doesn't count, he doesn't aggregate; he's just going to assess you on the basis of how well you helped other people be better people.— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, 2012

Put side by side, the two ideas have a clear structure. The first is about where resources flow: what makes sense in the short run for small units adds up to long-run failure for large ones. The second is about which yardstick to use: aggregation is a workaround for limited minds, and using it to measure a single life measures the wrong thing. Both make the same point. You have a chance to stop a mechanism from deciding for you only once you can see that it is deciding for you.

Beneath the surface

Two ideas on stage; six in the original. The 2010 Harvard Business Review article turns the last class into three questions. How can I be sure I'll be happy in my career? How can I be sure my relationships with my spouse and family become an enduring source of happiness? How can I be sure I'll stay out of jail? The last one sounds lighthearted, he writes, but it isn't: "Two of the 32 people in my Rhodes scholar class spent time in jail." Enron's Jeff Skilling was his HBS classmate. The article works through six models in turn: Frederick Herzberg's theory of motivation for the career question (what drives people isn't money but learning, growth, contribution and recognition), resource allocation for the family question, the Tools of Cooperation for deliberately building a culture at home, marginal costs for integrity, then humility, and finally the yardstick. The TEDx talk keeps only resource allocation and the yardstick.

"Just this once" is in our reading list's hook, but it isn't in this talk. The hook on this site's list says "just this once" is the most expensive marginal cost. It doesn't appear anywhere in the 2012 transcript. It is in the 2010 article and the 2012 book. The article explains that finance courses teach you to ignore sunk and fixed costs and decide on marginal costs and revenues, and that this doctrine biases companies toward leveraging what they already have rather than building the capabilities they'll need. Applied to ethics, it becomes a voice in your head: as a general rule people shouldn't do this, but in this particular extenuating circumstance, just this once, it's OK. Then he tells his own story. At Oxford he was the starting center on an undefeated varsity basketball team that reached the British equivalent of the NCAA final four, and the championship game was scheduled for a Sunday. At 16 he had promised God never to play ball on Sunday. Every teammate came to him and asked him to break the rule just this once. He prayed about it and did not play.

My life has been one unending stream of extenuating circumstances.— Clayton Christensen, How Will You Measure Your Life?, Harvard Business Review, 2010

His lesson: "it's easier to hold to your principles 100% of the time than it is to hold to them 98% of the time."

Two versions, two disappointments. In the article, the yardstick section opens with illness: "This past year I was diagnosed with cancer." He knows his ideas have generated enormous revenue for the companies that used them, but facing the disease he found that impact unimportant to him. The metric God will use for his life "isn't dollars but the individual people whose lives I've touched." On the TEDx stage the starting point became a job he didn't get, and he added a whole argument about finite minds that can only aggregate. The conclusion is the same, but the reasoning now looks more like his management course. One more difference stands out. On stage he closes with God, plain and simple. The article's editor's note says his thinking comes from deep religious faith but that these are strategies "anyone can use," and he writes himself that faith isn't the only thing that gives people direction.

The way he teaches is itself a theory. The article opens with a story. Before The Innovator's Dilemma came out, Intel chairman Andy Grove gave him ten minutes and asked what disruption meant for Intel. Christensen insisted on first describing the steel industry, where minimills started with the cheapest product, reinforcing bar, and moved up until they undercut the integrated mills. When he finished, Grove worked out Intel's response himself, which became the low-end Celeron processor. "Instead of telling him what to think, I taught him how to think." That's why he seldom answers "what should I do?" directly. He runs the question through a model, shows how it played out in some other industry, and lets the other person reach the answer. The TEDx talk uses exactly this method: first Lucent, then you notice for yourself that the reunions are the same story.

Lucent's real ending was messier than the one on stage. On stage, Lucent dies because Cisco climbed up from below. In May 2004, though, the SEC charged that Lucent had fraudulently and improperly recognized about $1.148 billion of revenue and $470 million of pre-tax income in fiscal 2000. Lucent neither admitted nor denied the allegations and agreed to pay a $25 million penalty for its lack of cooperation. The complaint says the defendants acted "in their drive to realize revenue, meet internal sales targets and/or obtain sales bonuses." So Lucent's decline wasn't only a disruption story. It also involved a bubble and fraud. Oddly enough, that detail fits the life half of the talk better: a group of people chasing the most immediate, tangible results ended up together somewhere none of them planned to go.

The "theories" rest on cases. On stage he defines theory as causal statement, in a tone that sounds almost like physics. What actually supports disruption theory is the disk-drive industry from his dissertation and a set of case studies in The Innovator's Dilemma. In disk drives he counted 116 new technologies and classed 111 as sustaining and 5 as disruptive. As for his classmates, the talk gives no numbers. "A very large portion" and "very many of them" are his observations, not statistics.

What happened next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-10

The life advice has independent support: the quality of relationships tracks happiness and health in old age, and small ethical lapses do slide step by step into larger ones. But the claim that this is the same mechanism that topples companies borrows from a theory whose own cases turned out to fit it poorly, and the Lucent case told on stage had a more complicated ending.

The talk stands on two legs: disruption theory itself, and the move of carrying it into a person's life. Each leg fared very differently.

2014: Lepore checks the cases. The Harvard historian Jill Lepore published "The Disruption Machine" in The New Yorker. She described The Innovator's Dilemma as "a set of handpicked case studies" and went through them one by one. In disk drives, Seagate, which he treated as an incumbent that responded too late, doubled its sales between 1989 and 1990 and was the industry's largest company by 1997. Most of the entrants he celebrated as disrupters no longer exist. In steel, he presented U.S. Steel as disrupted by minimills but never mentioned unions, or the 22,000 workers who didn't go to work in the 1986–87 labor action. Today the largest U.S. steel producer is still U.S. Steel. She also recorded two failed predictions. In March 2000 he launched a $3.8 million Disruptive Growth Fund that was liquidated within a year, having lost 64% while the Nasdaq lost 50%. And in 2007 he said the theory predicted the iPhone wouldn't succeed. Her criticism that bears most directly on this talk is about exporting business-school theory. Schools, churches, hospitals and newsrooms have all been fitted to the disruption template, yet their obligations to students, congregants, patients and readers lie outside earnings. She put it in four words:

People aren’t disk drives.— Jill Lepore, The Disruption Machine, The New Yorker, 2014

According to Electronic Design's account of his interview with Bloomberg Businessweek, Christensen didn't address the disk-drive example. He did say that he had first seen the iPhone as a sustaining innovation. He praised Lepore for trying to rein in loose use of the word "disruption" and invited her across the river to talk. In another interview he said: "I never thought … that the word disruption has so many connotations in the English language, that people would then flexibly take an idea, twist it, and use it to justify whatever they wanted to do in the first place."

2015: 77 cases, 9%. Andrew King of Dartmouth's Tuck School and Baljir Baatartogtokh published "How Useful Is the Theory of Disruptive Innovation?" in MIT Sloan Management Review. They took the 77 cases that Christensen and Michael Raynor's two books offer as examples of disruption and asked 79 industry experts whether the theory's four elements held in each one. Was the incumbent on a sustaining trajectory? Did its improvements overshoot what customers needed? Could it have responded? Did it falter as a result? Only 9% of the cases met all four, even though disagreements among experts were scored in the theory's favor. According to a footnote, a "circuit and packet switched" telecom case was in the sample; I could not read how it was coded. The authors did not call for throwing the theory out. They recommend using its best parts alongside classical strategic analysis. In King's words: "A theory is like a weed. Unless it is pruned back by empirical testing, it will grow to fill any void." In December 2015 Christensen, Raynor and Rory McDonald answered in Harvard Business Review with "What Is Disruptive Innovation?" I could read only its summary page, so I won't restate its arguments.

The life half: the advice holds up better. His premise is that family and close relationships are the deepest source of happiness. The independent evidence most often cited is the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which since 1938 has followed two groups totaling 724 men. Robert Waldinger, the study's fourth director, summed it up at TED in 2015: "Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period." The men most satisfied in their relationships at 50 were the healthiest at 80. The caveats are that the sample is all men and the study is correlational. His "just this once" has experimental support too. In 2015 Welsh, Ordóñez and colleagues published four multi-round studies in the Journal of Applied Psychology. In a series of gradually escalating lapses, people slid further and further, driven by accumulating moral disengagement, and putting people into a prevention focus slowed the slide. The example they cite at the start is the book on Enron's collapse, The Smartest Guys in the Room. Enron was his classmate Skilling's company.

No one has tested the bridge between the two legs. No study shows that "high achievers neglect their families" and "incumbents ignore disruption" share a causal mechanism. They look more like two phenomena with the same shape. His reunion story is an observation, not a statistic. So the most reliable part of the talk is the part that doesn't depend on disruption theory: people give their time to whatever shows results right away, and the wrong yardstick changes how you live.

Christensen died on January 23, 2020, after a year-long battle with leukemia. He was 67 and left his wife Christine, five children and nine grandchildren. In The Harvard Crimson's obituary, colleagues and students said less about his theories than about the time he spent on other people.

Crossovers

  • Behavioral economics · Ainslie's personal rules: In Breakdown of Will (2001), George Ainslie argues that people over-discount rewards that are close at hand. The defense is to bundle a series of choices into one rule, so that any single exception reads as the collapse of the whole rule rather than a small slip. That is nearly a formal version of "100% is easier than 98%." The difference is what holds the line. Ainslie's rules rest on self-prediction ("break it now and I won't keep it later"), while Christensen's rested on a promise to God.
  • Social science · Goodhart's law: The economist Charles Goodhart observed that a statistical regularity tends to break down once it is used as a target for control. Gaming RONA by shrinking the denominator has exactly this structure. The difference is the target: Goodhart was describing policy indicators being gamed, while Christensen worries that the measure quietly reshapes the person who uses it.
  • Complexity science · Schelling's segregation model: Thomas Schelling showed that if each resident only mildly prefers to have some neighbors like themselves, a city can still sort into sharply divided blocks. Like "everyone did everything right and it summed to disaster," each agent is reasonable and the whole fails. The difference is intent: Schelling's residents never wanted the overall pattern, whereas Lucent's employees all wanted the company to win.
  • Western philosophy · Kant's formula of humanity: Kant asks us to treat humanity always as an end and never merely as a means, which refuses to dissolve any person into a total. The difference is scoring. Kant describes duties owed to each person and doesn't count them up, whereas Christensen's God still keeps accounts, just one person at a time.
  • Distributed systems · mean latency vs. p99: A service's average response time can look fine while its slowest 1% of requests are terrible, so engineers watch percentiles and not just means, because averaging erases the individual case. The difference is the fix: engineers switch to a different aggregate, while Christensen's God doesn't aggregate at all.

Ten takeaways

  1. Christensen defines a theory as a statement of causality: what causes what and why, not mere correlation.
  2. Jobs-to-be-done theory says the customer is the wrong unit of analysis; the thing to understand is the job the customer needs done.
  3. No one at Lucent decided to die when Cisco attacked: everyone did something sensible, and it summed to disaster.
  4. Ambitious people instinctively give spare time and energy to whatever shows achievement soonest, usually their careers.
  5. Investment in family pays off only decades later, so people end up carrying out a life strategy they never planned.
  6. Finite minds have to aggregate, aggregation creates hierarchy, and so people judge lives by rank and money.
  7. Ratio measures like RONA and IRR can be raised by outsourcing or by short-term projects, so the yardstick shapes behavior.
  8. The marginal cost of "just this once" always looks low, which is why holding a principle 100% of the time is easier than 98%.
  9. Jill Lepore checked disruption theory's cases one by one in The New Yorker and warned that people aren't disk drives.
  10. When King and Baatartogtokh had 79 experts review 77 cited cases of disruption, only 9% met all four elements of the theory.
