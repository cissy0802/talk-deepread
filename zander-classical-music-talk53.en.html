The conductor makes no sound: a twenty-minute Chopin lesson, and a theory of leadership measured in shining eyes

Benjamin Zander · The transformative power of classical music

On the TED stage in February 2008, a 68-year-old English conductor sits at a piano and plays like a seven-year-old: every note leaned on equally, his head bobbing on each one. At eight the bobs come every two notes, at nine every four, at ten every eight. At eleven there is one impulse for the whole phrase, and his body tips sideways with the line until one buttock leaves the bench. He calls it "one-buttock playing." The room laughs, and half his argument is already made: whether music comes alive depends on whether you are thinking about each note or about the line that runs from beginning to end.

He is Benjamin Zander, conductor of the Boston Philharmonic. The job he sets himself that night sounds absurd. Of the 1,600 people in the hall, some adore classical music, some don't mind it, some never hear it. He will not go on, he says, until every single one of them comes to love and understand it.

His method is to take apart a Chopin prelude. The melody starts on B; the next note is C, and "the job of the C is to make the B sad." Then the line steps down through B, A, G, F, and he stops. What comes next? The audience sings the E without being asked. See, he says, nobody is tone-deaf. Chopin won't let you reach E yet, just as Shakespeare won't let Hamlet kill his uncle in Act One, or the play would be over. Before playing the whole piece he asks everyone to think of someone they loved who is no longer there, and to follow the line from B all the way home to E.

The second half turns from music to people. At 45, after twenty years of conducting, he says, he realized that the conductor doesn't make a sound: his power depends on making other people powerful. How do you know if it's working? Look at the players' eyes. If they aren't shining, ask yourself: "who am I being that my players' eyes are not shining?" Success, for him, is how many shining eyes he has around him. He closes with an Auschwitz survivor's vow: never to say anything that couldn't stand as the last thing she ever says.

The magic trick is real: he turns music theory into drama and an audience into a choir. But several factual claims he makes in passing have met different answers since. "Nobody is tone-deaf" goes too far: in a 2017 test of more than 16,000 adults, about 1.5% had congenital amusia, a lifelong inability to process pitch. The same study found that many people who believe they have no ear test perfectly normal, so that half of his claim holds. The idea that a leader never doubts his people has experimental support: high expectations from a boss really do raise performance. Yet when the Israeli psychologist Dov Eden and colleagues ran seven field experiments trying to teach that stance to managers, the effect was small. As for the claim that everybody loves classical music and just hasn't found out yet: in the National Endowment for the Arts surveys, the share of American adults who attended at least one classical concert in a year fell from 13% in 1982 to 9.3% in the year of the talk, and stood at 4.6% in 2022 (a pandemic-shadowed year). The verdict is "partly holds." Shining eyes are a real leadership skill. "Everybody loves classical music" is still a stance, not a forecast.

The moment

Three-quarters of the way through, Zander has just played the last chord of the Chopin. The hall is applauding, and so is he. He explains: he once did exactly this at a school in Boston with about 70 seventh graders, twelve-year-olds. At the end they went crazy clapping, he was clapping too, and then he asked:

"Why am I clapping?" And one of them said, "Because we were listening."— Benjamin Zander, TED, 2008

Sixteen hundred busy people listening, understanding and being moved by Chopin: "that is something," he says, and at once undercuts himself. Can he be sure every single person followed it? Of course not. This page is about the part he couldn't be sure of.

The speaker, and the year

Zander was born in England in 1939 and began composing at nine. Benjamin Britten noticed his pieces, and he spent summers taking lessons from Britten. He joined the faculty of the New England Conservatory (NEC) in 1967 and has conducted the Boston Philharmonic since 1979, known above all for Mahler and Beethoven. Morley Safer of 60 Minutes called him the "Energizer Bunny of classical music."

By 2008 he was more than a conductor. In 2000 he and his partner Rosamund Stone Zander had published The Art of Possibility with Harvard Business School Press. It became a bestseller and made him a fixture on the leadership-lecture circuit, which is why his TED talk swerves halfway through toward "the leader." Meanwhile the classical music world was worrying about vanishing audiences. His opening joke, the two shoe salesmen sent to Africa, is aimed at that gloom: one cables home "Situation hopeless. Stop. They don't wear shoes," the other "Glorious opportunity. They don't have any shoes yet."

The talk, in full

Rather than go into statistics and trends, he says, and tell you about orchestras closing and record companies folding, let's do an experiment tonight. "Actually, it's not really an experiment, because I know the outcome." It comes in three steps.

Step one is the child who grows from seven to eleven. The change, Zander insists, isn't that the boy suddenly got passionate, found a new teacher or hit puberty. The impulses were reduced: one on every note at seven, every other note at eight, every four at nine, every eight at ten, one for the whole phrase at eleven. Nobody told him to lean; "the music pushed me over." A company president from Ohio who watched him coach a pianist wrote afterwards that he had turned his whole firm into "a one-buttock company." Inside the laugh is the skeleton of the talk: make a point clearly in music, then carry it into the human world.

Step two sorts the audience. Of the 1,600, he guesses about 45 are passionate; a bigger group doesn't mind classical music, a little Vivaldi with a glass of wine; a third group never listens and hears it only "like second-hand smoke at the airport"; and a small group thinks it is tone-deaf. He abolishes that last group on the spot:

Actually, you cannot be tone-deaf. Nobody is tone-deaf.— Benjamin Zander, TED, 2008

The evidence is everyday: you can change gears in a stick-shift car, tell someone from Texas from someone from Rome, and when your mother says "Hello" on the phone you know not only who it is but what mood she's in. Then he announces he won't go on until everyone in the room loves and understands classical music. Look at my face, he says: not the slightest doubt.

It's one of the characteristics of a leader that he not doubt for one moment the capacity of the people he's leading to realize whatever he's dreaming.— Benjamin Zander, TED, 2008

Imagine, he adds, if Martin Luther King had said, "I have a dream. Of course, I'm not sure they'll be up to it."

Step three is the Chopin (from the melody he describes, the Prelude in E minor, Op. 28 No. 4; he never names the number). He plays it once, guesses that your mind drifted off to next year's summer holiday, and then turns the blame around:

But has it ever occurred to you that the reason you feel sleepy in classical music is not because of you, but because of us?— Benjamin Zander, TED, 2008

Then the note-by-note dissection. B, then C: "the job of the C is to make the B sad," and composers know it. The line falls to A, G, F; he stops, and the hall sings the E on its own. "Oh, the TED choir." Every village in Bangladesh and every hamlet in China, he says, knows the E is coming. Chopin doesn't want to reach it yet, because then it would be over, like Hamlet. The critics say Hamlet is a procrastinator or has an Oedipus complex; "No, otherwise the play would be over, stupid." Chopin finally reaches E on the wrong chord, a deceptive cadence, "because it deceives us." He tries again and again until at last he arrives. Someone in the front row goes "Mmm," and Zander says it's the same sound that man makes when he turns off the car after a long day: I'm home.

The point of all this is the line. To join B to E he has to stop thinking about every note along the way. Then comes one last request before he plays it through: think of somebody you adore who is no longer there.

Afterwards come the seventh graders' answer and a street kid from Northern Ireland during the Troubles. The boy had never listened to classical music, but while Zander played he thought of his brother, shot the year before, and "it felt really good to cry for my brother." Classical music is for everybody, Zander decides. His profession says three percent of people like it and the problem would be solved at four. How would you walk and talk if you believed that? How would you walk and talk if you believed "Everybody loves classical music — they just haven't found out about it yet"? Totally different worlds.

The final act moves from music to people. At 45, after twenty years of conducting, he realized the conductor doesn't make a sound:

He depends, for his power, on his ability to make other people powerful.— Benjamin Zander, TED, 2008

His job, he saw, was to awaken possibility in other people. The test is in the eyes, and when they don't shine, there is a question to ask:

who am I being that my players' eyes are not shining?— Benjamin Zander, TED, 2008

We can ask it of our children too. Success, he says, isn't wealth, fame or power; it's how many shining eyes he has around him. He ends with an Auschwitz survivor. At fifteen, on the train, she scolded her eight-year-old brother for losing his shoes, and it was the last thing she ever said to him. She came out with a vow:

I will never say anything that couldn't stand as the last thing I ever say.— Benjamin Zander, TED, 2008

Can we do that? No, he admits, "But it is a possibility to live into." He leaves the stage saying it twice: shining eyes.

Beneath the iceberg

The silent conductor has a fuller version in the book. The fifth practice in The Art of Possibility is called "Leading From Any Chair," and it is more self-deprecating than the talk: conducting, Zander writes, is "one of the last true bastions of totalitarianism in the civilized world." The book dates the insight to when he "had been conducting for nearly twenty years," and says he started asking "What makes a group lively and engaged?" instead of "How good am I?"

On stage there are only the eyes. In the book there is a system. Zander admits that looking wasn't enough. In rehearsal, talk flows only from the podium down; players speak back through the concertmaster, and almost always disguised as a question. So he put a blank sheet of paper on every stand, inviting any observation that might help him: the "white sheets." He braced for criticism and got almost none. Instead the sheets asked him to beat a passage in two rather than four, and carried interpretive ideas that changed performances. The book also asks a question that goes a step beyond "who am I being": how much greatness are we willing to grant people? The same listless player can be seen as bored and resigned, or as a lover of music whose spark has dimmed. The conductor, he writes, "decides who is playing in his orchestra."

The other half of the idea is "giving an A." In a 2010 interview with Boston University's Journal of Education, Zander told the origin story. He complained that his graduate students were too afraid of competitions, grades and reviews to take risks. Rosamund suggested giving every student an A on day one, on condition that each write a letter in September dated the following May, beginning "Dear Mr Zander, I got my A because…", describing who they will have become. When he guest-conducts a new orchestra, he gives every player an A before the first downbeat.

The prison guards line has a source. The book says orchestral players' job satisfaction falls "just below that of prison guards." That matches the study by Harvard psychologist J. Richard Hackman and Jutta Allmendinger of 78 professional orchestras in four countries: musicians' internal motivation was higher than any other group's, yet their satisfaction at work came out below that of flight attendants and prison guards. The fire hadn't gone out; the room the job gave it had shrunk. That is exactly the problem Zander's practices address, though he cites no numbers on stage.

Where the stage numbers come from. The "45 passionate" figure is an on-the-spot guess. The "three percent" he attributes to his profession, without a source. The best data at the time came from the NEA's Survey of Public Participation in the Arts: the share of American adults attending at least one classical concert a year was 13.0% in 1982, 12.5% in 1992, 11.6% in 2002 and 9.3% in 2008. By that measure, the "three percent" he mocks is too low, but the decline he waves away is real. And his proof that nobody is tone-deaf (gear changes, accents, a mother's tone of voice) rests on speech, intonation and timbre, not on telling melodic pitches apart. Using the first to prove the second is exactly where the stage version runs wider than the research.

What happened next

Partly holds · as of 2026-11

Leaders' expectations do change how their people perform, but the stance is hard to teach. "Nobody is tone-deaf" overreaches, though most people who think they are aren't. As a forecast about audiences, the idea that everybody loves classical music and just hasn't found out yet has so far found no support in the American survey data.

The leadership half: evidence existed before the talk, and it was more complicated. Eight years earlier, Dov Eden of the Hebrew University and colleagues had reported seven field experiments in The Leadership Quarterly. They tried to turn the Pygmalion effect (high expectations from a superior raising a subordinate's performance) into training for managers: set high expectations, build a supportive climate, credit subordinates' successes to internal causes. The workshops grew from one day to three. The result: "little evidence that the workshops influenced leaders or followers," with a mean effect of r = .13 across 61 effects. Earlier Pygmalion studies had found medium-to-large effects; the authors put the gap down to efficacy under controlled conditions versus effectiveness in everyday settings. In Zander's terms: when you truly don't doubt people, they do get better. Tell someone not to doubt, and they mostly can't. Giving an A and the white sheets sidestep that problem by turning the expectation into an act rather than an attitude.

2010: can explaining backfire? The music psychologist Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis published "When program notes don't help" in Psychology of Music. As a 2020 review summarizes it, 16 listeners without musical training heard excerpts of Beethoven string quartets after reading a dramatic description, a structural one, or none, and the descriptions reduced their enjoyment. I have not read the original, only that summary. Zander's explanation is live and performed, and nobody has tested whether it dilutes the experience the same way.

2012: what the data say about the silent conductor. D'Ausilio and colleagues published a small study in PLoS ONE: 8 violinists, 2 conductors, 5 pieces, motion capture on bows and batons, and Granger causality (a test of whose movements predict whose) to find the direction of influence. Performances were rated higher when the conductor's influence on the players was stronger and the players' influence on one another weaker, with one piece as an exception. The authors note that the sample is tiny and correlation is not causation. It sits a little awkwardly with Zander: the conductor makes no sound, yet his influence is measurable, and good performances went with players "watching each other" less.

January 2012, off stage. According to Boston public radio station WBUR, NEC dismissed Zander after 45 years. He had hired a registered sex offender, who had served a sentence for a crime involving a minor, to film the conservatory's preparatory students. Zander apologized for his lack of judgment, and that November his newly founded Boston Philharmonic Youth Orchestra gave its first concert. None of this refutes his argument. It is a reminder that checking whether eyes are shining is no substitute for judgment about everything beyond them.

2017: how many people really are tone-deaf. Isabelle Peretz and Dominique Vuvan of the University of Montreal published a large-scale estimate in the European Journal of Human Genetics: more than 20,000 people took three online tests, and 16,625 adults were analyzed. By the strict criterion, pitch-based congenital amusia affects about 1.5%; by a lenient one, 4.2%. Zander's "nobody" is wrong, then: about one person in 70. But about a fifth of the people who tested normal also said they had no sense of music, and they far outnumber the true amusics. His line is right about them.

2017 to 2022: the audience. In the NEA's data, 8.6% of American adults attended a classical concert in 2017, no significant change from 2012. In 2022 the figure was 4.6%, which the NEA says probably reflects the lingering effect of the pandemic. In interviews Zander points to the other side: Venezuela's El Sistema and China's millions of child pianists. "Everybody loves classical music" was never a statistical claim; he himself says its use lies in "How would you walk? How would you talk?" As a stance, it got 1,600 people to sing the E. As a forecast, at least in America, it hasn't come true.

Crossovers

  • Cognitive science · David Huron's theory of expectation: In Sweet Anticipation (2006), Huron argues that much of music's pleasure comes from the brain predicting the next note, and from those predictions being confirmed or fooled. A deceptive cadence is a carefully engineered prediction error, and Zander's "because it deceives us" is almost the theory in plain speech. Where it differs: for Huron these expectations are learned statistically within a musical culture, so "every village in Bangladesh knows the E is coming" is precisely the line to question.
  • Buddhism · maraṇasati, mindfulness of death: Speaking every sentence as if it were your last has the same structure as the Buddhist practice of recollecting that death may come at any moment, so that this moment's conduct is right. Where it differs: maraṇasati aims at seeing impermanence and letting go, while the survivor's vow grew out of one irreparable regret and points toward responsibility to others.
  • Psychology · self-determination theory: Deci and Ryan hold that intrinsic motivation rests on three needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. The white sheets supply autonomy; giving an A supplies competence. Where it differs: Hackman's data say orchestra musicians' motivation was already high and what they lacked was room in the job, so Zander is repairing the setting, not the motivation itself.
  • Complexity science · coordination without a leader: A flock turns as one without a lead bird, each bird watching only its neighbors. The book's Kolisch Quartet, playing everything from memory, and the Cuban and American stand partners are this kind of "leading from any chair." Where it differs: in D'Ausilio's data, better performances went with less influence among players and more from the conductor, so an orchestra with a score may need a center more than a flock does.

Ten takeaways

  1. In Zander's account, a pianist's progress from age seven to eleven is the impulses thinning from one per note to one per phrase, which he calls "one-buttock playing."
  2. Joining the first note of a phrase to its destination means stopping thinking about every note along the way and following the long line.
  3. Chopin's E minor prelude keeps postponing its return to E with deceptive cadences, the way Shakespeare postpones Hamlet's revenge until Act Five.
  4. Zander shifts the blame for dozing at concerts from the listeners to the performers and explainers.
  5. A conductor makes no sound; his power depends on his ability to make the players powerful.
  6. The test of leadership is whether the other people's eyes are shining, and if not, the question "who am I being?"
  7. The Art of Possibility turns the idea into acts: white sheets on every music stand and an A for every student on day one.
  8. A 2017 test of more than 16,000 adults found about 1.5% with congenital amusia, while many self-described tone-deaf people tested normal.
  9. Dov Eden's seven field experiments found that high leader expectations are hard to instill through workshops, with a mean effect of only r = .13.
  10. In NEA surveys, American adults attending a classical concert in a year fell from 13% in 1982 to 9.3% in 2008, and stood at 4.6% in 2022.
