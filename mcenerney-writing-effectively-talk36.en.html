Clear and Useless Is Useless: The Most-Watched Eighty Minutes of Chicago’s Writing Program

Larry McEnerney · The Craft of Writing Effectively

Larry McEnerney’s office has a writing corner with two chairs: his, and one for the writer who has come for help. Next to the writer’s chair sits a box of Kleenex.

He insists this is not a joke. The people in that chair are often University of Chicago faculty and doctoral students whose grant proposals were turned down, whose drafts came back, whose articles will not get published, with careers hanging on the outcome. Sometimes the problem is that the writing is not clear, sometimes that it is not organized, sometimes that it is not persuasive. But overwhelmingly, it is that the writing is not valuable.

If it’s clear and useless, it’s useless.— Larry McEnerney, The Craft of Writing Effectively, 2014

McEnerney directed the University of Chicago Writing Program for nearly thirty years. After this eighty-minute lecture from 2014 went up on YouTube, it spent a decade circulating among researchers. Its argument is a single one, pushed all the way to the end.

You have written perhaps a hundred academic texts in your life. Did any reader ever stop halfway? Never, because those readers were teachers, and teachers read your work because they are paid to care about you, to see whether you learned. Every writing rule you know was learned in that system of readers paid to care. After school the system is gone. Editors, reviewers and colleagues read you for one reason only: the work is valuable to them. If it isn’t, they put it down.

So he wants two words deleted from your head: “new” and “original.” New knowledge is cheap. Count the people in this room and the world has a fact nobody knew, but nobody will read that paper. A friend of his from graduate school found, in a library in Norwich, England, thirty years of travel journals by a nineteenth-century woman. She read them all, wrote them up and handed the work to her committee, which sent it back: it is new, it is original, “but it is not knowledge.” What counts as knowledge is decided by a specific group of readers.

What are those readers looking for? He had the room circle words in a biology grant proposal: nonetheless, widely, accepted, however, although, inconsistent, reported, anomaly. One kind tells readers that a community cares about this; the other creates instability, a place where what you thought was settled does not fit. A good introduction does not start from background, definitions or a sweeping generalization. It starts with a problem the readers care about, then offers a solution. He even bet that in any journal, every article from the last twenty years says somebody is wrong, only politely: first you tell them how smart they are, then you mention “this little thing.” And announcing that you will fill a gap is more dangerous still, he argued, because knowledge is infinite: fill one gap, and infinitely many remain.

How much of this has held up? Partly. The backbone comes from his mentor Joseph Williams’s theory of problems: a destabilizing condition plus a cost the readers must bear. Genre analysis of research introductions (John Swales’s CARS model) found the same core move in real articles: you carve out a niche for your study. But two of his specific claims go further than the evidence. When Swales revised his model in 2004, “indicating a gap” was precisely the default way to establish a niche; and a 2014 study of 50 introductions in two writing-studies journals found that in one of them, 60% skipped the niche move entirely. As for “new,” words like “novel” rose by up to 15,000% in relative frequency in medical abstracts over forty years. The reader communities are buying novelty, even if the researchers who counted suspect it is packaging.

It has also been bent in retelling. One widely shared summary turns “everything having to do with writing is dominated by the issue of value” into his signature line, yet neither transcript contains it. His own hedges, “I’m not saying gap can never work” and “there’s a ton of ethical issues involved in that,” have mostly vanished from the paraphrases.

The Moment

Early in the lecture, McEnerney asks the room how many academic texts each of them has written. He supplies the numbers himself: three hundred? Five hundred? A thousand? Two hundred? A hundred? A hundred, then, since one listener seems happier with that.

“Did the readers stop reading them?”

Never. Why not? Someone answers: they were grading it. Let’s be a little cruder, he says: they were paid.

Teachers read text because they are paid to care about the students. You’ve learned to write in a system where you’re writing to readers who are paid to care about you. That will stop.— Larry McEnerney, The Craft of Writing Effectively, 2014

Academics get all freaked out when he talks about money, he says, and then finishes the thought. Every rule you have learned about writing was generated inside that system, and the system is over. After school nobody is paid to care about you. Colleagues, editors and reviewers read your work only because they think it is valuable to them.

The audience was mostly social-science doctoral students and researchers (he remarks at one point, “fortunately I’m talking to social scientists”), people who had written all their lives and mostly written well. That one question turned the most solid thing they owned, a hundred experiences of being read to the end, into a trap.

The Speaker, and the Year

McEnerney came to the University of Chicago in 1978 for a PhD in English, meaning to study literature. He later said he discovered “I was bad at scholarship because I didn’t like literature, which is a bad combination.” There he met Joseph Williams, a professor of English and linguistics, who with Gregory Colomb was building a graduate writing course that became known as the Little Red Schoolhouse. McEnerney left in 1981 for the family business, was later called back by Williams to help develop course materials, joined the writing program formally in 1987, and became its director in 1992. He spent eleven years as a resident dean in a dormitory and taught his last class in the spring of 2020.

The lecture opens by explaining what makes Chicago’s program unusual: it works “top-down.” Other schools build writing programs around freshmen; Chicago has no freshman composition course at all. The program was founded in the late 1970s and early 1980s to help not the students but the faculty. As its founder used to put it: our freshmen write pretty well, our third- and fourth-years less well, our graduate students struggle, and the people with the real writing problems are the faculty.

This Leadership Lab lecture was recorded in 2014 (Wikipedia gives 2014; a 2020 university news story says 2015). Recordings of his lectures caught on on YouTube in 2014 and 2015, this one most of all; Wikipedia puts its views at about 8.8 million. By then he had been teaching for three decades. His handout ran to thirty pages, and in eighty minutes he got as far as page 25.

Glossary

  • Little Red Schoolhouse: a ten-week course in academic and professional writing at the University of Chicago, founded around 1978 by Joseph Williams and others. The lecture’s method comes from this course.
  • Community of readers: McEnerney’s key term. Not readers in general but a specific group (a field, a journal, a committee) with its own problems, its own doubts and its own code words.
  • Instability: the Chicago program’s name for a problem. Something in what readers took to be a settled understanding does not fit: a contradiction, an anomaly, an inconsistency.
  • Literature review: the part of a paper that surveys earlier research. The student version lists it by year; the professional version uses it to deepen the problem.

The Talk, Unpacked

McEnerney begins by saying what this is not: not a remedial course, and not a course in rules. Rules suit someone who writes a short memo every day or two, each of relatively low value. That is not the level of value his audience’s work has to generate. Hence his first piece of advice: stop thinking about rules and start thinking about readers.

The people in the room are “expert writers”: not necessarily good at writing, but writing about subjects where they work at the frontier. That creates a problem only experts have. His high-school teacher told him to finish thinking before writing a word, and to prove it, to hand in his outline with every paper. “This was not a problem for me. First I wrote the paper, then I wrote the outline.” Nobody works the other way, he says. You use writing to help yourself think. He draws two axes. On the horizontal axis you generate text while you think; on the vertical axis readers read it. Experts use language in one set of patterns to think and read with a different pattern, so text written for thinking interferes with reading, even when the readers are experts too. First readers slow down and reread, then they misunderstand, then they get aggravated, and then they stop. Why didn’t you stop when it happened to you? Because you needed to read it. “What do you suppose happens if they don’t need to read it? They don’t.”

Then come the hundred texts and the readers who were paid. From there he ranks the demands on writing: clear, organized and persuasive all matter, but from now on what matters more than anything is that it is valuable. The box of Kleenex is there for writing that isn’t.

If it’s clear and useless, it’s useless.— Larry McEnerney, The Craft of Writing Effectively, 2014

That frightens people, who worry that their ideas might simply lack value. He calls the worry dopey. Value does not lie in the text; it lies in readers, and the only question is whether this particular community of readers values it. When a doctoral student tells him they are under pressure to get an article published, he asks which journal it is going to. The student asks, “What does that matter?” It matters completely: the same text can be terrifically useful to one group of readers and useless to another.

Then the central exercise. The handout has two imagined biology grant proposals, 1A and 1B, with the same first sentence, much the same content, and only a few words of difference. 1A says “Observation of coexistence in vertebrate taxa has been reported.” 1B says “Nonetheless, observation of coexistence in vertebrate taxa has been widely reported.” 1B adds “the accepted model,” “However, although the FNV model correctly predicts…, its predictions are inconsistent with reported performances in mixtures,” and ends: “Proposed study will examine whether the anomaly may be explained by…” Everyone picks 1B, because it seems important. He presses: tell the writer of 1A that her work seems unimportant and she will say you didn’t understand it, and then she will explain. Don’t. The explaining you learned is demonstrating to a teacher that you understand. “No one cares about the inside of your head. At least, not unless you pay us.”

It’s not conveying your ideas to your readers. It’s changing their ideas. Nobody cares what ideas you have.— Larry McEnerney, The Craft of Writing Effectively, 2014

He turns the knife on himself. He used to ask students “Why do you think that?” until he realized it was a teacherly question, still asking what was in the student’s head. Switching to “Why should I think that?” is not enough either, because of a rule of Western academia: nothing will be accepted as knowledge or understanding until it has been challenged by someone competent to challenge it. Teachers read to care about you; someone asking directions reads for information; professional readers have the function of challenging what you say. So explanation comes last: first readers must see value, then persuasion begins. And don’t begin with a definition. Teachers love definitions because they show you know the definition.

Someone says 1B seems “new.” Worse word, he says. Find the synapse in your brain that stores “new,” or worse “original,” and kill it. You are not here to do original work; you are here to do valuable work. New knowledge takes thirty seconds: count the people in this room. The friend’s Norwich journals were new too, and the committee still sent them back: “It’s new and it’s original. But it is not knowledge.” He draws two pictures. One is the positivist brick wall, knowledge piling up year by year, every unknown fact a new brick; that model, he says, is dead. The other is a group of people moving through time in conversation, and they get to say what counts as knowledge. He concedes that historically those people have looked a lot like him (his niece calls him “too male, too pale, too stale”), that they are changing too slowly, and that this is nonetheless how it works. The conversation also leaves things behind as it moves.

He has everyone circle the words in 1B that make it valuable. The room calls them out: nonetheless, widely, accepted, however, although, inconsistent, reported, anomaly. Then his first piece of advice, one to “make your writing better starting this afternoon”: spend fifteen minutes a week for the rest of the year with printed articles from your field, circling every word that creates value for readers, and build a word list. A week before a deadline, check your own first two paragraphs; if you cannot underline ten such words, go to the list. It isn’t always magic, he says, but sometimes it is. Every community has its own codes. A PhD takes five or six years because half the time goes to learning more stuff and the other half to learning your readers, since persuasion depends on what they doubt.

The circled words fall into two groups. Widely, accepted and reported signal that a community cares. You were taught to think of however and although as “flow words” or “transition words” that keep prose smooth, but their real job lies elsewhere. Tell readers that you have read their work, you know what they think, and you have something to say; or tell them you have read their work, you know what they think, but they are wrong. Which one will they pay attention to? Someone objects that those people are the editors. He bets on the spot: name a journal, and every article in it for the last twenty years says somebody is wrong. You just have to know the code: “wow, are you smart,” you have advanced this community in fabulous ways, but there is this little thing you’ve got here. And then you had better have an argument.

Do not explain. Argue.— Larry McEnerney, The Craft of Writing Effectively, 2014

Saying “I have something I want to add” is risky too: the risk is “we don’t care.” “I’d like to put my voice into the conversation” is worse. Then he mocks his own program:

A lot of people think we’re fascists. I don’t dispute it. Here’s what we teach people to do. We say, identify the people with power in your community and give them what they want.— Larry McEnerney, The Craft of Writing Effectively, 2014

He says he is also teaching people to challenge the community, only inside its terms. When someone worried about their career is sitting in his office, it cannot be about their individual voice. He grants there is “a ton of ethical issues involved in that” and says he only wants to put them on the table.

A handout passage written for high-school students on “why people write essays” gets taken apart. It defines an essay as a structured, creative written composition on a subject from a personal point of view, and says writing lets the writer analyze ideas and “preserve them indefinitely.” Not a word about readers or value. He calls himself Wittgensteinian here: what something means is what it does, and what writing does is help a particular set of readers understand better something they want to understand well. As for preserving indefinitely: a former student of his, now chair of a philosophy department, finished a 400-page book and kept revising because someone might read it in 500 years. McEnerney sent him two words: “You’re done.” The function of your writing is to move the conversation forward, and it cannot do that from a desk drawer. A passage from Lyotard gets the plain-language treatment too: once, praising a professor meant praising how much she had in her head; now it means praising what she has done in the space between heads.

Then he comes down “out of the stratosphere.” But, however, inconsistent and anomaly are different parts of speech doing one job, creating tension, which the program calls instability. Many people leave school with an explanatory opening: background, definitions, generalization, then thesis, then back out to generalization at the end, the “martini glass model.” It is the brick-wall model again, using a language of continuity to tie the thesis to what is already known. Yet at the opening readers are searching precisely for inconsistency; give them continuity and they may stop two paragraphs in. So open with a problem, and with the readers’ problem, not “Ever since I was young, I’ve been fascinated by…” A thesis counts as a solution only once readers have seen the problem, and nine times out of ten, ninety-nine times out of a hundred, he says, the problem has to be on the page. Beyond instability, problems need a language of costs and benefits: show readers that the inconsistency costs them something, or that solving it brings them a benefit. Look at the journal you are aiming for, see which language it favors, and if there is a pattern, follow it.

Midway, someone asks about literature reviews. The student version is a list by year: in 1998 he said this, in 2000 he said this. The professional version uses the review to enrich the problem: what was said in 2001 sits in tension with 2004, 2005 complicates both, and done well, the review names a cost. Not only were they wrong, but those of us building on their ideas are wrong too.

He ends with real articles. The historian William Sewell opens by saying that ever since Herodotus, historians have written about events: battles, alliances, scandals. A high-school teacher would call that background, but the next word is “but”: he is building a problem. When a professor asks a student for more background, he says, what the professor means is “I can’t figure out why this matters.” The background they need is a problem. Sewell’s next sentence says the event itself has rarely been scrutinized as a theoretical category. Is that a gap? Here McEnerney turns to the handout’s pages on gap versus error. Young academics afraid to tell important people they are wrong reach for the language of gap, but gap assumes knowledge is bounded, like a crossword puzzle with a fixed number of squares.

If knowledge is infinite, and you filled in a gap, how many gaps are left? You have done nothing.— Larry McEnerney, The Craft of Writing Effectively, 2014

Sewell’s sentence is not a gap: for a community using a basic category it does not understand, that is a gigantic problem. So his next two sentences name two communities, traditional narrative historians and historical sociologists, and make clear who has the problem.

The finale is John Tutino’s article on Mexican independence. The first paragraph looks like background; the second opens by saying the interpretation just given challenges an entrenched vision of Mexican history. McEnerney points to the footnote. The essay was first presented at a seminar, where, he is sure, Tutino showed the tables that come later: rents on one estate from 1820 to 1825, in pesos. People at the seminar would have thought: why would I care? Later the journal’s readers asked him to make the larger significance explicit, and a final reader suggested that his interpretations appeared controversial. Tutino had probably worked on the piece for three years before learning, at the very end, that his work implied the community was wrong about the Hidalgo revolt. That became the article’s first sentence. You have to think on the horizontal axis, McEnerney concludes, but the more you can alter the text for readers on the vertical one, the more likely you are to succeed. He apologizes for running long and for being theatrical. The Kleenex is real, he says, and he is dramatic in the hope that you will not end up in that chair.

Below the Waterline

In eighty minutes McEnerney cites no experiment and no writing research. His evidence is of three kinds: decades of experience in the writing clinic (the Kleenex, articles that got published after an hour’s revision); real articles he selected for the handout; and a bet placed on stage and never settled there, that every article in any journal for twenty years says somebody is wrong. So the hidden mass of this lecture is not in a laboratory. It is in forty years of theory built up by the Chicago writing program.

The backbone comes from Joseph Williams. In 2004 he published, open access through the WAC Clearinghouse, a book titled Problems into PROBLEMS: A Rhetoric of Motivation, which reads almost like the paper version of this lecture. A problem, Williams writes, has two components: a “de-stabilizing” condition, which can be any state of affairs, from a flat tire to winning the lottery, and consequences the person claiming the problem does not want, which he calls its “Costs.” To become a PROBLEM that can be written for readers, it needs a third component: a community of readers who perceive the Cost as undesirable to themselves, people who are not just interested in a topic but have an interest in the problem being solved. He offers a test: insert “So what?” between the condition and the cost, and if the reader can answer “Oh, I see,” you have found them.

Both stories in the lecture have prototypes in Williams. Counting the people in the room corresponds to his “How many stars are in the sky?” I don’t know, but I don’t care, so for me it is no problem. For an astronomer, not knowing the number of stars means not knowing how much matter is in the universe, and so not knowing whether it will expand forever or collapse. That is a problem. The Norwich journals correspond to his “number of trees on the island of Zanzibar”: if I were obsessed enough to lose sleep over it, I would have a problem, but no one else would pay the cost, so it would have no rhetorical dimension. McEnerney’s “instability” and his “language of costs and benefits” are essentially Williams’s Condition and Cost.

One difference is worth noticing. When Williams lists the words that define the condition of a conceptual problem, he names ignorance, misunderstanding, error, paradox, discrepancy, puzzle, conflict and dispute, “words that imply some gap in knowledge or flaw in understanding.” For Williams, a gap is a legitimate condition, provided a cost to readers follows. When McEnerney calls gap “very dangerous” and usually ineffective, he is sharpening his mentor’s theory in one direction, though he leaves himself an exit: “I’m not saying gap can never work.”

The same theory exists in a much gentler version. Williams and McEnerney co-wrote Writing in College, a guide for first- and second-year students at Chicago, which describes argument as “an amiable and lively conversation” with someone who respects you and is interested in what you say but will not agree just because you say it. A good claim says something readers did not already know and is “at least mildly contestable.” The stage line about giving powerful people what they want and the guide’s amiable conversation share one model: your claim must be open to reasonable challenge, and you must anticipate the reader’s “But what about…?” The only difference is that the version for freshmen assumes a teacher willing to hear you out, and the version for researchers takes that assumption away.

The Lyotard passage comes from The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge, written in 1979 at the request of Quebec’s universities council, a diagnosis of the state of knowledge. McEnerney uses it as a reality to be accepted: your relationship to your own knowledge is like a farmer’s to wheat. That is a large step, and he covers it with a single “I bet for many of you that doesn’t feel very good.”

What Happened Next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-10

Locating value in readers and opening with the readers’ problem agree with decades of findings in genre research. But three claims, that every article says somebody is wrong, that gap usually fails, and that the word “new” should be killed, have been narrowed by later corpus and word-frequency studies. The method as a whole has never been tested experimentally.

McEnerney’s method has no controlled trial behind it: no one has randomly assigned writers to learn value-word circling or not and compared acceptance rates. We also found no scholarly article criticizing this lecture specifically. What can test it is an independent discipline that counts how real papers are written: genre analysis.

Its founder is the linguist John Swales, who began analyzing research-article introductions in 1981 and in 1990 proposed the CARS model (Create a Research Space). Introductions typically make three moves: establish a territory (the topic matters, here is earlier work), establish a niche, and occupy the niche by presenting the study. This much agrees with McEnerney: the core move of an introduction is not laying out background but creating a niche readers can see. In 1990 Swales listed four ways to establish a niche, and the first was counter-claiming, followed by indicating a gap, question-raising, and continuing a tradition.

The disagreement comes later. In Research Genres (2004) Swales revised the model, and establishing a niche came down to two main options: indicating a gap, or adding to what is known, plus an optional step of presenting positive justification. Counter-claiming no longer had a step of its own. McEnerney says gap usually fails and adding something invites “we don’t care”; the discipline that counted large numbers of introductions made gap and addition the default.

In 2014 Demet Yayli and Suresh Canagarajah applied Swales’s 2004 model to 50 empirical-article introductions from two writing-studies journals, Written Communication and the Journal of Second Language Writing, published 2005 to 2007. Sixty percent of the Written Communication introductions and 36% of the JSLW introductions had no niche-establishing move at all. The authors went straight from surveying the field to announcing their study, bridging with a kind of sentence the researchers named territorial justification. The sample is small and covers one young, interdisciplinary field, but it lands directly on McEnerney’s bet that any journal, for twenty years, says in every article that somebody is wrong. In the journals of the people who study writing, at least, the bet would lose.

Killing the word “new” meets a different set of numbers. In 2015 Christiaan Vinkers and colleagues counted words in every PubMed abstract from 1974 to 2014 in the BMJ. The frequency of 25 positive words rose from 2.0% in 1974–80 to 17.5% in 2014, with robust, novel, innovative and unprecedented rising by up to 15,000% in relative frequency, while neutral words, random words and positive words in published books showed no similar rise. If the code for value is set by reader communities, then in biomedicine, at least, “new” has become an ever more important code. Yet the data cut both ways. The authors concluded that scientists “look on the bright side of research results. But whether this perception fits reality should be questioned,” which sides with McEnerney’s suspicion that new is not the same as valuable, only that readers increasingly respond to it.

The remaining question is what the lecture became in transmission. It is most often met not as the video but as a summary. In 2022 Justin Mikolay published a “distilled” version on Substack, noting that he had reduced the 80 minutes “to its essence (and rewrote some sentences for clarity).” Its line “Everything having to do with writing is dominated by the issue of value” appears in neither transcript. The most widely used third-party transcript dates the lecture to May 9, 2024. More important is what gets cut. On stage he said “I’m not saying gap can never work,” “I’m not suggesting this is always magic,” and “there’s a ton of ethical issues involved in that.” None of that survives in slogans like “nobody cares about your ideas.” The original is a judgment for frontier researchers, attentive to differences between communities; the slogan is a rule meant to apply everywhere. And rules are exactly what the whole lecture argued against.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy of science · Kuhn’s anomaly: In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962), Kuhn argued that major scientific change begins with anomalies, results that defy the paradigm’s expectations and pile up into crisis. “Anomaly” is one of the very words McEnerney circles, and a group of people deciding what counts as knowledge resembles Kuhn’s scientific community. The difference: Kuhn held that most everyday science is puzzle-solving inside a paradigm, much closer to the gap-filling McEnerney disparages. For Kuhn, filling gaps is the norm and overturning is the exception.
  • Neuroscience · Prediction error: Predictive processing holds that the brain gives priority to signals that violate its predictions and heavily damps what it expected. Readers scanning an introduction for instability look like the same mechanism at the level of reading. The difference: prediction error is automatic, while McEnerney’s instability can be recognized only through a community’s code.
  • Economics · Menger’s subjective value: Carl Menger argued in 1871 that value is not a property of a good but depends on how important it is to someone’s needs. “Value lies in readers, right? Not in the thing” is nearly the same sentence. The difference: Menger’s valuers are independent individuals, while McEnerney’s readers are a community with gatekeepers and a shared code.
  • Psychology · The curse of knowledge: In 1990 Elizabeth Newton had one group tap out the rhythms of well-known songs and another guess the titles. Tappers predicted half would be recognized; listeners got 3 of 120. Experts cannot simulate the uninformed, which is the mismatch of McEnerney’s horizontal and vertical axes. The difference: he insists the interference happens even when readers are experts, so the problem is not how much readers know but writing aimed at the wrong function.

Ten takeaways

  1. Larry McEnerney argues that writing rules suit high-volume, low-value writing, and that frontier researchers should stop thinking about rules and start thinking about readers.
  2. Experts use writing to think, but the language patterns of thinking interfere with reading: readers slow down, misunderstand, get aggravated, and finally stop.
  3. School readers are teachers paid to care about students, so student writing is always read to the end; after school, readers read only what is valuable to them.
  4. Clarity, organization and persuasion all matter, but without value none of them counts; value lies not in the text but in a particular community of readers.
  5. Professional writing changes a reader community’s ideas rather than conveying the writer’s own; establish value first, then persuade, and explain last.
  6. New and original do not mean knowledge: counting the people in a room or transcribing an unknown traveler’s journals is new, but no reader cares.
  7. A good introduction opens with an instability readers care about, marked by words like however, inconsistent and anomaly, then offers a solution instead of starting from background and definitions.
  8. Spending fifteen minutes a week circling value-creating words in published articles builds a word list for checking the first two paragraphs of a draft.
  9. The method descends from Joseph Williams’s theory of problems: a destabilizing condition, a cost borne by readers, and a community that cares about that cost.
  10. Swales’s genre research treats indicating a gap as the default way to establish a niche, and many writing-studies introductions establish no niche at all.
