TEDxPeachtree · 2011

The Thrown-Back Cucumber: Is Morality Older Than We Are?

Frans de Waal · Moral behavior in animals

On the screen, two capuchin monkeys sit in cages side by side. The rule is simple: hand the experimenter a small rock, get some food. The monkey on the left gets cucumber, and eats the first piece happily. Then she watches her neighbour hand over the same kind of rock and get a grape. Her turn: cucumber again. The audience laughs. Next round she taps her rock against the wall, as if checking it's genuine, and hands it over. Cucumber. The speaker, the primatologist Frans de Waal, adds a line:

So this is basically the Wall Street protest that you see here.— Frans de Waal, TEDxPeachtree, 2011

It was November 2011, seven weeks into Occupy Wall Street. Applause. The clip went on to become one of the most famous videos in animal behaviour research.

De Waal, Dutch-born and based at Emory University in Atlanta, spent these sixteen minutes taking aim at a very old picture: "man is a wolf to man", human nature is selfish and aggressive at bottom, and morality is a coat of paint applied later by civilisation. That, he said, is unfair even to wolves, which are highly cooperative animals; it's why so many people have a dog at home.

His argument is a checklist. Morality rests on two pillars: reciprocity (with its sense of fairness) and empathy. Then he shows both pillars in other animals. A chimp that has already eaten still helps its partner pull in a heavy box. An elephant learns to stand on a rope and let its partner do the work. A chimp that has lost a fight screams, and a youngster comes over and puts an arm around him. Given two colours of token, one that feeds only themselves and one that feeds them and their neighbour, chimps pick the second more often. Then, last, cucumbers and grapes.

What the stage left out: the fairness study first appeared in Nature in 2003, with five female capuchins. The monkeys in the video were new ones, filmed later because, in de Waal's words, they thought new monkeys might react more strongly. What the audience remembers is the image, not the data.

What happened next? Partly holds up. The part that drew the most fire was exactly the part that got the biggest laugh. In 2004 Nature ran critiques: a monkey that throws away its cucumber while its neighbour still eats the grape makes the split more unequal, not less; this looks less like hating unfairness and more like snubbing the worse food when better food is in sight. In 2017 a Leipzig team had a machine hand out the food instead of a person, and chimps stopped refusing; what upset them was "this human could have given me better", not "why does my neighbour get more". In 2024 a meta-analysis pooling raw data from 23 studies and more than sixty thousand trials found no evidence of inequity aversion in this kind of task, only evidence of simpler disappointment. De Waal had died that March. Animals cooperate, console and get disappointed. But "monkeys understand fairness", the version that brought the house down, did not survive in the very experiment that made it famous.

The moment

With two minutes left, de Waal says: "I still have two minutes left -- let me tell you a funny story about this."

Once the cucumber study got famous, letters came in, mostly from anthropologists, economists and philosophers, who "didn't like this at all", because they had decided that fairness is a very complex matter and animals can't have it. Then the best one:

And so one philosopher even wrote us that it was impossible that monkeys had a sense of fairness because fairness was invented during the French Revolution.— Frans de Waal, 2011

The room erupts. He mentions another critic, who said he'd only believe it had to do with fairness if the monkey getting grapes refused them too. As it happens, his collaborator Sarah Brosnan had found a few chimpanzee pairs where the one getting the grape would refuse it until the other also got one. His closer:

And I think philosophers need to rethink their philosophy for a while.— Frans de Waal, 2011

That is the posture of the whole talk: a man who has watched apes for more than thirty years, smiling at the armchair and saying "come and look first". Half of that posture was right. The other half took more than a decade to settle.

The speaker, and the year

De Waal was born in 1948 in 's-Hertogenbosch in the Netherlands, the town the painter Hieronymus Bosch took his name from. In 1975 he began watching the chimpanzee colony at Arnhem Zoo for his PhD and found that they make up after fights; he called it "reconciliation". His first book, Chimpanzee Politics (1982), was about power struggles among the apes. The launch photo he shows has chimps gathered around it: "I'm not sure how well the chimpanzees read it, but they surely seemed interested in the book."

From 1991 he worked at Emory University and the Yerkes primate centre in Atlanta. His 2006 book Primates and Philosophers attacked what he called "veneer theory": morality as a thin coat over a nasty nature. In 2011 his team had just published two studies in PNAS, one on elephant cooperation and one on chimp token choice. So this TEDx was a home game in Atlanta: fresh data, a general audience, and an idea he had been making the case for over three decades.

Glossary

  • Capuchin monkey: a small, group-living monkey from Central and South America, a regular in animal cognition labs.
  • Inequity aversion: an economists' term for a human preference: giving up some of your own payoff to make a split more even. Objecting when you get less than others is "disadvantageous" inequity aversion.
  • Token exchange: first teach an animal to trade a small object (a rock, a plastic chip) for food, then see whether it keeps trading under different splits.

The talk, all the way through

He opens with a painting. Bosch's The Garden of Earthly Delights has been read as humanity before the Fall, or humanity with no Fall at all. That sets up his question: if we hadn't tasted the fruit of knowledge, if there were no religion, what kind of morality would we have?

Then his own turn. As a young researcher he studied power and aggression; the whole field was about competition. Then he photographed two male chimps after a fight, up in a tree, one holding out a hand to the other. A second later they met in the fork of the tree and kissed and embraced. If all that matters is winning or losing, why reconcile? Bonobos make up with sex, on the same principle: a valuable relationship has been damaged by conflict, so you repair it. That brings him to the picture he wants to overturn, "man is a wolf to man":

I think it's a very unfair image for the wolf. The wolf is, after all, a very cooperative animal.— Frans de Waal, 2011

Then the frame: two pillars of morality, reciprocity (with justice and fairness) and empathy (with compassion). Human morality is more than this, but remove the two and there wouldn't be much left.

On reciprocity, first an old Yerkes film: two young chimps pulling in a box too heavy for one, in sync. Then one has already been fed and isn't interested in the food, yet it still helps pull, and at the end the other "takes basically everything". He reads two things into it: the hungry chimp understands it needs a partner, and the fed one is willing to work, probably because favours get returned later. Then elephants in Thailand. A single rope runs around an apparatus; two elephants must pull both ends at once, or the rope slides out. Release one elephant first and see whether it waits. One came up with a solution nobody taught it:

Now this elephant does something illegal that we did not teach it.— Frans de Waal, 2011

It put its big foot on the rope and stood there until the other arrived and did all the work. Freeloading, but proof that it understood how the cooperation worked.

On empathy, he describes two channels. The body channel: talk to a sad person and you take on their posture and expression, and soon you feel sad too. Many mammals have it, which is why people keep mammals at home and not turtles or snakes. The cognitive channel, taking someone else's perspective, is rarer; he thinks elephants and apes can do it. The oldest part of the body channel is synchrony, which in humans can be studied through contagious yawning: highly empathic people catch yawns more, and, he says, "People who have problems with empathy, such as autistic children, they don't have yawn contagion." Chimps watching an animated yawning head yawn too. A step up is consolation: a male chimp screaming after losing a fight, and a juvenile who comes over and puts an arm around him.

Then altruism. For decades, he says, it was assumed only humans care about others' welfare. Chimps got a bucket of tokens in two colours: one fed only the chooser, one fed both. The chooser is rewarded either way, so it should choose at random, but they prefer the prosocial token. They choose it more when the partner draws attention to itself, and less when the partner spits water and intimidates them. The laugh line: "It's as if they're saying, 'If you're not behaving, I'm not going to be pro-social today.'"

Then the finale. Two capuchins, both trading rocks for cucumber, happy to do it 25 times in a row; cucumber, he says, is "only really water in my opinion". Then give the partner grapes:

the food preferences of my capuchin monkeys correspond exactly with the prices in the supermarket— Frans de Waal, 2011

Then the video, the Wall Street line, the French Revolution letter. He gathers the list: empathy and consolation, prosocial tendencies, reciprocity, a sense of fairness. Morality is more than this, but impossible without it.

So let me summarize. I believe there's an evolved morality.— Frans de Waal, 2011

The goal, he says, is to see whether a morality can be built "from the bottom up", without necessarily involving God and religion. The argument is shaped like an ingredients list, one video per ingredient.

Below the waterline

The monkey video is not the data. The fairness study was published in Nature in September 2003 as "Monkeys reject unequal pay". According to a 2024 meta-analysis that tabulated every study's sample, it involved five capuchins, all female. The abstract has a result the talk skipped: refusals were even stronger when the partner got the grape without doing any work. The clip on stage, by de Waal's own account, was filmed "recently" with new monkeys who had never done the task, "thinking that maybe they would have a stronger reaction, and that turned out to be right." The image that went round the world was the strongest reaction on offer.

"Inequity aversion" is an economists' term, and it changed shape in transit. In 2004 the anthropologist Joseph Henrich argued in Nature that the results contradict the predictions of the very inequity-aversion model the authors cited, and that humans in similar situations don't behave like the capuchins. The logic is simple: a monkey that throws away its cucumber while its neighbour still eats the grape turns "I got less" into "I got nothing". The gap gets bigger. The psychologist Clive Wynne, in the same issue, was blunter: this shows only that monkeys reject a lesser reward when better rewards are available. Brosnan and de Waal's reply gave half a step of ground: their aim "was not to demonstrate that capuchin monkeys make a human response to inequality, but rather to elucidate evolutionary precursors". On stage, "we're getting very close to the human sense of fairness" went further than that reply did.

The chimp token effect was small. The 2011 PNAS paper: seven female chimps, each tested with three partners, 30 choices per pairing. Individual prosocial rates ranged from 52.9% to 66.7%. The no-partner control was run afterwards, with a different token set, and one of the seven had died before it. The talk says that "for decades it had been assumed" only humans care about others. It doesn't say the paper was a rebuttal to a 2005 Nature study with the opposite result, "Chimpanzees are indifferent to the welfare of unrelated group members", one of whose authors was Brosnan. De Waal's team argued the earlier setup, with apes about three metres apart, had unintentionally handicapped them.

One small number. He says the Yerkes cooperation experiments started "about a hundred years ago". The 2011 elephant paper itself describes "a classical 1930s cooperation paradigm", roughly 75 years before the talk.

The yawning and autism line was later revised. In 2011 de Waal and a student reported that 23 chimps caught yawns from familiar group members more than from strangers, and took this as support for contagious yawning as a measure of empathy. But in 2013 Usui, Senju and colleagues used an eye tracker to make sure children were looking at the face before the yawn played, and children with autism caught yawns just like other children. The earlier "no contagion" may have reflected how little they spontaneously look at faces. A 2017 systematic review by Massen and Gallup called the evidence linking contagious yawning to empathy "inconsistent and inconclusive".

Where it sits in his thinking. His 2008 Annual Review of Psychology paper gives the fuller version: empathy is probably as old as mammals and birds. Seeing another's emotional state automatically puts the observer into a matching state, and as cognition grows, that state-matching develops into concern for the other and perspective-taking. Morality grows upward in layers rather than being painted on from above. That's what "bottom up" means. On stage he gave the ingredients, not the growth diagram.

What happened next

PARTLY HOLDS UP · AS OF 2026-10

The research programme, looking for precursors of moral ingredients in other animals, is alive, and the observations of cooperation and consolation were not the target of the fight. But the loudest piece of evidence in the talk, monkeys understanding fairness, did not survive the largest meta-analysis to date, published in 2024: the data favour disappointment, not inequity aversion.

2004: the axis is set. Henrich's and Wynne's letters (above) pinned the debate on one question: does a monkey refuse the cucumber because it compared itself with its neighbour, or simply because it saw something better?

2006 and 2009: no replication. Roma and colleagues re-ran the test with four female capuchins: the watching monkeys rarely refused cucumber, and whether they did had nothing to do with what the neighbour got. Their results "fail to support findings of Brosnan and de Waal", and a frustration account could explain both their data and the original. Three years later Bräuer, Call and Tomasello at the Max Planck Institute tested chimpanzees, bonobos and orangutans with an improved method and found no extra refusals when a competitor got the better food.

2017: disappointment, not envy. Engelmann, Tomasello and colleagues in Leipzig ran a two-by-two design: food handed out by a person or by a machine, partner present or absent. Chimps refused low-value food more when a human gave it than when a machine did, and a partner's presence made no difference. They called it "social disappointment": chimps don't compare payoffs with a partner; they compare how they were treated with how they could have been treated. Protests ranged from throwing the tool at the experimenter to dropping it and walking to the corner, aimed at whoever handed out the food.

2020: Brosnan's position. Asked in an interview looking back at the 2003 paper whether its main conclusions still held, she said yes: "We and others have repeated this study in different forms on capuchins and continue to get the same results."

2024: the meta-analysis. De Waal died of stomach cancer on 14 March, aged 75. In November, Ritov, Völter, Raihani and Engelmann published the field's largest test so far in Proceedings of the Royal Society B. They asked the authors of 30 eligible papers for raw data, got 23, and reanalysed 18 species, 302 individuals and 60,430 observations with pre-registered models. Result: no evidence of inequity aversion in accept/reject tasks, across all species, across primates, and in chimpanzees or capuchins alone. Food disappointment, though, had a significant effect. They state the limits: of the seven missing studies only one reported a positive finding, and animals might still care about fairness in other paradigms or more natural settings.

2025 to 2026: not closed. In 2025 van Leeuwen's group reported that bonobos refused more often when disadvantaged in a token task, and that a human-versus-machine control did not explain it away as social disappointment, though the authors note their second study was "only partially consistent" with inequity aversion. In 2026 a study with Brosnan as senior author tested chimpanzees, capuchins, squirrel monkeys and owl monkeys: when inequity came up on only 25% or 50% of trials, none of the four refused more, even though these capuchins and chimps had reacted in earlier studies where every trial was unequal.

The prosocial side is unsettled too: a 2022 paper is titled "Adult bonobos show no prosociality in both prosocial choice task and group service paradigm".

So the account runs like this. The wolf portrait has been loosened by de Waal and the field around him: animals cooperate, reconcile, and protest when they're short-changed. But after twenty years and sixty thousand trials, the funniest video in the talk looks more like a monkey who saw a grape and was let down by her cucumber than one protesting an unfair split. The philosophers may need to rethink less than he said they did.
