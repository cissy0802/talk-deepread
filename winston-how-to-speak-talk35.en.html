The T Is Very Small: An AI Professor's Forty Years of Teaching How to Speak

Patrick Winston · How to Speak

Decades ago, Patrick Winston was skiing at Sun Valley on what he had heard was Celebrity Weekend. One of the celebrities was Mary Lou Retton, the Olympic gymnast with perfect 10s in the vault, and she was said to be a novice skier. He looked over at the novice slope and saw a young woman who, whenever she lost her balance, made a certain move (on stage he acted it out). That's got to be her, he thought.

Then a second thought arrived. He was a much better skier than she was, and she was an Olympic athlete, and an outstanding one at that. Why?

And I was a better skier because I had the K, and I had the P, and all she had was the T.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

K is knowledge, P is practice, T is talent. Winston was an MIT professor of artificial intelligence who ran MIT's AI Laboratory for twenty-five years. From around 1980 he gave a one-hour talk on how to speak every January, for about forty years. The 2018 session was filmed for MIT OpenCourseWare; he died the following summer. The talk opens with that formula: the quality of your speaking depends on K, P and T, and the T is written very small. So the whole hour is about K, the kind of knowledge you can learn and put to use right away.

The advice is concrete. Don't open with a joke, because people are still putting their laptops away and adjusting to your voice. Open with an "empowerment promise": tell them what they will know at the end that they don't know now. Cycle through the main idea three times, because at any moment about 20% of the room is fogged out. Build a fence around your idea so it isn't confused with someone else's. Number your points out loud so people who drifted off can get back on the bus. After you ask a question, hold the silence; he counted seven seconds.

For tools, he preferred the blackboard, because the speed of writing roughly matches the speed at which people absorb ideas. Props are what people remember. Slides are always too many, with always too many words; they should be condiments, not the main event.

When the goal is persuasion, the rules get sharper. In a job talk you have five minutes to show that you have a vision and have done something. To make work memorable, collect five things that begin with S: a symbol, a slogan, a surprise, a salient idea, and a story. His own PhD thesis on learning arches happened to have all five, and only years later did he understand why it became mildly famous. The last slide should say "Contributions," not "Conclusions," and never show a list of collaborators. And don't end on "thank you," because it suggests everyone stayed only out of politeness.

How has this held up? Most of it is craft, accumulated by a man who gave the same talk for forty years. Nobody has tested the system as a whole, and nobody has refuted it. But the numbers he hung on it run looser than the research. The seven seconds most likely traces to Mary Budd Rowe's work, which studied elementary-school science classes and recommended three to five seconds. The 20% figure is low next to mind-wandering studies, which typically find 30 to 40% off-task. And his explanation for why blackboards work, mirror neurons, which he himself called a "lunatic fringe view," rests on a theory neuroscientists have challenged hard. The plainest advice holds up best: put fewer words on slides, which multimedia-learning research backs again and again. Verdict: partly holds.

The way it has been inflated is typical, too. In 2026 a viral post claimed this one hour would teach you more than every book, course and mentor combined. Winston's own promise was much smaller: somewhere in the hour there would be one technique, "maybe only one," that would someday, at a moment you can't predict, help you get the job.

The Moment

A long wire hangs from the ceiling of MIT's room 26-100, with a big steel ball at the end. Winston is remembering 8.01, MIT's freshman mechanics course. Alan Lazarus was at the front, teaching conservation of energy, kinetic and potential.

Lazarus pulled the ball up against the wall, put his head flat against the wall to steady himself, and let go. The pendulum took many seconds to swing out and back, then gently kissed his nose. "And so you have many seconds to think, this guy really believes in the conservation of energy."

Then the warning: do not try this at home. The first time, you may not just let go. "There's a natural human tendency to push."

The room laughs, because everyone knows what that push would mean. This comes in the middle of the hour, in the section on props. A few minutes later Winston points to Jim Glass, sitting up in the hall, who saw the talk about twenty years earlier and remembered it as "the one where you broke the pointer." "It's amazing how props tend to be the things that are remembered." The whole argument of the hour sits in those two pictures: people remember concrete things, not the words on your screen.

The Speaker, and the Year

Patrick Henry Winston earned his MIT PhD in 1970 under Marvin Minsky, one of the founders of artificial intelligence. Two years later he took over MIT's Artificial Intelligence Laboratory and ran it until 1997, twenty-five years in all. Late in his career he led the Genesis Group, which tried to get computers to understand stories, because he was convinced that understanding stories is a defining feature of human intelligence. The talk shows a flash of his competitiveness: Delores Etter, a fellow member of the Navy Science Board, "made me so jealous I could spit because she'd written 21 books, and I'd only written 17."

According to MIT Open Learning, it began around 1980: griping about the quality of most presentations, Winston was persuaded to give a talk on how to speak himself. It became an IAP fixture, growing in scope and in audience every year. In 2008 Cal Newport described the scene on his blog: every seat filled, every step filled, the floor around the podium filled, and a crowd spilling into the hallway.

The 2018 session was recorded for MIT OpenCourseWare, by which point the talk was nearly forty years old. Winston died on July 19, 2019, at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, aged 76. On January 30, 2020, during IAP, MIT screened the recording in his place, with two colleagues adding ten minutes each. The same year, MIT Press published Make It Clear, the book he had expanded from the talk.

Glossary

  • IAP (Independent Activities Period): MIT's four-week January term, filled with short non-credit courses and talks.
  • Job talk: the hour-long research talk a candidate for a faculty post gives to the whole department; it largely decides the hire.
  • Empowerment promise: Winston's term for telling the audience, at the start, what they will know at the end that they didn't know at the beginning.
  • Heuristic: a rule of thumb that usually works but isn't guaranteed. For an AI researcher, it's shop talk.

The Talk, Unpacked

He opens with military law. The Uniform Code of Military Justice specifies court martial for any officer who sends a soldier into battle without a weapon. Students deserve similar protection: they shouldn't go out into life unable to communicate, because

your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak, your ability to write, and the quality of your ideas, in that order.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

"In that order" is deliberate: the quality of your ideas comes last. Then come the K-P-T formula and Retton on the novice slope, with the lesson that the right knowledge can beat talent. Then the promise for the hour: you'll see many techniques, and one of them, "maybe only one," will someday be the one that gets you the job. It is, he says, "a very non-linear process."

Before starting he sets a rule of engagement: no laptops, no cell phones. He gives three reasons. Humans have only one language processor, so if you're reading email you aren't listening. You distract the people around you, and "Studies have shown that." And an open laptop "drives me nuts," so he does a worse job and your attentive friends don't get the performance they came for. Midway through the explanation he stops to ask someone, "could you shut the laptop, please?"

How to start. Some people open with a joke. He doesn't recommend it: at the start, people are still putting their laptops away and adjusting to your vocal parameters, so jokes usually fall flat. Open instead with the empowerment promise. His example is this hour: by the end of these 60 minutes you'll know things about speaking you don't know now, and one of them will make a difference in your life.

Four heuristics he always carries. First, cycle: go around the subject, then around it again, then again. That isn't treating people as unintelligent; it's because "at any given moment, about 20% of you will be fogged out no matter what the lecture is," so to make sure everyone gets it you have to say it three times. Second, build a fence: show what your idea is not. He draws an arch, then some things that are not arches; in technical terms, my algorithm might seem similar to Jones's, except his is exponential and mine's linear. Third, verbal punctuation: people who fog out need landmarks where they can get back on the bus, so number your sections. He demonstrates it: first came cycling, second building a fence, and now the third. He doesn't name the fourth; he asks the audience. Someone answers: ask a question. So he turns to questions. How long can you pause? He counted seven seconds. It felt like an eternity, "but that's the standard amount of time you can wait for an answer." The question has to be pitched right: too obvious and people are embarrassed to say it, too hard and nobody has anything to say.

Here he states the point of the whole hour. If you now believe there is knowledge to be had about speaking, he has already succeeded. Watch the speakers you admire, ask why they work, and build your own repertoire and your own style.

Time and place. The best time is 11 AM, because "most people at MIT are awake by then, and hardly anyone has gone back to sleep," and it isn't right after a meal. The room should be well lit; dim lights tell humans it's time to sleep. When audio-visual staff say the slides will look better with the lights off, his reply is:

it's extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

Case the place, the way you'd visit a bank a few times before robbing it. Wherever he speaks, he first asks the host to show him the room. That morning, as usual, he also imagined every seat filled with "disinterested farm animals," so that however badly it went, it couldn't be as bad as that. And the room should be more than half full; with ten people in a big hall, everyone wonders what more interesting thing is going on elsewhere.

Boards, props, slides. He divides the tools by purpose: the board for informing (teaching), slides for exposing ideas (job talks, conferences). The board has three virtues. It's graphic. Writing speed roughly matches the speed at which people absorb ideas. And it gives your hands a target. Novices suddenly become aware of their hands, as if they were private parts, and shove them in pockets or behind their backs, which in some cultures is insulting; in a convent in Serbia, a nun simply pulled his hands out from behind his back. He once watched Seymour Papert (the MIT educator and computer scientist) lecture twice, first for content and then for style, and noticed that Papert was constantly pointing at the board at things unrelated to what he was saying. It worked anyway.

The custodians of knowledge about props, he says, are the playwrights. Then he gives Papert's bicycle wheel. Spin it, put some torque on the axle: which way does it go? Mechanical engineers reach for the right-hand rule, forget how to align their fingers, and get it right about half the time, so "their very fancy education gets them up to the point where they're equivalent to flipping a coin." Think about it differently and it's easy: put duct tape on a small piece of the rim, and ask only what happens to that piece when it comes over the top and you blow on it. And then there is Lazarus's steel ball.

Why do students always say "more chalk, less PowerPoint"? He offers what he calls his "lunatic fringe view": empathetic mirroring. When you watch him write on the board, the mirror neurons in your head become actuated and you feel yourself writing; when he describes the steel ball swinging, you feel the ball as if you were him. A slide can't do that. He adds that speed is also involved and has to be separated out.

Slides start with a story from Logan Airport. Coming home from a miserable conference, he stopped for coffee for the only time in his life, and a stranger recognized him. The man was on his way to Europe to give a job talk: would Winston critique his slides? "You have too many, and they have too many words." How did he know? Winston had never seen the man's talk:

There are always too many slides, always too many words.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

Then he walks far from the screen and reads, word for word, a crowded slide that says not to read your slides, committing several crimes at once: too many words, and speaker and screen so far apart that heads swing back and forth as at a tennis match. Then he fixes it step by step: remove the background junk, most of the words, the logos, the title ("I'm telling you the title. It doesn't have to be up there."), and the pointless bullets. The principle is one sentence:

You want the slides to be condiments to what you're saying, not the main event— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

He cites a small experiment by one of his students. Teaching some web-programming ideas, the student put half the information on slides and said the other half, with the halves reversed for a control group. Subjects remembered best what they read on the slides. In the after-action report one said, "I wish you hadn't talked so much. It was distracting." The subjects were freshmen at the student's fraternity.

Then a list of crimes. Fonts too small: asked for a minimum, an audience member says 40 or 50, and Winston agrees; 35 is getting too small, not because you can't read it but because you're probably using it to cram in words. The laser pointer: using one means turning your head, so the audience sees only the back of it. To point at something, put a small arrow on the image and say "look at that guy at the end of arrow number one." Slides too heavy: print the deck and lay it on a table, and too much text and too little white space and imagery jump out. His counterexample is one of his own talks, mostly pictures, with only three or four text slides, such as an excerpt from the Dartmouth AI conference proposal, and he stops to let people read those.

Do these crimes really happen? He shows a photo, to repeated laughter: a talk in MIT's Stata Center, speaker far from the screen, laser pointer in hand, on slide 80, which announces that it is the first of ten conclusion slides. The sponsor of the meeting is reading his email. The co-sponsor is examining the lunch menu. A third man looks attentive, "but just because it's a still picture."

Informing: how to inspire. When his department chair asked him to give this talk to new faculty and stress how to inspire students, he realized he'd never thought about it, so he asked around. Incoming freshmen said they were inspired by a high school teacher who told them they could do it. Senior faculty said someone had helped them see a problem in a new way. Everyone said they were inspired by people who showed passion for what they did. His example is the opening of his own AI class: a program for coloring a map of the United States that would run until the sun had exploded and consumed the earth, and, with a slight adjustment, finishes in seconds. "Isn't that cool?" He also notes that faculty say the most important thing they do is teach people how to think, but ask them how and you get a blank stare. His answer: we are storytelling animals, from fairy tales on through law, business and medical school, so teaching people to think means giving them the stories they need, the questions to ask about them, and ways to analyze them, combine them and judge how reliable they are.

Persuading: oral exams, job talks, getting famous. People fail oral exams mostly through failure to situate (to place the work in its field) and failure to practice. Don't practice in front of people who know your work, your supervisor included, because they will hallucinate material that isn't in your talk. Find friends who don't know what you're doing and start by telling them that if they can't make you cry, you won't value them as friends anymore. The amount of flak you get, he adds, is inversely related to age, so take a committee of gray-haired examiners when you can.

The job-talk rules come from a bar in San Diego. He asked Etter and Bill Weldon of the University of Texas what they look for in a faculty candidate. Within a microsecond Etter said: some kind of vision. Weldon followed: proof they've done something. How long does a candidate have to show both? Five minutes. A vision is a problem somebody cares about plus something new in your approach. He demonstrates with his own work: the problem is understanding human intelligence; the approach is asking what makes us different from chimpanzees and Neanderthals; the answer is that we are symbolic creatures who string descriptions of events into stories. "So that's my stump speech." You show you've done something by listing the steps the problem requires, even if not all are finished, and you close by listing your contributions, mirroring those steps.

Once you have the job, you need to be recognized. Why care about fame? At a fundraising dinner he sat next to Julia Child, the famous American TV cook, and all evening people came over for autographs. He asked her whether it was fun to be famous. She thought for a second: "you get used to it." He continues:

You never get used to being ignored.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

So caring about packaging is legitimate: "Your ideas are like your children. And you don't want them to go into the world in rags." How do you get remembered? He draws Winston's star, five points that all begin with S: symbol, slogan, surprise, salient idea, story. His example is his PhD thesis. The symbol was the arch. The slogan was "one shot learning," because the program learned something definite from every example. The surprise was that you don't need a million examples; one is enough if you use it well. The salient idea was the "near miss," an example that is not an arch but doesn't miss by much: show the program a non-arch whose top piece isn't on top, and it learns that the piece has to be on top. Salient, he stresses, doesn't mean important; it means sticking out, since some theses have too many good ideas and no one knows what they're about. The story is how you did it, how it works and why it matters. That work became mildly famous, he says, and only years later did he realize it had accidentally hit every point of the star.

How to stop. He goes through candidate final slides. A list of collaborators is a letdown: MIT has a thousand faculty, and a screenful of names makes your share look tiny. Collaborators belong on the first slide. He shows several other common final slides (the transcript records his verdicts but not what was on them): one is "the worst possible way to end a talk," which can stay up for twenty minutes and squanders the real estate; another he often sees, "I never see anybody write it down." "Conclusions" are legitimate conclusions nobody cares about. What people care about is what you did, so the final slide should be headed "Contributions," mirroring the steps from the start and staying up while people ask questions and file out.

And the final words? A joke is fine at the end, because by then people have adjusted to you. In another bar, this time in Austin, he asked his colleague Doug Lenat, a fantastic speaker, for his secret:

oh, I always finish with a joke, and that way, people think they've had fun the whole time.— Doug Lenat, as quoted by Winston, How to Speak, 2018

Thank you, he does not recommend:

You will not go to hell if you conclude your talk by saying thank you, but it's a weak move— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

"Thank you," and worse, "thank you for listening," suggests everyone stayed out of politeness while longing to be somewhere else. He plays the endings of two convention keynotes; Chris Christie and Bill Clinton both close with "God bless you, and God bless America." He replays Clinton: here he seems to press his lips together, forcing himself not to say thank you; there he gives a little salute. Another option is to salute the audience: say how much you've enjoyed being there, how stimulating their questions were, and that you look forward to coming back.

That is how he ends. "You know what? I'm glad you're here." By coming, they have shown they understand that how you present and package your ideas matters, "And I salute you for that." Last line: come back again, and bring your friends.

Below the Waterline

In the whole hour, Winston invokes research or gives numbers in only a few places; the rest is forty years of craft. Pulled out one by one, those hooks are generally broader on stage than in the original studies.

Seven seconds. "Wait time" after a question is an old topic in education research, founded by Mary Budd Rowe. Her 1972 paper to the National Association for Research in Science Teaching analyzed over 300 tape recordings of elementary-school science lessons: teachers waited about one second after asking a question, and reacted within an average of 0.9 seconds after a student answered. When training stretched both waits to three to five seconds, analysis of more than 900 tapes showed longer answers, more unsolicited appropriate responses, fewer failures to respond, and more answers from students rated as "relatively slow." Her figure is three to five seconds, not seven, and her setting was elementary classrooms, not an MIT lecture hall. The "standard" seven seconds has no source in the talk.

20% fogged out. No source either, and probably an undercount. Farley, Risko and Kingstone, in Frontiers in Psychology in 2013, note that prior mind-wandering research typically finds off-task responses 30 to 40% of the time. Their own experiment had just 21 undergraduates watching a 40-minute lecture video and rating their attention every five minutes; attention and retention both fell over time while fidgeting rose. That supports cycling and verbal punctuation: listeners drop out more and more as the talk goes on.

Laptops distract neighbors. The study usually cited for "studies have shown that" is Sana, Weston and Cepeda (2013, Computers & Education). In Experiment 1, 40 analyzed introductory-psychology students took laptop notes in a simulated class, and a random half also did unrelated online tasks; test scores were 55% versus 66%. In Experiment 2, 38 students took notes with paper and pencil, and half sat behind two confederates multitasking on laptops in their line of sight; scores were 56% versus 73%. The paper calls these drops of 11% and 17%; strictly, they are percentage points. Small samples and a staged classroom, but the direction matches Winston.

Fewer words on slides. The student experiment he cites (fraternity freshmen, half on screen and half spoken) has no publication this page could find, so all we can report is that he mentioned it. But a body of research supports the advice. Richard Mayer and colleagues found in 2001 that adding on-screen text to a narrated animation made learning worse, the "redundancy effect" in multimedia learning. A 2023 review of 63 studies notes refinements since then: Mayer and Johnson found in 2008 that a few embedded keywords helped memory. The research supports few words, not zero. Titles are the interesting twist. Garner and Alley (2013) showed 110 engineering students (59 versus 51) the same technical presentation on how MRI detects tumors, using either PowerPoint defaults (topic title plus bullets) or "assertion-evidence" slides (a full-sentence claim as headline plus visual evidence). The assertion-evidence group showed better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load and stronger recall at a delayed post-test. Both reject walls of bullets, but where Winston deletes the title, Alley turns it into a sentence.

Mirror neurons. Winston labeled this his "lunatic fringe view," and that candor deserves credit. The theory behind it, that mirror neurons let us understand others' actions, was challenged in 2009 by Gregory Hickok in the Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience: there is no evidence from monkeys that directly tests the theory, and human evidence "makes a strong case against" it. The blackboard may well beat slides, but mirror neurons don't explain why.

The star and his research. Winston's star was a retrospective analysis; he says he didn't know at the time why the arch work caught on. The 1970 thesis, Learning Structural Descriptions from Examples, was supervised by Minsky. The "stump speech" in the talk, humans as symbolic creatures who tell stories, was the program of his Genesis Group. Teaching people to think by teaching stories was not just a classroom tip; it was his theory of human intelligence. Teaching people how to speak and studying how machines understand stories were two sides of one project. Make It Clear (2020) expanded the job-talk structure into the VSN-C framework: Vision, Steps, News–Contributions.

The formula itself changed. In Newport's 2008 notes it reads I = f(K, P, T), with I for impact and the terms "in decreasing order of importance." By 2018 the left side had become the quality of communication. The talk kept evolving for forty years, and the recording is a cross-section of one of them.

What Happened Next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-10

The structural advice (open with a promise, cycle, punctuate, show vision in five minutes, end on contributions) is craft, untested but unrefuted. The minimal-text advice for slides has multimedia-learning research behind it. Most of the numbers and mechanisms he attached (seven seconds, 20%, mirror neurons, "the T is very small") claim more than the evidence does.

When Winston died in July 2019, MIT's obituary singled out the "hugely popular" talk he gave every year. The recording traveled far. In 2022 MIT Open Learning listed the five most-viewed videos in OCW history, with How to Speak listed first, at a combined 41.5 million views. This page could not verify the single video's count from a primary source.

The research threads around it went different ways. Laptops drew the most attention. In 2014 Mueller and Oppenheimer's "the pen is mightier than the keyboard" reported better conceptual answers from longhand note-takers, and it became a main argument for classroom laptop bans. In 2021 Urry and colleagues ran a direct replication with 145 Tufts students (the original had 67), plus a mini meta-analysis of eight studies, and found "only small, statistically nonsignificant differences in quiz performance as a function of note-taking medium." But Winston's reason for banning laptops was never note-taking. It was distraction of yourself and your neighbors, and Sana's 2013 experiments still support that. His rule outran his reasons, and the reasons themselves still stand.

No one has measured K, P and T for speaking. The nearest evidence comes from expertise research. A 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald found that deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance in performance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education and less than 1% for professions. That cuts against P, not K: the heaviest term in Winston's formula, knowledge about speaking, is exactly what nobody has measured. So that claim stays unresolved, with a warning that "talent doesn't matter," as a slogan, says more than any study does. On mirror neurons, Hickok expanded his critique into the 2014 book The Myth of Mirror Neurons (this page read only his 2009 paper).

How the talk got distorted is typical. An August 2026 Upworthy write-up embedded a post claiming the hour teaches more than every book, course and mentor combined. MIT News's summary of that article calls "I always finish with a joke..." something Winston "quipped," but the line is Doug Lenat's, which Winston was quoting. A talk that kept warning against too many words and overclaiming got squeezed into quotable lines, one of them credited to the wrong person.

What stands firmest is the advice Winston didn't tie to any study: tell people at the start what they'll get, and leave them at the end with what you did. No experiment has tested it, but no evidence cuts against it either. The assertion-evidence slides now taught in engineering education point the same direction: fewer bullets, and the speaker as the main event.
