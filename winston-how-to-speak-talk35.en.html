MIT IAP Lecture · 2018

The T is very small: an AI professor packs forty years of podium craft into one hour

Patrick Winston · How to Speak

Patrick Winston opens with military law. The Uniform Code of Military Justice, he says, specifies court martial for any officer who sends a soldier into battle without a weapon, and students deserve the same protection, because sending them into life unable to communicate is sending them in unarmed. Then comes the line everyone quotes:

your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak, your ability to write, and the quality of your ideas, in that order.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

The phrase "in that order" is a joke and also the whole thesis. On the board he writes a formula for the quality of communication: knowledge K, practice P, talent T, and he points out that the T is very small. His evidence is a skiing story. At Sun Valley he spotted the Olympic gymnast Mary Lou Retton wobbling on the novice slope and realized he was a better skier than an outstanding Olympic athlete, because he had the K and the P and all she had was the T.

For the next hour he breaks speaking into small, learnable moves. Don't open with a joke: people are still putting laptops away and adjusting to your voice. Open with an "empowerment promise" instead, telling them what they will know at the end that they don't know now. Cycle around the point three times, because at any moment about 20% of the room is fogged out. Build a fence around your idea so nobody confuses it with someone else's. After you ask a question, dare to wait. He counted his own pause: seven seconds.

On tools he favors the blackboard, because the speed of chalk is roughly the speed at which people absorb ideas. Slides are for exposing ideas, not teaching them. A stranger at Logan Airport once asked him to critique his slides; without looking, Winston told him there were too many and they had too many words, because there always are.

The second half covers specific occasions. A job talk must establish your vision and show you've done something within five minutes. To make work memorable, collect the five items of "Winston's star": symbol, slogan, surprise, salient idea, story. The last slide should list your contributions, and the last words shouldn't be "thank you", which suggests the audience stayed out of politeness. He plays the end of a Bill Clinton convention speech twice so the room can watch Clinton pressing his lips together to stop himself from saying it.

Winston gave this talk at MIT every January for more than forty years. The 2018 version was recorded for OpenCourseWare; he died the following year, and it became one of OCW's most-viewed videos.

How well has it held up? Partly. The skeleton is a veteran's craft knowledge: it doesn't need experiments and is hard to falsify. But the research he hangs on it runs looser than the research. Seven seconds is no standard; Mary Budd Rowe's classic classroom recordings found teachers waited about one second, and stretching that to three to five seconds improved student answers. The 20% figure has no source, and mind-wandering probes usually catch 30–40% of students off task, which is worse than he claimed. Students asking for "more chalk, less PowerPoint" are stating a preference; a 2018 meta-analysis of 48 studies found no difference in learning between the two. Laptops distracting neighbors does have an experiment behind it, but its own authors called a laptop ban "extreme and unwarranted." As for small talent, a meta-analysis of deliberate practice found it explains very little of the differences in education and the professions, so practice may carry less weight than his formula gives it. What holds best is the part he never backed with data: what a speaker should do at the start, the middle and the end.

The moment

Near the end, Winston plays the closing seconds of two political speeches. First Chris Christie at the Republican convention: "God bless you, and God bless America." Then, so as not to be partisan, Bill Clinton at the Democratic convention. The applause rises, and Winston says: let's go back a little bit and redo it.

This time he tells the room to watch for two things. At one point Clinton "seems to be almost pressing his lips together, forcing himself not to say thank you." Then there is a little salute. The clip reaches the spot: "That's where he's pursing his lips." Laughter. A bit further: "There's the salute." More laughter.

An AI professor spends the last minutes of his hour taking a full hall frame by frame through a former president suppressing a pleasantry. That is the lecture in miniature. Nothing in speaking is "natural"; every move is a part that can be taken apart, named and practiced.

The speaker, and the year

Patrick Winston was born in 1943 in Peoria, Illinois, arrived at MIT as an undergraduate in 1961 and never left: bachelor's, master's and doctorate there, faculty from 1970, and director of the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory for 25 years, from 1972 to 1997. His doctoral work with Marvin Minsky taught a program what an "arch" is from a handful of examples. That arch comes back in this lecture.

How to Speak was his standing January event during MIT's Independent Activities Period (IAP), a four-week winter term of short, optional courses. The OCW course page calls it "an MIT tradition for over 40 years," usually given to overflow crowds. The January 2018 session was recorded in full. Students filled the seats, along with colleagues; when he gets to a pointer he once broke on stage, he calls out Jim Glass, who had seen the talk twenty years earlier and remembered it as "the one where you broke the pointer."

At the time Winston was leading the Genesis project, which models how humans understand stories. His belief that storytelling sits at the center of intelligence runs through the whole hour. He died in Boston on July 19, 2019, at 76, which made the 2018 recording the version the world keeps.

The talk, in full

He sets out his argument before getting to work. After the K-P-T formula and the ski story comes his own empowerment promise: you'll see a lot of techniques this hour, and some one of them, maybe only one, will be the one that gets you the job. Then the rules of engagement: no laptops, no phones, because "we humans only have one language processor." If yours is busy browsing, you can't listen, you distract everyone around you, and an open laptop "drives me nuts" and makes him do a worse job.

The first block is how to start: no joke, a promise instead. Then he shows four heuristics he always keeps in mind. The first is cycling, saying the point three times. That isn't because the audience is slow but because

at any given moment, about 20% of you will be fogged out no matter what the lecture is.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

The second is building a fence. To teach a Martian what an arch is, you also show what is not an arch; in technical terms, you might say your algorithm seems similar to Jones's, except his is exponential and yours is linear. The third is verbal punctuation: announce the outline and number the items, so people who drifted off have a place to get back on the bus. For the fourth he makes the room guess. Someone calls out "ask a question," and he moves on to how long you can wait for an answer, reporting what he just counted: seven seconds.

The second block is time and place. The best time for a lecture is 11 AM: people are awake, and it isn't right after a meal. The room must be well lit. When AV staff say slides look better with the lights down, his reply is that "it's extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids." And you case the venue in advance, the way a bank robber visits the bank before the robbery.

The third block, tools, is the longest. The board is for informing: it has graphic quality, the right speed, and it gives nervous hands a target. Props are the playwrights' specialty. Decades ago he saw Ibsen's Hedda Gabler, and what he remembers is the stove fire growing all evening until you just know the manuscript will go in. In his freshman physics course, Alan Lazarus put his head against the wall, released a big steel pendulum ball from his nose, and let it swing across and back to gently kiss his nose, giving the class many seconds to think "this guy really believes in the conservation of energy." Don't try it at home, Winston adds: the first time, there's a natural human tendency to push.

Why do students always say "more chalk, less PowerPoint"? He offers what he calls "my lunatic fringe view": "empathetic mirroring." Watching someone write on the board, or watching the ball swing, your mirror neurons let you feel yourself doing it, and a slide can't give you that.

Then come the slide crimes. Too many words, reading the slides aloud, background junk, logos, titles, bullets: he deletes them one by one in front of you. A laser pointer turns you around so the audience sees the back of your head; a student once told him, "we could all leave, and he wouldn't know." He describes a student's experiment: half the information on slides, half spoken, and subjects remembered what they read. One subject said afterward, "I wish you hadn't talked so much. It was distracting." Then the evidence that it really happens: the 80th slide of a talk, labeled the first of ten conclusion slides, with the meeting's sponsor reading his email.

The fourth block is special cases. To inspire, show passion; when he surveyed people from incoming freshmen to senior faculty, everyone had been inspired by someone who showed passion about what they were doing. Oral exams are usually failed through failure to situate the research and failure to practice. Don't rehearse in front of your advisor, because people who know your work hallucinate material that isn't in the talk. And a job talk has a clock:

So if you haven't expressed your vision, if you haven't told people that you've done something in five minutes, you've already lost.— Patrick Winston, How to Speak, 2018

On getting famous, he recalls sitting beside Julia Child at a fundraiser. Asked whether fame was fun, she said, "you get used to it." What struck him was the other half: "You never get used to being ignored." So packaging is legitimate: "Your ideas are like your children. And you don't want them to go into the world in rags." He uses his own thesis to take apart Winston's star. The symbol is the arch. The slogan is "one shot learning." The surprise is that you don't need a million examples. The salient idea is the "near miss." Last comes the story.

The last block is how to stop. Collaborators belong on the first slide, not the last. "Thank you," "Questions?" and "Conclusions" all waste the most valuable real estate; the final slide should say "Contributions." A joke works at the end, once people have adjusted to your voice. As for thanking them: "You will not go to hell if you conclude your talk by saying thank you, but it's a weak move." Then comes Clinton's lips. Winston's own close is a salute to the audience: "I'm glad you're here," because by coming you've shown you understand that how you package your ideas matters. "And I salute you for that."

Beneath the iceberg

Only in a few places does Winston say "Studies have shown that" or give a number; everything else is craft from forty years at the lectern. Pull those hooks out one at a time, and the stage version is consistently broader than the underlying research.

Seven seconds. The time a teacher waits after a question is an old topic in education research, founded by Mary Budd Rowe. Her 1972 conference paper, the forerunner of her 1974 journal articles, analyzed over 300 tape recordings of elementary science lessons. The mean wait was "on the order of one second," and after a student answered, teachers came back within an average of 0.9 seconds. When training stretched waits to three to five seconds, more than 900 tapes showed longer answers, more unsolicited answers, fewer non-responses, and more participation from "relatively slow" students. Her numbers are three to five seconds, not seven, and they come from elementary classrooms, not an MIT lecture hall. Winston calls seven seconds "the standard amount of time." That standard has no source.

Twenty percent fogged out. This figure has no source either, and it is probably low. A 2013 paper by Farley, Risko and Kingstone notes that mind-wandering research typically finds off-task responses at rates between 30 and 40%. Risko and colleagues found in 2012 that mind wandering rises the longer a lecture runs, and the more students wander, the less they remember. Bunce and colleagues had chemistry students self-report lapses with clickers in 2010 and found mostly short lapses of a minute or less, coming more often as the lecture went on. That supports cycling and verbal punctuation: audiences drop out in short bursts.

Laptops and neighbors. Here "Studies have shown that" has a real referent: Sana, Weston and Cepeda's 2013 experiments at York University. In the first, 40 students watched a 45-minute simulated lecture; those told to multitask online scored 55% on the test against 66% for controls. In the second, 38 students took notes on paper; those seated behind two multitasking laptops scored 56%, against 73% for those with no laptops in view. The effects are substantial, but the authors attached limits. The comparison of effect sizes across the two experiments "should be interpreted with caution," attention was never measured directly, and "A ban on laptops is extreme and unwarranted." Winston takes evidence pointing his way and pushes it to the step its authors rejected.

One language processor. The student experiment he describes, run on freshmen at the student's fraternity, does not appear to have been published, so this page can only report that he described it. The formal research is Richard Mayer's redundancy principle: adding on-screen text to an animation with narration overloads the visual channel. But Adesope and Nesbit's 2012 meta-analysis of 57 studies is more mixed. Hearing and reading together beat hearing alone, especially with materials that have no pictures, and showing only key terms drawn from the narration worked best. What hurts is long text that duplicates your words alongside a picture. That is exactly what his slide surgery cures, but the broader claim that every word on a slide competes with you does not hold.

Chalk versus PowerPoint. Students may prefer chalk, but preference isn't learning. Baker and colleagues' 2018 meta-analysis of 48 studies compared PowerPoint with traditional "chalk and talk" and found an average effect of g = 0.067 with a confidence interval spanning zero. In other words, no difference, and no effect at all for college students. Winston himself labeled the mirror-neuron explanation "lunatic fringe," and the modesty was warranted: the neuroscientist Gregory Hickok's 2014 book The Myth of Mirror Neurons challenges the foundations of that story (this page read only the publisher's description).

The small T. The K-P-T formula has no data behind it, only a ski story. And an Olympic gymnast skiing badly mostly shows that gymnastic talent doesn't transfer to skiing, not that talent is unimportant. There is research on the practice side. Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald's 2014 meta-analysis found deliberate practice explained 26% of performance differences in games, 21% in music and 18% in sports, but 4% in education and less than 1% in the professions, concluding practice is "important, but not as important as has been argued." It measures practice, not the knowledge Winston emphasizes, so it doesn't refute him. But it suggests P may be smaller than he drew it.

What the stage version also leaves out is where this lecture sits in his thinking. His line that "we are storytelling animals" isn't rhetoric. In his 2011 paper on the "Strong Story Hypothesis," he argued that story telling and understanding play a central role in human intelligence and help explain why humans are smarter than other primates. Teaching people to tell their work as a story was the same project he pursued in his lab. He expanded the lecture into a book, Make It Clear: Speak and Write to Persuade and Inform, published by MIT Press in November 2020, after his death, at 360 pages. The book's description carries advice that sits oddly beside the lecture. On getting critiques, it says "do not ask for brutal honesty," while on stage he tells students to open practice sessions by telling friends that if they can't make him cry, he won't value them as friends anymore.

What happened next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-10

The structural advice (open with a promise, cycle, show vision in five minutes, close on contributions) is craft knowledge that has been neither tested nor refuted. The numbers and studies he hung on it, such as seven seconds, 20%, chalk over slides and small talent, mostly claim more than the evidence shows.

By January 2018, How to Speak had been running at MIT for forty years. Winston died on July 19, 2019, and MIT News's obituary singled out the "hugely popular talk every year." On OpenCourseWare the recording traveled far. In 2022, MIT Open Learning listed it among OCW's five most-viewed videos, which together had 41.5 million views (this page could not verify the figure for this video alone). In 2020 his book expanded the lecture into the VSN-C framework: Vision, Steps, News, and Contributions.

The research threads around his claims have moved in different directions. The laptop thread got the most attention. Mueller and Oppenheimer's 2014 "The pen is mightier than the keyboard" reported that longhand note-takers did better on conceptual questions, and it became a favorite argument for classroom laptop bans. In 2019, Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson's replication did not reproduce the conceptual advantage (this page relied on the Learning Scientists blog's summary, not the paper). In 2021, Urry and colleagues' replication, with 74 laptop and 68 longhand participants, concluded that "results do not support the idea that longhand note taking improves immediate learning via better encoding of information." But Winston's reason for banning laptops was never note-taking. It was distraction of the user and the people nearby, and that is supported by Sana's 2013 experiments and by Wammes and colleagues, who in 2019 found in real classrooms that more media multitasking went with worse learning. His rule goes further than his reason; the reason itself holds.

On slides, both the redundancy meta-analysis and the PowerPoint meta-analysis narrow what he said. Too many words does hurt. That any words hurt doesn't hold up, and that chalk teaches better hasn't been measured. On wait time and mind wandering his numbers are off, but the direction (dare to pause, keep giving people a way back in) matches the research, and it is where the research supports him most. On talent, the argument over how much practice explains continues, and nobody has tested his K, P and T.

This page found no scholarly critique of the lecture itself, including its most-repeated rule, never end on thank you. Like the rule against opening with a joke, that is the taste of a very good speaker: worth following, not worth citing as a finding. Online, the lecture now circulates as a set of iron laws of public speaking, and that is where it is most easily bent. Winston said plainly on stage that his real objective was for you to watch the speakers you admire, ask why they work, and "develop your own personal style."
