Nobel Lecture in Literature · 1993

The bird is in your hands: a novelist at the Nobel podium turns and cross-examines herself

Toni Morrison · Nobel Lecture

Stockholm, December 7, 1993. Toni Morrison steps up to give her Nobel Lecture, and her first line is the opening of a fairy tale:

“Once upon a time there was an old woman. Blind but wise.”— Toni Morrison, Nobel Lecture, 1993

The story is short. The old woman is the daughter of slaves, living alone outside of town, famous for her wisdom. Some young people come to expose her as a fraud. They pick the one question only a blind person can't answer: "Old woman, I hold in my hand a bird. Tell me whether it is living or dead."

She doesn't answer. They ask again. Still nothing. The silence goes on so long the young people can barely hold in their laughter. Then she speaks, softly:

“I don’t know whether the bird you are holding is dead or alive, but what I do know is that it is in your hands. It is in your hands.”— same

Morrison says she will read the bird as language and the woman as a practiced writer. Language can die — but by a dead language she doesn't mean Latin. She means language "content to admire its own paralysis": statist language, censored and censoring language, the calcified jargon of the academy, language designed to estrange minorities. Then comes the line that would be quoted more than any other: oppressive language does more than represent violence, it is violence; does more than represent the limits of knowledge, it limits knowledge.

Up to here it sounds like a fine, pointed acceptance speech. Then she does something unexpected: she turns and cross-examines the old woman — which is to say, herself.

What if there was no bird in the children's hands? What if the visit was a clumsy trick to get an adult world that only ever talked about them, and for them, to finally talk to them? The second half of the lecture belongs to the children, and it is an indictment: your answer is artful, but its artfulness embarrasses us and ought to embarrass you. Don't tell us what to believe, what to fear. Tell us a story — "Narrative is radical, creating us at the very moment it is being created."

She doesn't. So the children tell one themselves: a snowy night, a wagonload of slaves left in the dark outside an inn; the door opens and a girl and a boy climb into the wagon with warm cider, bread — and a look. "The next stop will be their last. But not this one. This one is warmed."

Finally the old woman says, "I trust you now." There was no bird in their hands, but they have truly caught it. Her last words: "Look. How lovely it is, this thing we have done – together."

The lecture makes two claims that are harder and more important than the famous line about violence: language is an act, and whoever uses it answers for whether it lives or dies; and an elder who offers only clever riddles, and won't tell her own world, is failing at her job.

What happened next is that one sentence was cut loose. In 1997 the philosopher Judith Butler opened Excitable Speech with a close reading of this lecture — and drew from it an argument against letting the state police speech. Twenty years later, in the American campus fight over whether speech is violence, a conservative commentator quoted the line as the motto of the other side — though the lecture itself denounces "literature banned because it is interrogative." Meanwhile Morrison's own The Bluest Eye kept landing on the American Library Association's list of most challenged books, on the grounds that its depictions of sex and abuse were unfit for children. Verdict: misused. The lecture's meaning holds up; what circulates is half a sentence stripped of its context.

The Moment

Past the halfway mark, it sounds as if she has said what she came to say. "It is in your hands" has been read airtight: the bird is language, the woman is a writer, the young people are the powerful who abuse language, and the responsibility is theirs. She has even delivered an ending fit for a bookmark — "We die. That may be the meaning of life. But we do language. That may be the measure of our lives."

Then she rewinds and tells the opening again, differently:

“But she does not; she keeps her secret; her good opinion of herself; her gnomic pronouncements; her art without commitment.”— Toni Morrison, Nobel Lecture, 1993

A writer who has just received her art's highest honor takes the figure of the wise old writer down off the pedestal and hands her over to a group of children for judgment. This is the hinge of the whole lecture. The first half defends language; the second admits that someone who only defends it may be the one letting the bird die.

The Speaker, and That Year

Morrison was born in 1931 in Lorain, a steel town in Ohio. In his presentation speech, Sture Allén, Permanent Secretary of the Swedish Academy, told a story from her childhood: when her parents fell behind on the rent, the landlord set fire to the house they lived in — while they were in it. The family's response was not resignation but laughter. That, Morrison said, is how you put distance between yourself and the act and take your life back, your integrity back.

In 1993 she was 62, a humanities professor at Princeton, after twenty years as a senior editor at Random House. She had published six novels; Beloved (1987) had won the Pulitzer Prize, and 1992 alone brought the novel Jazz and the critical book Playing in the Dark. She was the first Black woman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Her audience was the Swedish Academy and the Stockholm literary world — the keepers of a canon, in other words. And in those years the loudest fight in American universities was the canon debate: whose books should be taught, and whether "great literature" was universal or a reading list drawn up by white men. Morrison had taken that question head-on five years earlier in her Tanner Lecture at the University of Michigan. So when she spoke in Stockholm about "language designed for the estrangement of minorities" and "the proud but calcified language of the academy," she brought that war with her. The Academy's press release put it plainly: she delves into language itself, a language she wants to liberate from the fetters of race.

Glossary

  • Griot: a hereditary West African storyteller and oral historian. Morrison opens by wondering whether the figure in the tale is "a griot soothing restless children."
  • Tower of Babel: the biblical story of people building a tower to heaven, halted when God confuses their language.
  • Speech act: a concept from philosophy of language — some utterances don't describe the world but do something in it: "I now pronounce you married," "I'm warning you."
  • Canon debate: the American argument of the 1980s and 1990s over which "classics" universities should teach and who gets into literary history.

The Talk, in Full

She starts from a place of deliberate uncertainty. "Once upon a time there was an old woman. Blind but wise." Immediately she wavers: or was it an old man? A guru? A griot? She says she has heard the story in the lore of several cultures. Then she settles on the version she knows — the woman is the daughter of slaves, black, American, living alone outside of town; "Among her people she is both the law and its transgression."

The young people's question is cruel because it targets her disability. Her answer is masterly because she refuses to choose between dead and alive and moves attention from the question to the hands asking it. In Morrison's words, "The blind woman shifts attention away from assertions of power to the instrument through which that power is exercised."

Then she announces her reading: the bird is language, the woman a practiced writer, who thinks of language "partly as a system, partly as a living thing over which one has control, but mostly as agency – as an act with consequences." So "Is it living or dead?" is not an unreal question. Language can die. What does a dead language look like?

“For her a dead language is not only one no longer spoken or written, it is unyielding language content to admire its own paralysis.”— Toni Morrison, Nobel Lecture, 1993

Her list does not aim only at enemies: "the faux-language of mindless media," "the proud but calcified language of the academy," "the commodity driven language of science," language designed for the estrangement of minorities — all are on it. Then the sentence:

“Oppressive language does more than represent violence; it is violence; does more than represent the limits of knowledge; it limits knowledge.”— same

The second half of that sentence usually gets dropped. For Morrison the worst crime of such language is not that it hurts but that it keeps people from thinking: it "cannot form or tolerate new ideas," and it blocks cognition "for both the excluder and the excluded."

She then uses two texts to show what living language looks like. The first is Babel. The conventional reading treats the tower's collapse as a misfortune — with one language, heaven would have been reached. "Whose heaven, she wonders? And what kind?" Perhaps paradise was premature if no one would take the time to understand other languages; had they done so, heaven might have been found at their feet. The second is Lincoln at Gettysburg. "The world will little note nor long remember what we say here" is alive, she argues, precisely because it refused to "encapsulate" 600,000 dead, refused the "final word." "Language can never “pin down” slavery, genocide, war. Nor should it yearn for the arrogance to be able to do so." Its force lies "in its reach toward the ineffable."

She closes the first half with the line later printed on countless posters:

“We die. That may be the meaning of life. But we do language. That may be the measure of our lives.”— same

That would have been a complete lecture. Then comes the turn described above. She reopens the story: perhaps what the children heard in "the bird is in your hands" was "It’s not my problem. I am old, female, black, blind. What wisdom I have now is in knowing I cannot help you. The future of language is yours." What if there was nothing in their hands? What if it was a trick to get an adult to speak to them for once?

Almost the entire second half belongs to the children, and they do not hold back: "Your answer is artful, but its artfulness embarrasses us and ought to embarrass you. Your answer is indecent in its self-congratulation." Is there no song, they ask, no "poem full of vitamins," to pass along? Then comes the lecture's real thesis:

“Make up a story. Narrative is radical, creating us at the very moment it is being created.”— same

They don't want instruction — "Don’t tell us what to believe, what to fear" — they want the world she has known "in the dark places and in the light." Still the old woman says nothing, so the children tell the snowy-night story themselves, with details as dense as one of her novels: slaves whose breath is "indistinguishable from the falling snow"; a boy from the inn who "will have a gun in three years, but now he carries a lamp and a jug of warm cider"; a girl handing out "One helping for each man, two for each woman. And a look."

Silence. Then the old woman:

“Finally”, she says, “I trust you now. I trust you with the bird that is not in your hands because you have truly caught it. Look. How lovely it is, this thing we have done – together.”— same

And there the lecture ends. The first half argues that language is an act and must be answered for; the second turns that argument on the speaker herself. A writer's responsibility is not to produce a clever answer but to tell the story — and the story is best told together with the next generation.

Under the Iceberg

The lecture has no footnotes, but two long texts from the five preceding years sit underneath it.

The first is "Unspeakable Things Unspoken," her Tanner Lecture on Human Values, delivered at the University of Michigan on October 7, 1988 — more than forty pages in print. She had planned to call it "Canon Fodder," setting canon beside cannon: "Cannon defending canon, you might say." This lecture holds the full argument behind the Nobel lecture's catalogue of bad language. Defending Western literature as "universal" and even "race-free," she suggests, may have amounted to "lobotomizing that literature." And she states the alternative to the language of estrangement:

“We are not, in fact, “other.” We are choices.”— Toni Morrison, Unspeakable Things Unspoken, 1988

The second half of the Tanner Lecture is the more revealing. She takes the first sentence of each of her novels to show what "language as an act" looks like inside one sentence. The Bluest Eye opens: "Quiet as it’s kept, there were no marigolds in the fall of 1941." "Quiet as it's kept," she explains, was a phrase she heard as a child from black women talking among themselves — conspiratorial, "Shh, don’t tell anyone else." The reader is pulled into a secret before there is time to put up a defense, while she knows what's coming is "a terrible story about things one would rather not know anything about." The Nobel lecture's abstract claim that language is "mostly agency" becomes, here, a craft.

The Tanner Lecture also rereads Moby-Dick: Melville, she proposes, was exploring not white people but whiteness idealized — the white whale as the moment "when whiteness became ideology." That line of thought became Playing in the Dark in 1992. The Academy's presentation speech quoted its best-known image: she had been looking at a fishbowl — the golden scales, the fronds, the bubbles — and suddenly saw the bowl, "the structure that transparently (and invisibly) permits the ordered life it contains to exist in the larger world." The press release quoted her too: "My work requires me to think about how free I can be as an African-American woman writer in my genderized, sexualized, wholly racialized world." And: "My project rises from delight, not disappointment."

Put these texts back underneath, and the Nobel lecture's list stops looking like a string of angry adjectives. Its "oppressive language" has a precise target: language that calls itself neutral, universal, raceless, and so cannot see its own bowl. That also explains a detail the circulating versions lose — the list includes the language of the academy and of science. She was not criticizing only politicians and demagogues, but also the Princeton she worked in.

The stage left one gap that later mattered. She says oppressive language "is violence" but not what follows from that. The verbs she offers are "rejected, altered and exposed," and among the wrongs she names is "literature banned because it is interrogative; discredited because it is critical; erased because alternate." Her remedy, in the text, points toward more and better language, not less — but she never spells that step out. As for the tale of the blind woman and the bird, she says only that it appears in the lore of several cultures; this page could not locate the version she drew on and makes no claim about its source.

What Became of It

MISUSED · as of 2026-10

"Language is an act with consequences" was taken up and developed seriously by philosophy of language; but the lecture's most famous sentence, cut from its context, became a slogan for the "speech is violence" side of a free-speech fight three decades later — while the lecture itself stands against censorship.

1997: the most careful reader. The philosopher Judith Butler opens Excitable Speech: A Politics of the Performative with a line-by-line reading of this lecture. She admires that Morrison doesn't simply assert "language is agency," but enacts it through a parable:

“Morrison not only offers agency as a figure for language, but language as a figure for agency”— Judith Butler, Excitable Speech, 1997

Yet the book as a whole argues against legal scholars — she names Catharine MacKinnon and Mari Matsuda — who treated hate speech and pornography as conduct to be regulated by the state. Her reason: there is a gap between speech and its effects, and that gap is what lets the injured "resignify" the words and talk back; close it, and you hand the power of judgment to the state, which may later turn it against the very movements that asked for it. In her words: "To insist on the gap between speech and conduct, however, is to lend support for the role of nonjuridical forms of opposition." The philosopher who read the lecture most closely, in other words, read it as an anti-censorship text — consistent with its own denunciation of banned literature.

2017: cut down to a slogan. That spring, protesters shut down a talk by the social scientist Charles Murray at Middlebury College, and similar episodes followed. In July the psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett argued in the New York Times that some abusive speech, by causing chronic stress that damages the body, can count as a form of violence; four days later Jonathan Haidt and Greg Lukianoff rebutted her in The Atlantic (both are paywalled and this page reports their arguments only from summaries). Morrison's line was carried straight onto that battlefield. In April the commentator Mark Bauerlein, in a piece excerpted from The American Spectator, wrote that "words can hurt" had become a truism of identity politics, "As Toni Morrison said in her 1993 Nobel Prize speech," quoted the line, and added his own sneer: "So free speech by conservatives is violence." By then the sentence had lost its parable, its second half about limiting knowledge, and the lecture's anti-censorship stance. It had become a team label.

Meanwhile: her own books. The irony is that "these words will harm readers" is the charge Morrison's own books face most often. The American Library Association's annual top ten most challenged books included The Bluest Eye in 2006, 2013, 2014, and five years running from 2020 to 2024; Beloved appeared in 2006 and 2012, for reasons such as sexual content and depictions of child sexual abuse. In the final week of Virginia's 2021 governor's race, Republican Glenn Youngkin ran an ad featuring a mother who had campaigned to remove Beloved from her son's classes; the ad never named the book or its author. His Democratic opponent, Terry McAuliffe, called it "a racist dog whistle." Morrison had died two years earlier, on August 5, 2019.

Where it stands. "Language is an act with consequences" is a serious proposition in philosophy of language; Butler built directly on it. What remains open is the gap the lecture left itself: if language can be violence, should the answer be more language or less? The text points to more; its most famous half-sentence has been used by one side as a banner and by the other as a target. The verdict is "misused" — not because the sentence is wrong, but because what circulates is only half of it.
