Stanford GSB · 2014

Dare to be dull: how to open your mouth when you are put on the spot, and how much of the science behind it holds

Matt Abrahams · Think Fast, Talk Smart: Communication Techniques

A hundred-odd Stanford business school alumni are on their feet, pointing at objects around the room and shouting the wrong names for them. A chair becomes “porcupine,” the podium “California,” then “salt shaker,” “tennis racket,” “purple.” After thirty seconds Matt Abrahams calls time and asks a question: before we started, how many of you already had your first five or six words lined up?

Plenty of hands go up. That is the disease he came to treat. The game has no way to lose, and the only way to spoil it is to stockpile your answers in advance. In round two he asks people to notice when they are stockpiling, or running a pattern (fruits, words starting with A), to say “thank you, brain,” and to ignore it.

It is a Saturday morning in October 2014, Stanford’s reunion weekend, and the audience will head to the football game afterward. Abrahams teaches strategic communication at the Graduate School of Business. The workshop has a practical origin: a survey asked students what would help them succeed, and the top answer was handling the cold call, the moment a professor turns to you and asks, “What do you think?”

His framework has two layers. First, get anxiety under control: when your heart pounds and you start sweating, tell yourself “this is me feeling nervous”; treat speaking as a conversation rather than a performance, because a performance has a right answer and a talk does not; and drag your attention back into the present, his favorite tool being a tongue twister. Then four steps: get out of your own way, with an improv maxim, “dare to be dull”; see the moment as an opportunity rather than a threat, practiced through a game of trading imaginary gifts; slow down and listen, practiced by holding a conversation in which every word is spelled out; and answer with a structure, most often “What? So what? Now what?” Structure, he says, works in a counterintuitive way:

Structure sets you free and I know that’s kind of ironic, but it’s true— Matt Abrahams, Think Fast, Talk Smart, 2014

The video went on to draw more than ten million views on YouTube. Nine years later he expanded it into a book, Think Faster, Talk Smarter, reorganizing “anxiety plus four steps” into six steps and renaming “dare to be dull” as “maximize mediocrity.”

On stage he says these techniques are “based on academic research,” but he barely names which research. Checked one by one, the verdict is partly holds. The closest experiment to “greet your anxiety” had spider-fearful people describe their feelings while approaching a tarantula: a week later their skin conductance was lower, but their self-reported fear was unchanged, almost exactly his own caveat that it may not reduce anxiety but stops it spiraling. The closest match to “see it as an opportunity” is Alison Wood Brooks’s 2014 “I am excited” study at Harvard Business School; a 2025 direct replication found that saying “I am excited” did make people feel more excited, but judges could not see any better performance. Improv lowering social anxiety has support from a study of Detroit high schoolers, but without a control group. His claim that we process structured information “roughly 40% more effectively and efficiently” I could not trace to any source. The skeleton of the hour is sound: manage the body, then attention, then lean on structure. The numbers hung on it are looser than the stage version.

The moment

On reaching his third way to tame nerves, Abrahams announces that the whole room is going to say his favorite tongue twister together. He warns them first: it is his favorite because if you say it wrong, you say a naughty word, and he will be listening for naughty words this morning.

Repeat after me. It’s only three phrases. I slit a sheet. A sheet I slit. And on that slitted sheet I sit. Very good, no shits.— Matt Abrahams, Think Fast, Talk Smart, 2014

After the laughter he explains the point. For those few seconds, nobody was thinking, “I’m in front of all these people, this is weird, this guy’s having me do this.” Everyone was so focused on getting it right, and on figuring out what the naughty word was, that they were in the present moment. A slightly dirty joke acts out the core mechanism of the hour: nervous people are living in the future, and you have to haul them back into now.

The speaker, and the year

Abrahams graduated from Stanford in 1991. He teaches strategic communication at the GSB and public speaking in Stanford’s Continuing Studies program. He likes to tell the story of training as a campus tour guide: a full quarter of it, including walking backward in a straight line across the Quad and starting over if you drifted, “To this day, I can walk backwards in a straight line.” As an undergraduate he did research with Phil Zimbardo, of Stanford Prison Experiment fame, who also helped start one of the first shyness institutes.

The workshop has a concrete origin. A GSB survey asked students what would help them succeed, and at the top was handling cold calls. So this workshop was built, and the vast majority of first-year GSB students take it. That Saturday morning he gave alumni a “hybrid version.” Most of the room were business school graduates who would head off to the football game afterward, and that same week Chapman University had released a survey of American fears, which he worked in on the spot. He had already published a book, Speaking Up Without Freaking Out, and co-taught a class on improvised speaking with Adam Tobin, a film and new media lecturer, called “improvisationally speaking.”

The video went up on the GSB YouTube channel that December. An old archived copy of the page shows more than fourteen million views. A podcast of the same name followed, and in 2023 the expanded book, Think Faster, Talk Smarter. This hour is where all of it started.

The talk, in full

He opens with a self-deprecating line: “the title of my talk is grammatically incorrect. I thought that might scare a few of you away.” Teaching at the business school taught him that catching people’s attention is hard, and a small trick like that might draw people in. Then he declares the session interactive, because “the way we become effective communicators is by actually communicating.”

The first activity: a sentence on the screen, count the letter f. Most people find three; the answer is six, and the word people miss is a two-letter word ending in f. He says that when he first saw it over twelve years ago he found only three and felt really stupid, so he likes to pass that feeling along, then corrects himself: the real reason is that a room of very smart people did not get this small thing right, and speaking is the same. Little things make a big difference.

Then he sets the scope. Not keynotes, conference presentations or formal toasts you plan for, only spontaneous, off-the-cuff speaking, which he argues is more common than the planned kind: introducing someone at dinner, your boss suddenly asking what you think, a surprise toast, the Q&A. The agenda is simple: planned or not, you need your anxiety under control first.

Anxiety first: three tools

He offers a number and a punch line:

85% of people tell us that they’re nervous when speaking in public. And I think the other 15% are lying.— Matt Abrahams, Think Fast, Talk Smart, 2014

He cites the Chapman survey from that week: among the things Americans fear most, alongside a surprise terrorist attack and identity theft, was public speaking, in the top five. But his chosen word is “manage,” not “overcome”: anxiety gives you energy, helps you focus and tells you that what you are doing matters.

Why must it be managed? He asks how it feels to watch a nervous speaker. “Uncomfortable,” someone calls out. So audiences nod and smile, or disengage, and a nervous speaker looking out at nodding or disengaged faces only gets worse. From this he draws a ground rule for the hour: planned or spontaneous, a speaker’s job is to make the audience comfortable, “Because if they’re comfortable they can receive your message.” Comfortable does not mean sugar-coated. The message can be harsh, but the audience has to be in a place to receive it.

Tool one: greet your anxiety. For most people symptoms arrive in the minutes before speaking: a gurgly stomach, shaking legs, sweat. Then the self-talk starts, “oh, my goodness, I’m nervous. Oh. They’re going to tell I’m nervous. This is not going to go well.” And it spirals. He says research on mindful attention shows that if you greet the symptoms, saying “hey, this is me feeling nervous. I’m about to do something of consequence,” and acknowledge them as normal and natural, you can stem the spiral. He adds a careful limit: it is not necessarily going to reduce the anxiety, but it will stop it spinning out.

Tool two: reframe speaking as conversation rather than performance. This one, he says, he helped research in graduate school. A performance has a right and a wrong way; miss a note or a line and you have made a mistake that throws off the audience and the cast.

But when you present, there is no right way. There’s certainly better and worse ways. But there is no one right way.— Matt Abrahams, Think Fast, Talk Smart, 2014

Saying “this is a conversation” is not enough, so he gives two concrete moves. First, start with questions, which are two-way by nature; the first thing he did today was have everyone count the f’s and raise their hands. His outlines contain no bullet points, only a list of questions he will answer: “Right now I’m answering the question, how do we manage our anxiety?” Second, use conversational language. Nervous speakers back away physically, and they distance themselves linguistically too: “one must consider the ramifications,” or “today we’re going to cover step one, step two, step three.” Swap in “this is important to you,” “we all need to be concerned with,” “first what we need to do is this, the second thing you should consider is here.” The difference is in the pronouns, and it turns a recitation into talk.

Tool three: get present-oriented. This is the line of research he did with Zimbardo as an undergraduate, on how your orientation to time shapes how you react. What they learned is that if you bring yourself into the present instead of worrying about future consequences, you are less nervous. Students fear the grade, founders fear not getting funded, speakers fear not getting the laughs; all of those are future states. There are many ways in. He knows a professional speaker paid $10,000 an hour who does 100 push-ups backstage, but he does not recommend it, because the man comes out breathless and sweaty; a walk around the building will do. Or listen to a song, as athletes do. Or count backward from 100 by 17, “I’m going to pause because I know people in the room are trying.” And then the tongue twister, which has a bonus: it warms up your voice. Nervous speakers rarely warm up; they retreat inside themselves and say bad things to themselves.

Four steps: from getting in your own way to leaning on structure

With anxiety handled, he gets to the core. Four steps, each with an activity, “None of them are more painful than saying the tongue twister out loud,” and each capped with an improv maxim he learned from Adam Tobin, because they stick.

Step one: get out of your own way. The biggest obstacle in spontaneous speaking is yourself. We want to be perfect, give the right answer, make the toast memorable, and that effort is itself the burden. Most of this room are Type A personalities: hard-working, fast-thinking, intent on getting things right, which is exactly what backfires in the moment.

The activity is the one from the short version, “shout the wrong name”: stand, and for thirty seconds point at things and loudly call them anything but what they are. Before starting he asks who already has their first five or six words, and many hands go up. “You all are excellent gameplayers. I told you the game, shout the wrong name. And you have already begun figuring out how you’re going to master the game. That’s your brain trying to help you get it right.” Yet the only way to get this activity wrong is to do what you just did. Call a chair “chair” and there is no penalty, because nobody knows whether you were pointing at the floor under it.

After round one he asks about patterns: some were running through fruits and vegetables, some through words beginning with A. That is the brain getting devious: you told me not to stockpile, so I will give you patterns. Same problem. In their improv class they say your brain is there to help you, its habits have made you successful, but like a windshield wiper you want to wipe those suggestions away. Round two lasts fifteen seconds, with one added rule: when you catch yourself stockpiling or patterning, say “thank you, brain,” and disregard it.

Some say round two was no easier. That is fine, he says; these skills are not like a light switch. You can play the game anywhere; he plays it in traffic. You are working against a lifetime of muscle memory, a brain that acts very fast to help you solve problems. Here he plays with the etymology:

In essence we are reacting rather than responding. To react means to act again.— Matt Abrahams, Think Fast, Talk Smart, 2014

You have thought it, and now you act it out again; that takes too long and is too thoughtful. He wants a response that is genuine and authentic. The maxim is one of his favorites: dare to be dull. He apologizes, since telling a room like this to be dull is offensive, and then explains: rather than striving for greatness, dare to be dull, and if you allow yourself that, you will reach greatness. Set greatness as your target and you over-evaluate, over-analyze and freeze.

Step two: see the situation as an opportunity, not a challenge and a threat. When he coaches executives for Q&A with the media or investors, they often treat it as adversarial, me against them. The first thing he changes is that: a Q&A is an opportunity to clarify and to learn what people are thinking. Feel challenged and you do the bare minimum to protect yourself; see an opportunity and you explain and expand. When your boss at a corporate dinner says, “oh, you know him better than the rest. Would you mind introducing him?”, you say, “great, thank you for the opportunity,” not an inner groan.

The activity, timed for the holiday season, is “give a gift.” In pairs, B hands A an imaginary gift; A opens the box and names the first thing they see, not the thing they already thought of, nor the one after that, “Remember what we talked about before? … you’re stockpiling.” He once looked inside and saw a frog leg; “I don’t know why I saw a frog leg.” Then it is the giver’s turn: whatever the receiver names, you have to run with it, “I’m so glad you’re excited. I got it for you because…” There is no way to get it wrong; you can always return it later for what you wanted.

In the debrief one man found a transmission, and Abrahams is quick: “if you’ve got a Ferrari you need a transmission.” Someone else found a live unicorn. His observation is that at the start some faces looked a little dour and doubtful, and in this round everyone was smiling. Reframe spontaneous speaking as an opportunity, as something you co-create and share, and you become less nervous and less defensive. The maxim is improv’s most famous: yes, and. Many of us live our communication lives saying “no, but.” He is careful to say this does not mean answering yes to a question; it is about the stance you take toward the situation.

Step three: slow down and listen. We tend to listen just enough to think we have got it, then start planning our reply. But a communicator is fundamentally in service of the audience, and if you do not understand what they are asking, you cannot fulfill that obligation.

He announces this game by spelling it: first the rule letter by letter, then the “translation.” With the same partner, tell them something fun you plan to do today, spelling every word. Bad spellers welcome; just don’t say you will “F-E-E-D T-H-E C-A-T” to save letters. One audience member spells G-O-T A-T G-A-M-E, and he spells back E-X-C-E-L-L-E-N-T.

What did we learn, besides that we are not great spellers? You have to pause between words, you have to focus and listen, and you cannot think ahead. This step gets his favorite maxim:

Don’t just do something, stand there. Listen. Listen, and then respond.— Matt Abrahams, Think Fast, Talk Smart, 2014

It flips the old saying, “Don’t just stand there, do something.”

Step four: respond with structure, tell a story. The key to spontaneous speaking, and planned speaking, he says, is structure. First the why: structure increases “processing fluency,” and “We actually process structured information, roughly 40% more effectively and efficiently than information that’s not structured.” Looking at the alumni, he says you will remember, as I do, having to memorize phone numbers. Ten digits are hard. How did you do it? You chunked them: three, three and four. Structure helps us remember.

Then the tour guide story. What was the single most important thing they drilled into the guides during that quarter of training? “Never lose your tour group.” He insists he is not joking. Presenting is the same, never lose your audience, and structure is how you keep them with you. Nobody would follow him if he said, “hi, my name’s Matt. Let’s go.” People want to know where they are going, why and for how long. Structure sets expectations.

He offers two workhorse structures. One is problem, solution, benefit: state the issue, how to solve it, the benefits of following through. It can become opportunity, solution, benefit, since not everything is a problem; maybe there is a market opportunity nobody has captured yet. The other is “What? So what? Now what?”: what it is, why it matters, what the next steps are. It works for answering questions and introducing people. For an introduction, the what becomes who: who they are, why they matter, what we do next, “Maybe listen to them, maybe drink our wine, whatever.”

Spontaneous speaking is hard, he says, because you have to work out two things at once: what to say and how to say it. A structure handles the how. The exercise: sell your partner a Slinky using either structure. The handout coming round lists more structures on the back, and here he delivers the line quoted in the short version: structure sets you free, because once you have it you can think about what to say. It reduces the cognitive load of figuring out content and form at the same time.

To close, he suggests everyone point at things and call them the wrong name on the way to the football game, “If most of us do it, then it won’t be weird.” He practices “What, so what, now what” on his two kids, who do not know it, but who notice that when their friends ask their dads questions, they don’t get “what, so what, now what.”

Q&A

The Q&A becomes a live demonstration of the method. Asked about hostile situations, he says to acknowledge the emotion but not to name it: say “you sound really angry” and the person replies “I’m not angry, I’m frustrated,” and now you are arguing over their mental state. Better: “I hear you have a lot of passion on this issue,” or “I hear there’s great concern from you.” Then reframe and respond. If someone says your product is ridiculously priced, you might say “I hear great concern, and what you’re really asking about is the value of our product,” give the value proposition, and come back to “because of the value we provide we believe it’s priced fairly.” You answered the price question, in a frame you are more comfortable with.

An expert witness asks about hostile cross-examination. He advises preparing a few themes with supporting evidence, not memorized phrasing, assembled as needed, and names the best tool for buying time:

The paraphrase is like the Swiss Army knife of communication.— Matt Abrahams, Think Fast, Talk Smart, 2014

Paraphrasing lets you reframe the question, think about your answer, and pause to make sure you got it right: “so what you’re really asking about is x, y and z.” Then the self-deprecation: “Now I’ve never been an expert witness, because I’m not an expert on anything.” The remaining questions (remote audiences, cultural differences, humor, how a journalist should question people) he handles the same way: listen first, apply a structure, paraphrase when needed.

Beneath the surface

On stage he says “all of these techniques are based on academic research.” Across the hour he names only three things: the Chapman fear survey, his time-orientation research with Zimbardo, and “research on mindful attention.” The rest comes from improv and teaching experience. Here they are, item by item. For the first two he cites no specific paper; what follows are the experiments that match the advice, not studies he cited.

“Greet your anxiety” matches affect labeling research. In 2007 Matthew Lieberman and colleagues at UCLA found in brain scans that choosing an emotion word for a negative image (say, “scared”) dampened amygdala activity while activating right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, which did not activate for non-emotional labels. In 2012 Katharina Kircanski, Lieberman and Michelle Craske took it into a clinical setting: 88 spider-fearful people (top quartile on a screening questionnaire) repeatedly approached a live tarantula with a six-inch leg span, in four groups: label your feelings, reappraise, distract yourself, or plain exposure. A week later, with a different spider in a different room, the labeling group showed lower skin conductance than all the others and marginally more approach than the distraction group, but no difference in self-reported fear. That matches Abrahams’s stage caveat (it may not reduce anxiety, only stop the spiral) surprisingly well. Two differences, though: participants described their emotions while facing the feared thing, which is more than a single “this is me feeling nervous”; and they feared spiders, not speaking.

“Opportunity, not threat” matches Alison Wood Brooks’s excitement reappraisal. Brooks, a Harvard Business School researcher, published the paper in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General in 2014, the year of this workshop. Her second study had 140 students at an elite northeastern university give a two-to-three-minute speech on “why you are a good work partner” in front of an impassive observer and a camera, told that a committee of peers would later review the tape. Beforehand, half said aloud “I am excited” and half “I am calm.” The excited group reported more excitement but not less anxiety; judges rated them more persuasive, competent, confident and persistent; and they spoke longer. Brooks attributed this to a shift from a “threat” to an “opportunity” mindset. The stage version is much broader: the experiment used a single sentence before speaking, while Abrahams describes a whole stance toward a situation; the experiment tested a short prepared speech, not being called on cold.

“Get present-oriented” is not quite Zimbardo’s own theory. Abrahams says he did research on time orientation with Zimbardo as an undergraduate; I could not find a published version of it. Zimbardo’s time perspective theory sorts people into time “zones,” and the present alone splits into hedonistic and fatalistic. On Abrahams’s podcast in 2021, Zimbardo said that when thinking about the present, some people think of their inadequacy, of being shy, while others think of their strengths. In his framework, living in the present guarantees no calm. Abrahams’s “present” is narrower and more concrete: pulling attention off imagined consequences and onto the task at hand, like getting a tongue twister right. That is closer to attention training.

Two numbers have no source. “85% are nervous” is what “people tell us,” unsourced; in the 2023 version it became “we have some research that says upwards of 85%,” still unnamed. For the claim that we process structured information “roughly 40% more effectively and efficiently,” I could find no study behind the figure. Processing fluency is a real concept in psychology, but it usually refers to how easy processing feels and how that feeling shapes judgments, not to a 40% gain in efficiency. The Chapman survey does check out: in 2014 it polled 1,500 people, and public speaking ranked fifth among fears, so “among the top five” is accurate.

The maxims come from improv, not experiments. On stage he credits Adam Tobin. Retelling the talk in 2023, he named his improv teachers as Patricia Ryan Madson, Adam Tobin and Dan Klein. Madson’s 2005 book Improv Wisdom lists thirteen maxims, and the fifth is “Be average.” Their evidence is the accumulated feel of generations of improvisers.

Nine years later. The 2023 version reorganizes “anxiety plus four steps” into six steps in two halves, mindset and messaging. Anxiety is split into symptoms and sources; the symptoms side adds belly breathing and holding something cold, the sources side is still present orientation. “Dare to be dull” becomes “maximize mediocrity,” followed by “so you can achieve greatness.” The opportunity step gains three tools: “not yet” from Carol Dweck’s growth mindset, “next play” from basketball coach Mike Krzyzewski, and treating mistakes as “missed takes,” as in film, where you simply do another take. Listening borrows colleague Collins Dobbs’s “pace, space, grace.” After structure comes a sixth step, focus, with his mother’s saying: “tell me the time, don’t build me the clock.” Neither 2014’s “treat it as a conversation” nor “greet your anxiety” appears as a separate item.

What happened next

Partly holds · as of 2026-10

Affect labeling has solid experiments behind it, with effects matching his own caveat; the only direct replication of the “opportunity” reframe reproduced “feeling more excited” but not “speaking better”; evidence that improv training lowers social anxiety is encouraging but lacks a control group; the two numbers on stage have no traceable source. The method’s skeleton is credible; the science hung on it is looser than the stage version.

Nobody has specifically tested the “anxiety plus four steps” package. What I could find are tests of some of its parts.

Affect labeling: holds, and more narrowly than popular versions. Brain imaging and the tarantula study point the same way: naming emotions lowers physiological arousal, while self-reported fear may not drop. Anyone who turns this into “say you’re nervous and the nerves vanish” has bent it; Abrahams himself does not.

Excitement reappraisal: the feeling replicated, the performance did not. Brooks’s paper has been cited more than 450 times and made it into textbooks, yet no replication had been published. In 2025 Michelle Poynter and Marcia Smith Pasqualini of Avila University ran a direct replication, adding an emotional Stroop task as an indirect measure of anxiety. Seventy-four students, the same topic, the same instructions. Those who said “I am excited” did report more excitement, with an effect size of d = 0.486, larger than the original’s 0.404; as in the original, self-reported anxiety and self-efficacy did not change. But judges found no difference between groups on any of six performance measures, and none of the small differences even ran in the predicted direction. The Stroop showed nothing either. The authors note their statistical power was sufficient to detect meaningful effects, and that Brooks’s data were not available for independent review. Their reading: one sentence changed what people reported feeling, but was not enough to change performance. The samples differed (the replication was mostly women; the original came from a more selective university), so for now it is one study against one, and neither settles it.

Improv training: promising, but the evidence is thin. Peter Felsman of Northern Michigan University and colleagues at the University of Michigan followed 350 students in grades 8 to 12 from low-income Detroit neighborhoods who took ten weeks of free improv classes during school. Before-and-after surveys showed clear drops in social anxiety and in intolerance of uncertainty, and the more the latter fell, the more the former did; the paper appeared in The Arts in Psychotherapy in 2022. Felsman himself says improv does not work immediately for everyone. With only before-and-after measures and no control group, the study can say that students who took the class grew less anxious, not that improv caused it. It supports the general direction of Abrahams’s class: improvising repeatedly in front of others is itself a form of exposure.

The rest is craft wisdom, neither right nor wrong. “Dare to be dull,” “Don’t just do something, stand there,” “What, so what, now what”: nobody has tested these experimentally; their evidence is the improv stage and the classroom. The thing to watch is not any single piece of advice being wrong, but the line “based on academic research,” which makes good craft wisdom look more like science than it is.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · Mahasi’s noting: in the vipassana of the Burmese teacher Mahasi Sayadaw, when a thought arises you silently note “thinking, thinking,” neither pushing it away nor following it, the same move as “this is me feeling nervous.” The difference is purpose: noting aims to see mental events arise and pass, not to give a better talk.
  • Psychology · Wegner’s white bear: Daniel Wegner’s ironic process theory found that telling people not to think of a white bear makes it pop up more, especially under cognitive load. In “shout the wrong name,” trying not to stockpile makes you stockpile: the same structure. The difference is the fix: Abrahams does not ask for suppression but for “thank you, brain,” acknowledging and then letting go, closer to acceptance than to suppression.
  • Cognitive science · Miller’s chunks: George Miller’s 1956 “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two” argued that short-term memory capacity counts chunks rather than single digits; a phone number split three-three-four is chunking. The difference is who benefits: Miller is about the listener remembering, while Abrahams also uses structure to lighten the speaker’s load.
  • Distributed systems · ACK: TCP answers incoming data with an acknowledgment, and the sender decides its next move from it; a paraphrase is a conversation’s ACK, “here is what I received.” The difference is content: an ACK echoes a sequence number, confirming exactly which bytes arrived; a paraphrase is lossy compression that can also reframe the question, which is precisely why Abrahams values it.
  • Chinese philosophy · Zhuangzi’s Cook Ding: the cook in the Zhuangzi carves an ox by meeting it “with spirit, not with the eyes,” not calculating step by step, the blade finding the gaps on its own, like the immediate response that follows “Don’t just do something, stand there.” The deeper difference comes next: Ding says his knife has served nineteen years, so that effortlessness was trained over two decades. Abrahams says it on stage too: these skills are not a light switch, and an hour-long workshop is only a beginning.

Ten takeaways

  1. Spontaneous speaking is more common than planned speaking: introductions, feedback on the spot, surprise toasts and Q&A all count.
  2. A speaker’s underlying job is to make the audience comfortable, since only a comfortable audience can receive the message, even a harsh one.
  3. Anxiety is to be managed, not eliminated: saying “this is me feeling nervous” stops the spiral but may not reduce the nerves.
  4. Treat speaking as conversation, not performance: a performance has one right way, a talk only better and worse ones.
  5. Nerves come from worrying about future consequences; tongue twisters, a walk or counting back from 100 by 17 pull attention into the present.
  6. “Dare to be dull”: targeting greatness makes people over-analyze and freeze, while allowing yourself to be ordinary lets you speak better.
  7. “What? So what? Now what?” hands the how of speaking to a structure, so the speaker only has to think about the what.
  8. Paraphrasing is the Swiss Army knife of communication: it buys time, confirms understanding and lets you reframe a hostile question.
  9. People who say “I am excited” do feel more excited, but in a 2025 replication judges could not see them speaking any better.
  10. Naming emotions lowers physiological arousal while self-reported fear stays put in the tarantula study, consistent with Matt Abrahams’s caveat.
