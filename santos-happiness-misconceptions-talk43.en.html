What you think will make you happy mostly won't: the direction held up, several numbers on stage don't match the papers they came from

Laurie Santos · The Science of Well-Being (1/3: our misconceptions about happiness)

In her own living room, Yale psychologist Laurie Santos holds up two pieces of colored construction paper in front of twenty-odd students. On the screen are Roger Shepard's two tables: one looks long and narrow, the other short and wide. The students say the left one is longer. She lays the cut-out paper over the tabletops and gets it wrong the first time ("Oh, no, it's upside down. Hold on."), then tries again. The two tops are identical. This is where you're supposed to say "Whoa!", she tells them, and then adds the point: now you know, and if you look again you will still see one long table and one short one.

That is how this course opens (The Science of Well-Being, launched on Coursera in 2018; its Yale undergraduate version drew the largest enrollment in the university's history). Its first argument is not about happiness but about knowing. She and the philosopher Tamar Gendler named it the "G.I. Joe fallacy," after the line that closed every public-service spot in the 1980s cartoon: "knowing is half the battle." Santos's claim is that knowing isn't even close to half. You can see through an illusion and your eyes are still fooled; you can understand the research on happiness and still live exactly as before. That is why this course has "homework," and why it is about habits.

Then she asks the students what they want: good grades, internships, high pay, grad school, love and marriage. Over the next hour she crosses the list out item by item. Didn't get the job you wanted? In Dan Gilbert's experiment people predicted a two-point drop and actually dropped far less; those who could blame an unfair decision barely dropped at all. Money? Kahneman and Deaton's data say Americans' day-to-day emotions stop rising at about $75,000 a year, and Yale graduates' median income at 34 is already past that line: "you guys are set." Marriage? Happier for a year or two, then back to baseline. Weight loss? The people who lost weight had the most depressed mood four years later. Good grades? A better grade feels less good than you expect, and a worse one less bad.

Her summary is blunt: you are clueless about what will make you happy. She ends on Sonja Lyubomirsky's famous pie chart as the hopeful note: roughly 50% of happiness comes from genes, 10% from life circumstances, and 40% from what you do, and that 40% is in your hands. The trouble is that we have been aiming at the wrong targets.

The framework is still solid today: people really do systematically overestimate how strongly good and bad events will affect their feelings (the "impact bias"), and they really do adapt to new circumstances. But trace the numbers on stage back to the original papers and a lot falls out. Gilbert's "job" was a $25 product-testing gig, and "afterward" meant ten minutes later. The paper she uses to show that grades don't matter much argues the opposite: when students were asked plainly how they would feel about the grade, their predictions were quite accurate, and part of the so-called overestimation came from how the question was worded. The $75,000 plateau was overturned in 2021 by Killingsworth's 1.7 million real-time reports; in 2023 he and Kahneman reanalyzed the data together and found the plateau only among the least happy people, at around $100,000. As for the 50/10/40 pie, Lyubomirsky herself conceded that the 40% was "likely an over-estimate," and the numbers always described differences between people, never the ingredients of one person's happiness.

So the verdict is partly holds: "your intuitions about happiness are often wrong" survived; the strong version on stage, "so these goals aren't worth chasing," did not. The most ironic and fairest note is that the course tested itself. In a 2021 study, people who completed it ended up happier than a control group who took Introduction to Psychology, a medium-sized effect, though without random assignment or long follow-up.

The moment

Two tables are drawn on a slide. The one on the left looks like a hallway; the one on the right looks like a chopping board. "Which is longer?" The students in the living room say the left. Santos came prepared: she has the tabletops cut out of colored construction paper. She lays one over the left table, then carries it over to the right one, upside down. "Oh, no, it's upside down. Hold on. We got this." Second try: a perfect fit.

This is where you're supposed to go, "Whoa!" Oh my goodness, right? And I actually invested in this construction paper.— Laurie Santos, The Science of Well-Being, video 3

Then comes the real punchline of the lesson: you now know the two tables are the same size, and when you look again you will still see one long and one short. The paper she bought for the demo proves that knowing doesn't change your eyes. She is about to spend an hour dismantling her students' intuitions about happiness, and this opening has already warned them: once they're dismantled, you will still feel them the same way.

The speaker, and the year

Laurie Santos's home field is monkeys. At Yale she works on comparative cognition, testing whether capuchins make the same decision errors people do, and on campus she teaches evolutionary psychology, which she mentions some of these students have taken. She is also head of Silliman, one of Yale's residential colleges, and lives among the students. Starting a new course wasn't required, she says, and preparing it is a lot of work, but she thinks the Yale students in front of her need it.

The recording itself is unusual: no podium, no lecture hall. She sits in her own living room facing about twenty-five Silliman students, with "more coffee and cake outside" afterward. She says outright that this is a pilot, that the goal is to scale it up into a big Yale course, so the students take a happiness survey now and again at the end, to see whether the course works. From her references to "last week" in the New York Times, being "a couple weeks into the new year," and the line "this is before our divisive election," the taping seems to date from early 2017. A year later the undergraduate version, "Psychology and the Good Life," opened for enrollment and drew nearly 1,200 students, about a quarter of Yale's undergraduates and the largest class in Yale's recorded history; the living-room version became the Coursera course.

Of her three reasons for teaching it, the third is the most interesting: she needs it too. She put a screenshot of her own survey on a slide and forgot it showed her score, which places her at about the 40% mark among people her age, below average. The professor teaching the happiness course is not especially happy. She doesn't hide it; she uses it to lead into the G.I. Joe fallacy. She has read all of these studies. She knows. It hasn't helped.

Glossary

  • Affective forecasting: predicting how you will feel after something happens. Santos names it in the Q&A and promises more next lecture.
  • Impact bias: the consistent tendency to overestimate how intensely and how long a future event will affect your feelings.
  • Hedonic adaptation: after life gets better or worse, feelings drift back toward where they were.
  • Emotional well-being vs. life evaluation: the first is how your days feel (did you smile yesterday, were you anxious); the second is the score you give your life as a whole when you step back (a ladder from 0 to 10). They don't always move together.
  • Set point: the hypothesized baseline each person's happiness returns to, partly set by genes.

The talk, in full

Santos opens by saying the course has two jobs: to teach the science of happiness and to teach its practice. One of the first things they will learn, she says, is that knowing what makes you happy doesn't make you happy. So there are no readings, but there is "homework": looking at your own habits. The roadmap: today, misconceptions; next, why our expectations are so bad; after that, what actually works and which strategies to use; finally, how to apply it to society.

She gives three reasons for teaching it. First, the time is ripe: governments in the UK and the US use "nudges" to get people to save more and eat better, and they work even at scale, so there has never been so much to teach. Second, we need it: she says antidepressants are prescribed at 400 times the rate of twenty years ago (a number we will come back to), that recent graduates are unhappier than before, and that the people who need this most are the Yale students in front of her. Third, she needs it herself.

The third reason takes her straight to the lesson's first argument. Thinking "I know this stuff, so I should be good at it" is the first fallacy of the course, one she named with the Yale philosopher Tamar Gendler: the G.I. Joe fallacy. G.I. Joe was a 1980s cartoon, and every episode ended with a public-service spot teaching kids to look both ways and not talk to strangers. The kid would say "Thank you, G.I. Joe. Now I know," and G.I. Joe would answer with the catchphrase: knowing is half the battle. That, Santos says, is the fallacy.

Merely knowing something is not enough to actually change your behavior. And that's the GI Joe fallacy.— Laurie Santos, video 3

To make the fallacy visible she borrows from vision: first the Müller-Lyer illusion, two equal lines that look unequal even to students who have seen the picture before; then Shepard's tables and the construction paper. She could teach them the vision science behind it, she says, and they would still see it the same way. Changing behavior means changing habits, not just learning; that is why their professor is "just as mopey" as they are. The way out, she says, is to do it together, as a socially supported community that commits to habits and gets better.

Then she asks the students. What do you want this semester? "Good grades." This summer? "Internships." Volunteer or paid? "Paid." Low pay or high pay? "High pay." Five years from now? "Grad school." Someone says good friendships and family relationships, and she catches it: I bet many of you are applying for internships right now, and very few of you are putting as much time into making sure your friendships will be good next year. Others want a relationship or marriage. With the goals on the table, she announces that most of what they just said won't make them as happy as they think; anyone who wants to hold on to their false intuitions can leave now and go work on their grades.

A good job. First she has them simulate: pick the opportunity you want most this summer, apply, don't get it. How will you feel? She describes Dan Gilbert and colleagues' experiment, in which college students went for a desirable paid job, predicted how they would feel if rejected, and then were rejected. The students in the room play along, calling out their current scores (sixes, sevens) and their post-rejection scores (four, five, three). The finding: when the rejection was fair, people predicted about a two-point drop and actually dropped "way less than a single point"; when the decision seemed unfair, say the interviewer never looked at their qualifications, they still predicted a two-point drop and actually dropped not at all. Once you can tell yourself "they don't know what they're talking about, they missed the best candidate they ever had," she says, your mood doesn't fall.

But what students want is a well-paid job. How much is enough? She cites Sonja Lyubomirsky's book The How of Happiness: people earning $30,000 say they would need about $50,000 to be really happy; people earning $100,000 don't say "I'm fine," they say they need $250,000. What we think we need jumps up every time we get more.

Money. She opens with a number: in 2015 Americans spent $70 billion on lottery tickets, more than they spent on books, music, movie tickets, sports teams and video games combined. Then the American Freshman Survey, run since 1967; its 2005 round covered over 200,000 students from almost 400 schools. In 2005, 71% of freshmen said being very well off financially was very important; only about half said the same of developing a meaningful philosophy of life. In 1967 it was flipped, with only about 40% prizing wealth.

Does money make people happier? She draws scatter plots for the students, from a correlation of 0.9 down to 0.25, where the dots are so spread out you can't guess someone's satisfaction from their income; then she gives Ed Diener's cross-national data: the average correlation between income and life satisfaction is about 0.1. In poor countries more income goes with more satisfaction, because money buys clean water and basic health care; in rich countries there is barely any relationship. In the 1940s only about two-thirds of homes had indoor showers and hot water, yet people then reported happiness no lower than the 7.2 out of 10 reported in 2005. She cites David Myers's The American Paradox: compared with their grandparents, today's young adults have grown up with much more affluence, slightly less happiness and a greater risk of depression.

Finally, the item she spends the most time on: Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton, both Nobel laureates in economics ("they know what they're talking about"). They asked whether there is a threshold past which more income stops raising happiness. Looking at measures of daily emotion (how much you smiled yesterday, whether you were "not blue," whether you were stress-free), they found things improve with income at the low end and then flatten out, at about $75,000. She brings it straight to the students: the New York Times had just published median income at age 34 by college, and Yale's was $76,000, just over the line.

So, basically once you guys turn 34, you guys are set.— Laurie Santos, video 5

She isn't done. Kahneman and Deaton also measured "life evaluation": imagine a ladder from 0 to 10 and say which rung your life is on. That kept rising with income, only flattening a little at the top. Santos thinks this is where our bad intuition comes from: day-to-day feelings stop moving past $75,000, but our judgment of our own life keeps climbing. Someone making $200,000 thinks "I must have a really good life," without that showing up in how their days feel. Her verdict: money might help a little, say if you're an American making $10,000 a year, but for most people it makes little difference, far less than we think. "If you had that as a goal for the summer, just get rid of that."

Awesome stuff. "If only I had ___, I would be so happy." Her own answers: a pan without scratches on the bottom and a Canada Goose jacket. Yet people in the 1940s had none of this and were no less happy. Worse, wanting it seems to hurt: Nickerson and colleagues surveyed the materialistic attitudes of freshmen entering in 1976 and went back two decades later; the more materialistic had lower life satisfaction, and, she says, more diagnosed mental-health disorders.

True love. She cites Richard Lucas and colleagues' long-term tracking and a German data set: people are happier in the first year or two of marriage, a "honeymoon effect," and then return to baseline, no happier than the unmarried.

A perfect body. A few weeks into the new year, social media is full of before-and-after photos. Jackson and colleagues followed about 2,000 obese people for four years (she describes it as a diet program): the group that lost weight had the most depressed mood, nearly double the others. Cosmetic surgery? In a study following Norwegian adolescents for 13 years, those who later had surgery were already worse off beforehand on suicidal ideation, alcohol use and conduct problems, and afterward those measures got worse.

Good grades. She saves this for last, because it was the students' first wish and "of all the visual illusions" the one they believe most strongly. She describes a study by Levine and colleagues in the University of California: students predicted how happy they would be, on a 1-to-9 scale, if their grade came in higher than expected, as expected, or lower. The students in the room play along: higher, eights and nines; lower, fours and fives, and a few ones, which she praises as honest. The study's predictions looked almost the same: around 8 for higher, around 4 for lower. What actually happened? A higher-than-expected grade felt no different from an expected one; a lower one felt a little worse but not significantly, and much better than predicted. And this was measured right when students saw their grades, not months later.

List crossed out, she asks them to pause and take it in: these goals were hers as an undergraduate and are hers now; where they put their energy, what they apply for, what they stress about, is basically the wrong direction.

you are clueless about what is going to make you happy.— Laurie Santos, video 7 (offered, she says, as the blunter version)

Then she takes on the two explanations students usually offer. One: these things don't work because happiness can't be changed; it's genetic, you're a glass-half-full or glass-half-empty person and that's that. The other: circumstances swamp everything, or good and bad luck balance out. Both, she says, are wrong according to the data, and she puts up Lyubomirsky's pie chart: genes estimated from comparing identical and fraternal twins, circumstances estimated from people who suffered major events (paralysis, job loss, widowhood). Result: genes about 50% ("we do actually have a genetic set point," but smaller than we think); life circumstances only about 10%, "the smallest component"; and the remaining roughly 40% from actions, intentions and habits, which are under our control. The problem isn't that we can't change. It's that we've been working toward the wrong goals.

Last, she heads off a thought: "that science may apply to everyone else, but I really will be happier if I get that job." She calls it the first "annoying feature of the mind": when your mind hands you an intuition, you assume it is normatively correct. Intuitions are often simply wrong: in vision, the two tables; in reasoning, the bat and ball. A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together, the bat costs a dollar more than the ball; how much is the ball? The answer that pops out, 10 cents, is wrong; it's 5 cents. Happiness is the same: you strongly feel that being hit by a car and paralyzed would ruin the rest of your time at Yale, that an unknown relative leaving you 27 million would make it awesome, that a better grade would make things better. None of it is true.

So many of our intuitions about happiness are just wrong.— Laurie Santos, video 7

The Q&A takes up nearly a third of the lesson and adds caveats the main talk left out. A student asks: these are averages, so how do I know I'm not one of the extreme people who really are hit hard? She grants that there are individual differences but says almost everyone mispredicts, only to different degrees; she names the phenomenon, affective forecasting, and says people get both the intensity and the duration wrong. Her own example: a B in an intro class felt awful at the time and doesn't matter to her now. Asked about relative happiness, she says the mind thinks in relative terms, and the most salient reference is other people: people earning $100,000 hang out with people earning $100,000. Social media makes it worse: people post only the best frames of their lives, and you compare them with the full distribution of your own.

Asked how to fight these intuitions without doubting every decision, she gives the lesson's closest thing to a method: borrow from mindfulness and treat cravings as "just thoughts," a technique used most and best in treating addiction; then watch what actually happens.

Treating yourself like a real experimental subject and watching your own data rather than your intuitions— Laurie Santos, video 8

She tells the students to write down the "I'll be a one if I get a bad grade" they just called out, and check it when the grade arrives. Finally she admits a problem for the field: different studies measure different things, some "not blue," some life satisfaction, and we often collapse them together though they may not be telling us the same thing. Homework: take the Authentic Happiness survey and get your score, then take it again at the end.

Beneath the surface

The lesson runs on "one goal, one study," with each study getting a minute or two on stage. Traced back to the originals, the stage versions are usually broader than the papers, and in two places they pull against the papers' own point.

Gilbert's "job." This is Study 6 of Gilbert, Wilson and colleagues' 1998 paper "Immune Neglect." The participants were 91 female introductory-psychology students at the University of Texas at Austin. The "job" was testing products for local businesses for $25; the "interview" was answering 15 questions into a microphone. In the fair condition, three MBA students would reject someone only if all agreed; in the unfair condition, a single MBA student decided on the basis of barely relevant questions. Everyone was rejected, and "afterward" on stage means ten minutes later. Participants predicted that ten minutes on they would be 2.00 (fair) and 1.90 (unfair) points below baseline. In fact, in the fair condition those who had made forecasts were 1.26 below and those who hadn't were 0.84 below; in the unfair condition, 0.00 and −0.10. So "predicted a two-point drop" is right, "way less than one point" holds only for some, and "no drop when it's unfair" matches exactly. What it shows is how much the psychological immune system can do in ten minutes; it did not measure what losing a job means for someone's summer.

The grades paper argues the opposite. The "Lavine" Santos mentions is Linda Levine, Heather Lench and colleagues' 2012 paper "Accuracy and Artifact." Its grades study had 181 introductory-psychology undergraduates at UC Irvine, not "thousands of students in the UC system." What the stage version leaves out is the paper's thesis: that the impact bias is partly a procedural artifact. The standard question asks how happy you will be "in general"; participants read it as how happy they'll be when thinking about the grade, but are later asked about their general mood. Once the researchers asked plainly how participants would feel about the grade, the overestimation vanished: students whose grade was lower than expected predicted 4.34 and reported 4.04; those whose grade was higher predicted 7.33 and reported 7.78. One more detail: 60% of students expected an A-range grade, only 18% got one, and the "higher than expected" group had just 9 to 11 people per condition. On stage the paper supports "grades matter less than you think"; the paper itself says people "have more sophisticated self-knowledge than is commonly portrayed."

Two kinds of happiness at $75,000. Kahneman and Deaton's 2010 paper appeared in PNAS (on stage, "Science"), drew on more than 450,000 Gallup responses, and its title is its conclusion: "High income improves evaluation of life but not emotional well-being." Santos gets both halves right. What she doesn't mention is that the emotion measures were yes/no questions about yesterday, which later turned out to matter.

Weight: not the sample on stage. Jackson and colleagues' 2014 study used 1,979 overweight or obese adults aged 50 and over from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing: not people in a diet program but ordinary people whose weight changed over four years. Depressed mood rose by 289% in the group that lost at least 5% of their weight versus 86% in the stable group; but differences in life satisfaction weren't significant, and the weight losers had clear drops in hypertension and high triglycerides. The authors stress that this doesn't show weight loss causes depression; both may share a cause, such as illness.

The materialism study lost a qualifier. The abstract of Nickerson, Schwarz, Diener and Kahneman's 2003 paper says plainly that the negative impact of the goal for financial success on overall life satisfaction diminished as household income rose, and that the damage was concentrated in relationships and family life. "Materialists were less satisfied twenty years later" is the average; "less so for those who made the money" went unsaid. The extra mental-health diagnoses she mentions don't appear in that abstract, and I couldn't find their source.

The pie's 40% is what was left over. Looking back on the 2005 paper by Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade, Sheldon and Lyubomirsky wrote that the 50% and 10% were estimates "based on certain starting assumptions and a nonexhaustive review of the literature," and the 40% was "the remaining" share, not something measured directly. The original phrasing was that activities might account for "as much as 40% of the variance." Variance describes differences between people in a population, not how one person's happiness is made up. Saying "only about 10% of our actual happiness" comes from circumstances is exactly the chart's most common misreading.

Where two numbers come from. On stage, antidepressant prescriptions are at "400 times" the rate of twenty years ago. The most widely cited figure, from a 2011 CDC data brief, is that the rate of use rose nearly 400% between 1988–1994 and 2005–2008, about fivefold, not four hundredfold; I can't be sure which source she used. The $30,000-wants-$50,000 and $100,000-wants-$250,000 figures she attributes to Lyubomirsky's book; I didn't read the underlying data and report them only as what was said on stage.

What happened next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-10

"Your intuitions about what will make you happy are often wrong" has held up: the impact bias and hedonic adaptation remain mainstream findings. The hard numbers on stage have not: the $75,000 plateau was overturned and then rewritten, the 40% pie was withdrawn by its own authors, and the grades study's paper argues the opposite. The course itself has a medium-sized effect on well-being, on evidence that isn't yet strong.

From 2003, adaptation got complicated. The 2003 Lucas paper behind the marriage claim (German Socio-Economic Panel, 15 years, more than 24,000 people) has a line in its abstract the stage version skips: on average people return to baseline, but "there were substantial individual differences"; those who reacted strongly were still far from baseline years later, and many showed trajectories opposite to what adaptation theory predicts. In 2012 Luhmann, Lucas and colleagues pooled longitudinal data on nearly 66,000 people and found that the effects of life events don't sort neatly by whether the events are supposedly good or bad. "Back to baseline after a year or two" is the average curve, not everyone's fate.

2012–2013, the wording dispute. Levine and colleagues' meta-analysis concluded that overestimation depends heavily on how the question is asked; Gilbert and Wilson replied in 2013 that the impact bias is "alive and well." I haven't read that reply in full and report only that it exists. The safe reading today: people do overestimate, but by less than early studies reported, and the size depends on the wording.

2019, the pie comes apart. Nicholas Brown and Julia Rohrer published "Easy as (Happiness) Pie?" in the Journal of Happiness Studies, arguing that the three slices aren't independent (genes shape which activities and circumstances people end up in), that the 10% for circumstances rests mostly on demographic variables within single countries and is naturally small, and that even correct proportions say nothing about how much one person can raise their happiness. Sheldon and Lyubomirsky replied the same year, opening by saying they "mostly agree"; they wrote that the pie chart "appears to have outlived its usefulness," that the 40% was "likely an over-estimate," and that the effects of positive interventions are "rather small." What they still hold is the core premise: people can raise their well-being through intentional behavior, though the effects may be weaker than they first believed.

2021–2023, the $75,000. In 2021 Matthew Killingsworth reported in PNAS on 1,725,994 real-time phone reports from 33,391 employed US adults: both momentary feelings and life evaluation kept rising with log income, with "no evidence for an experienced well-being plateau above $75,000/y." He and Kahneman then ran an "adversarial collaboration" (two people with opposing results designing the analysis together), facilitated by Barbara Mellers, and in 2023 the three published "Income and emotional well-being: A conflict resolved." The reanalysis found the plateau only among the least happy (the paper speaks of the least happy 20%; the analysis shows it most clearly below the 15th percentile), whose suffering eased as income rose up to about $100,000 and very little beyond. For the happier majority, happiness rose steadily with income, and in the happiest group it accelerated. Kahneman and Deaton's yes/no questions, they wrote, had a ceiling effect and really measured unhappiness rather than happiness. So "at 34 you're set" was wrong: for most people the extra happiness from more money doesn't stop at $75,000. What survives is the weaker point that money's effect is smaller than we think and diminishing on a log scale: you need to double your income for each fixed step.

2021, the course tests itself. On camera Santos said she wanted to see whether this works. In 2021 she, David Yaden and colleagues published the answer in PLOS ONE: three cohorts of Coursera learners, each compared with a control group taking Introduction to Psychology. Both groups' well-being rose, but the Science of Well-Being students ended higher, with effect sizes of d ≈ 0.37 to 0.43, roughly medium. The paper lists its own limits plainly: no random assignment, students in the happiness course were more motivated to improve, many didn't finish, and well-being was measured only shortly after the course, so "the well-being effects we observed" may be "short-lived." In other words, the course at least partly got around the G.I. Joe fallacy: the people who finished didn't just know more, their well-being actually changed. Nobody yet knows for how long.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · craving (taṇhā): The Four Noble Truths trace suffering to craving; the things chased don't bring satisfaction in themselves, which is the same shape as Santos's crossed-out list. The difference is the way out: Buddhism aims to end craving itself, while Santos wants you to swap in better targets.
  • Philosophy · Epicurus's two kinds of wealth: Epicurus held that the wealth nature requires is limited and easy to get, while the wealth of empty desires has no limit. The $30,000-wants-$50,000, $100,000-wants-$250,000 data reads almost like a data version of that line. The difference is method: he reasoned his way there; this lesson tracks people.
  • Neuroscience · Berridge's "wanting" and "liking": Kent Berridge found that the dopamine system drives wanting, while liking runs through a separate, smaller circuitry, so people can intensely want something they don't much enjoy once they have it. The match is the gap between wanting and the feeling after getting; the mismatch is level: Berridge describes neural circuits, this lesson describes conscious predictions gone wrong.
  • Genetics · Lewontin's two pots of seeds: In 1970 Richard Lewontin described one bag of seeds split between rich and poor soil: within each pot, height differences are almost entirely genetic, while the difference between pots is entirely the soil. Heritability is a population statistic about where differences between people come from, not an ingredient list for one person. Reading the happiness pie as "half of my happiness is genes" makes exactly this mistake. The difference is purpose: Lewontin was warning against explaining group differences with heritability, while the pie's authors wanted to show there was room for change.
  • Cybernetics · set points and negative feedback: Hedonic adaptation works like a thermostat, pulling deviations back to baseline. Lucas's data, where some people never return, look more like a system whose set point can be moved by a shock than a thermostat with a fixed dial.

Ten takeaways

  1. The G.I. Joe fallacy, named by Santos and Tamar Gendler, is the belief that knowing is half the battle; knowing barely changes behavior.
  2. Seeing through Shepard's table illusion doesn't stop your eyes from being fooled, and understanding happiness research doesn't make you happier without practice and habits.
  3. In Gilbert's experiment, rejected applicants felt far better ten minutes later than they predicted, and barely worse at all when they could call the decision unfair.
  4. The income people consider "enough" rises with their income: people earning $30,000 want $50,000, people earning $100,000 want $250,000.
  5. Kahneman and Deaton found in 2010 that daily emotions stop rising at about $75,000 a year while people's evaluation of their lives keeps climbing.
  6. An adversarial-collaboration reanalysis found the income plateau only among roughly the least happy fifth, at about $100,000; most people's happiness keeps rising with income.
  7. The happiness boost from marriage fades after a year or two on average, but individual differences are large and some people never return to their old level.
  8. The authors of the grades study argue that the impact bias is partly an artifact of wording: asked how they would feel about the grade, students predicted quite accurately.
  9. "50% genes, 10% circumstances, 40% activities" describes differences between people, the 40% was a leftover, and its authors later called it likely an overestimate.
  10. People who finished The Science of Well-Being ended up happier than an Introduction to Psychology control group, a medium effect without random assignment or long follow-up.
