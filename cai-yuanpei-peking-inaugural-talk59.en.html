Peking University · 1917

A university is not a shortcut to office: how Cai Yuanpei redefined Peking University in 1,300 characters

Cai Yuanpei · Inaugural Address as President of Peking University (Jiuren Beijing Daxue xiaozhang zhi yanshuo)

In January 1917 the new president of Peking University stood before its students and opened by repeating what outsiders said about them: "people outside often denounce this school as corrupt, because those who study here all have their minds on becoming officials and getting rich." The evidence was in their course choices. Most students coming out of the preparatory years crowded into law, the quickest road to an official post. They picked teachers by official rank, not by learning.

The speaker was Cai Yuanpei: a scholar of the old imperial examinations, the Republic's first minister of education, just back from France. The address is short, about 1,300 characters of classical Chinese. Yet for a century, whenever a Chinese university has tried to say what a university is, it has started from one sentence in it:

A university is a place for the study of advanced learning.— Cai Yuanpei, Inaugural Address, 1917 (our translation)

The sentence works by contrast. Cai did not object to studying for a job; at a specialized professional school, he said, learning a trade and then going to work is only natural. What he objected to was treating the university as a trade school, or worse, as a waiting room for officialdom. If you want office, Beijing has law colleges; if you want money, sit the exam for a commerce school. "Why come to this university at all?"

Then he painted an unflattering picture: students who idle and carouse all term, cram the lecture notes before exams, shelve their books the moment exams end, coast for three or four years, and use the diploma to get ahead in society. That, he said, is wronging yourself. And when such people go on to teach or to govern, they wrong their students and the nation.

The address makes three demands: hold fast to your purpose, temper your character, respect your teachers and fellow students. For the third he offered a small example from Germany: in shops, owner and customer thank each other as money and goods change hands. "If ordinary people do this, how much more should university students?" He closed with two practical promises: lecture notes would be cut to outlines so students had to read for themselves, and the library would buy new books.

The weight of the speech lies mostly in what Cai did afterwards. He hired Chen Duxiu, editor of the radical magazine New Youth, as dean of letters, and later brought in Hu Shi. Champions of vernacular writing and defenders of classical prose taught side by side. He abolished the faculty deans in favor of fourteen departments, handed governance to bodies of professors, and in 1920 admitted Peking University's first women students. Two years after the speech, the May Fourth Movement marched out of this campus.

So what became of the idea?

Cai wanted a wall between the university and officialdom, and it did not hold even during his tenure. He opposed student political activism and resigned once over it in 1918. Yet he also urged students to be society's moral leaders, and the historian Timothy Weston argues that this sent students "complex messages" that helped pave the way to May Fourth. Late in life Cai tallied it up: president in name for ten and a half years, "actually at work in the school no more than five and a half; looking back, I am filled with shame."

The verdict is partly held. "A university studies advanced learning" became the standard Chinese definition of a university, and the school really did change its character within a few years. But the two things he meant to wall off, the lure of office and the pull of politics, were never walled off.

The moment

The new president began by scolding his audience on behalf of outsiders.

"People outside often denounce this school as corrupt, because those who study here all have their minds on becoming officials and getting rich." He did not say whether the charge was fair. He went straight to the course choices: graduates of the preparatory program mostly entered law and very few entered letters, because law was "the Zhongnan shortcut to an official salary." (In the Tang dynasty, men would retire to Mount Zhongnan near the capital to build a reputation for virtue in the hope of being summoned to office; the phrase came to mean any back road to a post.) Then:

Being hot for office, they ask of a teacher not how deep his learning is, but only how high his official rank.— Cai Yuanpei, 1917

Cai later recalled that when the Imperial University was first founded, its students were all serving officials. Students were addressed as laoye, "my lord," and the supervisors and teachers as "Grand Secretary" or "Excellency." The young men in front of him were heirs to that line of "lordly students."

The speaker, and the year

Cai Yuanpei came from Shaoxing in Zhejiang. He had passed the highest imperial examinations and served in the Hanlin Academy, the empire's elite scholarly bureau, then went off to study at the University of Leipzig. In 1912 he became the Republic's first minister of education and drove its earliest reforms of the school system. In the winter of 1916, in France, he received a cable from the ministry asking him to come home and run Peking University.

Most of his friends told him not to go. In his 1934 memoir, My Experience at Peking University, he recorded two kinds of advice. One: the place is too corrupt, and "if you go in and can't clean it up, it will only damage your reputation." The other: "if you know it is corrupt, all the more reason to go in and clean it up; even if you fail, you will have done your best." He "in the end followed the second."

Peking University was then China's only national university, and it had gone through several presidents since 1912. Cai knew its habits. In the last years of the Qing he had taught at the Imperial University's translators' college and watched teachers reprint their first-year lecture notes unchanged and read them aloud from the podium. Students crammed the notes before the exam, and "as long as they got through the exam, they never turned a page of them again."

The address was given on January 9, 1917, when he had been "at work in the school only a few days." Around the same time, on the advice of a friend, he read a stack of New Youth, went in person to Chen Duxiu's hotel, and asked him to become dean of letters.

Glossary

  • Preparatory program: the years before degree study, after which students were sorted into faculties. As minister in 1912, Cai had abolished the provincial upper schools and moved this stage inside the universities.
  • Specialized school: vocational colleges of higher education, such as law colleges and commerce schools, meant to turn out people ready for a job.
  • Zhongnan shortcut: a back road to official office; literally, seeking a post by advertising one's retreat to Mount Zhongnan.

The talk, in full

The opening is courteous. Five years earlier, when the translator Yan Fu was president, Cai had been at the ministry and had spoken at the opening ceremony; people change in three days, let alone several years. Then: "Now that I head this school," he has three things to tell them.

First, hold fast to your purpose. This is by far the longest section. The argument runs: to judge whether your purpose in coming here is right, you must first know what a university is. At a specialized school you learn and then take up a job, and that is perfectly natural; "but at a university it is otherwise. A university is a place for the study of advanced learning." Different institutions, different purposes.

Then comes the outsiders' charge: students want office, so they crowd into law and choose teachers by rank. Cai half-concedes the problem. Most Chinese experts in law and politics have gone into government and few teach full time, so the school has had to hire officials as part-time lecturers, "a measure taken because there was no alternative." Whether the charge is fair "we need not discuss," because "the best way to silence slander is to cultivate oneself."

Then he pushes the logic to its end:

If your aim is really to become an official and get rich, Beijing has no shortage of specialized schools ... why come to this university at all?— Cai Yuanpei, 1917

That sentence turns the wish for office from a moral failing into a choice of the wrong school: that road does not run through here. Hence the address's neatest line: "Those who enter law do not do so to become officials; those who enter commerce do not do so to get rich."

Then the portrait: "idling and carousing in ordinary times, cramming the lecture notes at exam time"; once the exams are over, "the books are shelved and never looked at again"; three or four years of going through the motions, and "diploma in hand," off to make one's way in society. He sets out the cost in two layers. For yourself: "with no learning at all, you have wronged yourself." For others, he invokes 1911: we overthrew the Qing because its officials were corrupt, and we are dissatisfied with today's authorities because they have lost their morals. If you do not lay your foundations now, then if you teach later you will fail your students, and "if you enter politics, you will fail the nation." That is wronging others.

Second, temper your character. His description of the capital is blunt: "Customs grow more debased by the day and morality is in ruins, and Beijing society is the worst of all"; "deeds that destroy virtue are everywhere you look." Because university students stand high, they must set an example and push back against the decay, not just keep themselves clean but "also inspire others." Here comes a practical turn. He knows students bent over their desks all day without amusement will suffer physically, so his remedy is "to replace improper amusements with proper ones." He did not ask them to give up amusement, only to change it. Everyone knew what "improper amusements" meant: Weston, reading the university's own daily bulletin, found that gambling, whoring and skipping class went on among students and staff well after Cai arrived.

Third, respect your teachers and fellow students. Treat teachers and staff sincerely; urge each other on, because "since we share this school, we share its praise and its blame." If a classmate disgraces himself, your own good conduct will not clear your name. Then comes the one foreign example in the speech:

When I was in Germany, whenever I went into a shop to buy something, the shopkeeper served me attentively, and as money and goods changed hands we thanked each other. This is a small matter, yet it is necessary in human dealings. If ordinary people do this, how much more should proud university students?— Cai Yuanpei, 1917

The final paragraph is a short program of two items. First, reform the lecture notes: advanced study differs from secondary school, and depends "not only on the teacher's lectures, but still more on one's own private study." From now on the printed notes would give only an outline, and the details and finer points would come "either from the lecturer in person, or from one's own reading." This is aimed squarely at the read-aloud, cram-the-notes teaching described above. Second, buy books: the library holds many, "but few new ones," and funds would be raised to buy more. "As we will meet often from now on, we can discuss things further as they come up," and that is the end.

The address says nothing about academic freedom and nothing about "inclusiveness." None of the slogans Peking University later became famous for had yet been spoken.

Beneath the iceberg

Layer one: the text exists in more than one version. This page compared two: the version on the PKU alumni site, and the Wikisource transcription of the text printed in Dongfang Zazhi (The Eastern Miscellany), vol. 14, no. 4. The structure is the same; the wording differs often. The alumni version has "people mock me as corrupt; my conscience is clear, so what have I to fear?" The Dongfang Zazhi version reads "people mock me as corrupt, but I am not corrupt; my conscience is clear, so what harm can it do me?" The clause "and fewer still entered science" is missing from Dongfang Zazhi altogether. Even the opening differs: in one, five years earlier Cai "was serving at the ministry of education"; in the other, he "was teaching." Quotations on this page use only sentences that match in both versions.

Layer two: the most-quoted line is not in this speech. In 1934 Cai recalled that in his first speech at the school he had declared: "University students should take research and learning as their calling, and should not treat the university as a ladder to office and wealth." That is crisper than anything in the 1917 text, and it appears in neither version of the speech. It is Cai paraphrasing himself seventeen years later: the meaning survived, and the phrasing was polished afterwards.

Layer three: "advanced learning" was not invented that day. In the memoir he says that as minister in 1912 he had already laid down rules: a university with law or commerce must also have letters, one with medicine, agriculture or engineering must also have science, and universities should have a graduate school for research by professors and senior students. His private ideal was more radical. University degree study would cover only letters and science, while applied fields such as agriculture, engineering, medicine, law and commerce would be spun off into specialized colleges, as in Germany and France, "to mark the distinction between learning and technique." He tried to spin off Peking University's law faculty, "but did not achieve it." So the line the speech draws between university and specialized school is the visible tip of a whole design for the school system.

Layer four: "inclusiveness" came a year later. In his November 1918 foreword to the Peking University Monthly, Cai laid out the idea: in the universities of every nation, idealism and materialism, interventionism and laissez-faire "stand side by side in profusion; this is the general rule of freedom of thought, and it is what makes a university great." The phrase he used there was a principle of "embracing and collecting all." In the 1934 memoir he said that as long as a view "argues coherently and has grounds," the different schools were allowed to coexist, which is why advocates of the vernacular such as Hu Shi and Qian Xuantong taught alongside staunch classicists such as Liu Shipei and Huang Kan. The 1917 address dealt with why students should come; the later texts dealt with what teachers may teach. They are two halves of one design.

Layer five: how he put "character" into practice. According to Weston, in 1918 Cai founded a Society for Moral Advancement whose members pledged not to drink, gamble or visit prostitutes, and even not to take official posts or seats in parliament. Cai tied the society to a lineage of "pure" scholar-dissenters: the partisans of the Eastern Han, the Neo-Confucians of the Southern Song, the Donglin academy of the late Ming. Weston notes that in shaping the modern intellectual, "some of his most important ideas were borrowed from the storehouse of Chinese history." The German shopkeeper is decoration. Underneath is the old ideal of the scholar-official: students must be upright because they are meant to be society's example.

What happened next

Partly held · as of 2026-11

"A university studies advanced learning" became the standard Chinese self-definition of a university, and Peking University did change its character within a few years. But the two things the address meant to wall off, the lure of office and wealth and the pull of politics, were not walled off even during Cai's own tenure, and late in life he described himself as "filled with shame."

The first two years: real change. Lou Aofei of East China Normal University, working from the university's daily bulletin, counts more than eighty students formally reprimanded or expelled for truancy or breaking rules in 1918 alone (she calls the count incomplete). Later the three faculties were dissolved into fourteen departments; from 1919 the school was run through committees made up of professors; and in 1920 it formally admitted women.

First crack: the students did not come "only to study." Cai consistently held that students in school "should take study as their highest aim, and should not have any political organizations." In 1918, when students marched to petition the president over a diplomatic dispute, he failed to stop them and resigned (he was persuaded to stay). On May Fourth 1919 he did not try to stop them; after arrests he helped get students released, and on May 9 he left Beijing secretly, leaving behind a notice that quoted the old saying "the one who kills your horse is the crowd cheering by the roadside." Weston's judgment is that Cai told students to bury themselves in learning while also urging them to think independently and lead society, "sending complex messages to the whole student body," and so helped pave the way for May Fourth. The address's own line, "you are university students, your position is high, and you shoulder this heavy responsibility," already carried that tension.

Second crack: the lecture notes. The address promised to reform the lecture notes. In October 1922 a small group of students, protesting a new fee for printed notes, stormed the president's office; Lou quotes Cai's resignation letter describing "coercion and wild shouting, all order gone." Cai resigned together with Jiang Menglin, Li Dazhao and others, and Lou finds that the wish to leave dates from this point. In January 1923 he resigned again, over the education minister's interference in a court case, left for Europe, and never again ran the school day to day.

The myth. Writing in the journal Dushu in 1998, Weston observed that Cai and May Fourth-era Peking University "have gone beyond the bounds of pure scholarship and entered the realm of legend and myth." He points out that the university mattered so much partly because it was the only national university, in the capital, something Cai himself reminded colleagues of in 1929. His overall judgment: Cai is revered "less for what he actually did than for what he tried to do." Weston's 2004 English monograph The Power of Position covers the university's first thirty years; this page did not read the book, only that article.

Today. The "hot for office" the address criticized has not gone away. For China's 2026 national civil service exam, 3.718 million people passed eligibility review, about 98 applicants per post, a ratio that has risen every year since 2023. That does not prove Cai wrong: the exam boom is mostly about the job market, and he always granted that studying for a career is natural. But the force his address set out to resist is still there a century on. In 2012 the Peking University professor Qian Liqun told a conference on "the ideal university" that some universities, his own included, were producing "refined egoists." It is the same disease as the 1917 line about students who "all have their minds on becoming officials and getting rich."

So the address's definition of a university held, and it remains the standard starting point when Chinese universities describe themselves. Its prescription for students, separating learning from office through personal resolve, has not worked in a hundred years.

Crossovers

  • Economics · Spence's signaling model: in 1973 Michael Spence argued that a degree can be valuable because it signals something to employers, not because of what was learned. On that model, the students who leave with "diploma in hand" are being rational. Cai treats this as a moral problem, Spence as an institutional one: as long as employers hire by diploma, exhorting students to higher purpose will hardly change what they do.
  • Social science · Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Students who care only about their marks and not about whether they have learned anything are an example: marks were meant to measure learning, and once students chase the marks, the marks stop measuring it. The difference is that Goodhart describes how indicators fail, while Cai lays the blame on students' motives.
  • Psychology · the overjustification effect: in Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's 1973 experiment, children who had been promised an award for drawing later spent less free time drawing. External rewards can crowd out intrinsic interest, the same structure as "hot for office" crowding out the wish to learn. The difference is that Cai never objected to external aims as such; he only sent people with such aims to the specialized schools.

Ten takeaways

  1. Taking office as president of Peking University in January 1917, Cai Yuanpei opened by repeating the charge that its students studied only to become officials and get rich.
  2. Most preparatory students at the time chose law over letters or science, because law was seen as the shortcut to an official career.
  3. The address's core definition calls a university a place for advanced learning, which separates it from vocational specialized schools.
  4. Cai did not oppose studying for a career; he argued that people seeking office or wealth belonged at law or commerce schools, not at the university.
  5. The address makes three demands (purpose, character, respect for teachers and peers) and promises only two practical reforms: outline-only lecture notes and new library books.
  6. The famous line about learning as a calling, not a ladder to office, comes from Cai's 1934 memoir and is not in the 1917 text.
  7. Cai's ideal university would teach only letters and science, with applied fields moved to specialized colleges to separate learning from technique, but the plan was never carried out.
  8. The principle of inclusiveness was first set out at length in the 1918 foreword to the Peking University Monthly, a year after the address.
  9. Urging students to lead society while telling them to stay out of politics was a contradiction that fed May Fourth and the politicization of Peking University.
  10. Spence's signaling model suggests that while employers hire by diploma, appeals to students' resolve will hardly stop them from coasting for the credential.
