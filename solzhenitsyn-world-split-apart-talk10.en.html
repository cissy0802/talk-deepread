Harvard Commencement · 1978

They expected praise for the free world. In the rain he said: you have lost your courage.

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn · A World Split Apart

June 8, 1978. Harvard’s Tercentenary Theatre, overcast, roughly twenty thousand people under umbrellas. The man on the platform was fifty-nine, in an olive military jacket, beard unkempt, back straight as rebar. Harvard had just given him an honorary doctorate of letters. Four years earlier he had been in a Soviet holding cell, then stripped of citizenship and put on a plane to West Germany. Everyone knew what was coming: a man who had walked out of the Gulag alive was about to thank the free world.

He spoke Russian. The loudspeakers carried an English translation on top of it — his assistant Irina Alberti’s voice, a second behind and much louder. The Harvard Crimson later wrote that the two “grated together, making it difficult to hear.” They grated for sixty-two minutes. He opened on Harvard’s motto, Veritas, said truth “is almost invariably bitter,” said he offered it “as a friend, not as an adversary” — and then began taking apart the place that had invited him.

The first thing an outside observer notices in the West, he said, is a decline in courage. Particularly among the ruling and intellectual elites. Courageous individuals remain, but they no longer have determining influence on public life.

Then the load-bearing beam of the whole speech: the West has handed its life over to the letter of the law. Every conflict is settled by legal text, and if you are right legally, nothing more is asked — nobody mentions that you might still not be entirely right. He had spent his life under a Communist regime, he said, and a society with no objective legal scale is indeed terrible; but a society with no scale other than the legal one is also less than worthy of man.

Then the press. It has become the greatest power in Western countries, exceeding legislature, executive and judiciary — so according to what law was it elected, and to whom is it responsible? And a line that lands harder forty-eight years on: the West has no censorship, yet fashionable ideas are fastidiously separated from unfashionable ones, and the unfashionable, never forbidden, barely reach periodicals, books or lecture halls.

The square went cold when he answered the obvious question — would he hold up the West as a model for Russia? “No, I could not recommend your society as an ideal for the transformation of ours.” Russians, he said, had reached a spiritual intensity through suffering that made the West, in its “spiritual exhaustion,” unattractive. He traced the disease to the Renaissance: man installed at the centre of everything, answerable to nothing above him. If man were born only to be happy, he would not be born to die. The last line was one sentence: “No one on earth has any other way left but—upward.”

Within three days the American editorial pages had worked him over, and First Lady Rosalynn Carter answered him in public: “the people of this country are not weak, not cowardly, and not spiritually exhausted.” He never gave an inch. Years later he wrote in his memoir that he had naively believed he had landed in a society where one could say what one thinks without flattering it — “It turns out that democracy expects to be flattered.”

Was he right? Half of it, and the halves split cleanly. The geopolitics collapsed: he said the West had lost the will to live, that “a hundredfold Vietnam” loomed, that the next war might bury Western civilisation forever. Eleven years later the Berlin Wall came down; thirteen years later the Soviet Union dissolved without a shot. The East he called firmer and stronger saw Russian homicide rates double and double again around the collapse. The cultural diagnosis reads more like today every year: censorship without censors, a press nobody elected, and whatever moral remainder is left once everything has been handed to the letter of the law.

Only most of the people quoting him now quote the first half-page — the decline in courage — and rarely the later line refusing their society as a model. He left the hardest footnote himself: in 1998 he turned down a state award from Yeltsin, saying he could not take it from a government that had brought the country to ruin; in 2007 he accepted a state prize from Vladimir Putin.

The moment

Harvard’s 1978 Commencement came with no sunshine and no encomiums — just umbrellas and grey. Around twenty thousand people sat in Tercentenary Theatre. The fifty-nine-year-old on the platform wore an olive military jacket, his beard unkempt, his back ramrod straight. Harvard had just conferred an honorary doctorate of letters on him.

He began in Russian. English came out of the loudspeakers at the same time — his assistant Irina Ilovayskaya Alberti reading the translation, a second late and considerably louder. That September the Harvard Crimson, looking back, wrote that the two voices “grated together, making it difficult to hear.” On the surviving film they grate for a full sixty-two minutes.

He read out Harvard’s motto first: Veritas. Then he said truth escapes us the moment our concentration flags, and that truth “seldom is sweet; it is almost invariably bitter.”

A measure of bitter truth is included in my speech today, but I offer it as a friend, not as an adversary.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

Nobody in the audience took that as a warning. A man who had done eight years in the camps, written The Gulag Archipelago, and been bundled onto a plane by his own country was obviously about to thank the free world.

The man, and that year

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn (1918–2008) was a Red Army artillery captain, arrested in 1945 for mocking Stalin in a private letter and sentenced to eight years in the camps plus exile. In 1962, during Khrushchev’s thaw, his novella One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich appeared in a Soviet journal — the first time Soviet readers met the camps in an official publication. He won the Nobel Prize in 1970. The Gulag Archipelago, assembled from the testimony of more than two hundred survivors, was published in Paris in late 1973. In February 1974 he was arrested, stripped of citizenship and flown to West Germany the same day. By 1976 he was living behind a fence in the woods of Cavendish, Vermont.

Why 1978, and why Harvard? This was the afterglow of détente. The 1975 Helsinki Final Act had just written Europe’s postwar borders onto paper; Saigon had fallen that April. To Solzhenitsyn the West was buying quiet at a negotiating table with money that people behind the Iron Curtain were paying.

More to the point, this was not his first attempt. The third sentence of the address points at something specific:

Three years ago in the United States I said certain things that were rejected and appeared unacceptable.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

He means his 30 June 1975 speech to the AFL-CIO at the Washington Hilton, and a second one in New York that July. In those he attacked not only the USSR but every attempt to accommodate it. Everyone in Washington wanted to meet him except one man: on Henry Kissinger’s advice, President Ford declined — while Solzhenitsyn was staying a block from the White House.

So the person who climbed onto the Harvard platform in June 1978 was not a grateful exile. He was a man this country’s centre of power had already politely stepped around once, and who had decided not to be polite back.

Glossary

  • Gulag: the acronym of the Soviet agency that ran the labour camps, which became the name for the whole camp system.
  • Détente: the 1970s policy of lowering US–Soviet confrontation through arms control and trade. Solzhenitsyn read it as life support for the oppressor.
  • Helsinki Final Act: signed by 35 states in 1975. Its “Basket III” wrote human rights and freedom of information into the text — and became a weapon in the hands of Eastern European dissidents.
  • Rationalistic humanism: in this speech, the post-Renaissance worldview in which man is the centre of everything and answerable to no higher force. This is the root disease Solzhenitsyn names.

The address, in full

He opens with a geopolitical commonplace and immediately knocks it over. Everyone can see the world split in two — two powers each capable of utterly destroying the other. But treating that split as a political problem, he says, and imagining the danger can be abolished by successful negotiation or a balance of arms, is an illusion. The rifts are deeper and more numerous than that.

How much more numerous? The phrase “Third World” already concedes three; the real number is larger. China, India, the Muslim world, Africa — every ancient, self-contained culture is its own world, “full of riddles and surprises to Western thinking.” Russia was such a world for a thousand years, and Western thinking systematically denied its special character and therefore never understood it. In passing he kills the era’s fashionable convergence theory (the idea that the US and USSR would evolve toward each other): a soothing theory that overlooks the fact that the two are not evolving toward each other at all — and convergence would mean accepting the other side’s defects too.

Since his audience is Western, he says, he will talk about the West. The first verdict is the most quoted line he ever spoke:

A decline in courage may be the most striking feature that an outside observer notices in the West today.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

He qualifies it: the decline shows up particularly in the ruling and intellectual elites; courageous individuals remain, but they have no determining influence on public life. And he gives it a testable shape — the decline is ironically underlined by the same functionaries’ occasional outbursts of boldness when they deal with weak governments and doomed currents, while they go “tongue-tied and paralyzed” before powerful governments, aggressors and international terrorists.

Then well-being. The welfare state finally delivered on the founding promise — government to serve man, man to be free and pursue happiness. But one psychological detail was overlooked: the constant desire for still more things and a still better life stamps worry and even depression on many Western faces. Even biology, he says, tells us that a high degree of habitual well-being is not advantageous to a living organism.

And then the load-bearing beam: the legalistic life. Western society has chosen the organisation best suited to its purposes, one he calls legalistic. The limits of rights and rightness are set by law, and those limits are broad. Every conflict is solved by the letter of the law and that is treated as the ultimate solution: if you are right legally, nothing more is required, and nobody may suggest you could still not be entirely right, or urge self-restraint, renunciation, sacrifice — that “would simply sound absurd.” Everyone pushes to the extreme limit of the legal frame.

I have spent all my life under a Communist regime and I will tell you that a society without any objective legal scale is a terrible one indeed. But a society with no other scale but the legal one is also less than worthy of man.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

Two branches grow off that beam. One: freedom for good deeds and freedom for evil deeds are not equal — a statesman with something constructive in mind must move timidly and prove every step flawless, so “mediocrity triumphs under the guise of democratic restraints.” The other is the line that made people wince that day: “It is time, in the West, to defend not so much human rights as human obligations.”

Then the press, where the writing is sharpest. What real moral responsibility does a journalist or a newspaper bear to its readership or to history? If they have misled public opinion with inaccurate information, even contributing to mistakes at state level, do we know of a single case of open regret from the same journalist or the same paper? No — it would damage sales. The nation may be worse for it; the journalist always gets away with it.

Such as it is, however, the press has become the greatest power within the Western countries, exceeding that of the legislature, the executive, and the judiciary. Yet one would like to ask: According to what law has it been elected and to whom is it responsible?— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

Next comes the most uncomfortable passage to read forty-eight years later. Someone arriving from the East gets a second surprise: the Eastern press is rigidly unified, the Western press has no unifying command and yet gradually reveals a common trend of preferences — a fashion, generally accepted patterns of judgment, whose net effect is not competition but uniformity.

Without any censorship in the West, fashionable trends of thought and ideas are fastidiously separated from those that are not fashionable, and the latter, without ever being forbidden, have little chance of finding their way into periodicals or books or being heard in colleges.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

Your scholars, he says, are free in the legal sense but hemmed in by the idols of the prevailing fad. He cites letters he has received: a teacher at some faraway small college who could do much for the renewal of his country, whom the country cannot hear because the media will not give him a forum.

All of that is merely sharp. What actually chilled the square came next. First he blocks an escape route: do not suspect me of offering socialism as the alternative — and he endorses by name the mathematician Igor Shafarevich’s book Socialism, saying it demonstrates that socialism of any shade leads to the total destruction of the human spirit, and that “so far no one has been found to refute it.” Then he answers the real question:

No, I could not recommend your society as an ideal for the transformation of ours.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

The reason: through deep suffering, Russians have achieved a spiritual intensity that makes the Western system “in its present state of spiritual exhaustion” unattractive. Westerners are weakening; Easterners are hardening. Then the famous illustration — the centre of your democracy and your culture loses electric power for a few hours only, and suddenly crowds of American citizens start looting and creating havoc; the smooth surface film must be very thin indeed.

The last third is political reckoning: Vietnam, George Kennan’s advice on unilateral disarmament, the “weakness” of Western diplomats at the Belgrade conference. Then he digs for the root. How did the West go from triumphal march to this sickness? There was no fatal turn, he says — the West advanced exactly as it proclaimed it would, with brilliant technology, and then suddenly found itself weak. So the error must lie at the base: in the worldview born in the Renaissance and given political expression by the Enlightenment, which he calls rationalistic humanism — the autonomy of man from any higher force, man as the centre of everything that exists. That way of thinking does not admit intrinsic evil in man, nor any task higher than happiness on earth.

The ending is two sentences, a question and an answer:

If, as claimed by humanism, man were born only to be happy, he would not be born to die.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)
No one on earth has any other way left but—upward.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

Under the waterline

First: what Harvard heard that day is not what he wrote. He wrote Russian; the square heard Alberti’s live English; everyone now quotes the later authorized translation. All three differ, and they differ where it matters.

Line up the authorized text against the verbatim transcription of the live audio and you can see what the live version dropped. After “everybody strives toward further expansion to the extreme limit of the legal frames,” the authorized text has a parenthesis that is simply absent from the live English: an oil company is legally blameless when it buys up an invention for a new kind of energy in order to prevent its use; a food manufacturer is legally blameless when he poisons his produce to make it last longer — after all, people are free not to buy it. Also missing is the self-qualification after his passage on crime: that Soviet camps hold a multitude of people called criminals who never committed a crime, and merely tried to defend themselves against a lawless state by means outside the legal framework.

Those losses run in one direction. Both are sentences that pull a claim back and tighten its boundary. What was left standing in the square was the harder version without the parentheses — and that is the version the audience, and the next morning’s editorials, remembered.

Second: behind “I could not recommend your society” sits a whole book he never mentioned on stage. On 5 September 1973 he wrote his Letter to the Soviet Leaders, made public the following year. In it he argued that Russia needs no Western parliamentary democracy; it needs to drop Marxist ideology, fall back to an authoritarian but non-totalitarian order, restore Orthodoxy as its spiritual support, stop the headlong industrial expansion and turn toward the empty northeast.

On 13 June 1974 Andrei Sakharov — father of the Soviet hydrogen bomb, later a Nobel peace laureate — answered him publicly in the New York Review of Books. Sakharov could not accept the tolerance of authoritarian rule: “I consider the democratic path of development the only possible one for any country.” He warned that this “nationalist and isolationist trend,” together with a “religious-patriarchal romanticism,” falling on the fertile soil of existing Great Russian nationalism, “may become dangerous.” He added that Solzhenitsyn nevertheless remained a giant in the struggle for human dignity.

Almost nobody in the Harvard audience had read that exchange. But it is the real answer to “then what would you recommend?” — and in June 1978 Solzhenitsyn said not one word of it.

Third: the one book he endorsed by name from that platform did not age well. Shafarevich was a first-rate algebraic geometer and a fellow dissident; Solzhenitsyn wrote the foreword to the 1980 English edition of The Socialist Phenomenon. In 1982 Shafarevich completed Russophobia, which argued that a small intelligentsia group “dominated by Jews” was hostile to traditional Russian values; it was officially published in the USSR in 1989 and drew wide accusations of antisemitism. The US National Academy of Sciences, whose charter forbade stripping an existing membership, could only ask him to resign; he denied the charge and did not send his resignation by fax until 2003, when the United States invaded Iraq. Solzhenitsyn had told Harvard in 1978 that “so far no one has been found to refute it.” Looking back, the problem is not whether anyone refuted the book — it is whose credit he had staked his own on.

Fourth: two checkable numbers, both weaker than the stage version.

The blackout: he says the power went out “for a few hours only.” In fact Con Edison’s system went down at 9:37 p.m. on 13 July 1977 and full power was not restored until 10:39 p.m. on the 14th — about twenty-five hours, not a few. That night 1,616 stores were damaged, firefighters answered 1,037 fires, roughly 4,500 looters were arrested and 550 police officers were injured. The event was far worse than he described; but compressing twenty-five hours into “a few” is what makes his conclusion work. The real data support “urban order is fragile,” not “it collapses instantly.”

The second number fails more completely. He said:

…though the best social conditions have been achieved in the West, there still remains a great deal of crime; there even is considerably more of it than in the destitute and lawless Soviet society.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, A World Split Apart, 1978 (authorized translation)

In 1978 nobody on earth could verify that sentence, including him. From 1965 to 1987 the Soviet state systematically extracted homicide and suicide deaths from its published statistical tables and filed them in a classified table designated “5B.” Western researchers only got the series around the collapse of the USSR. What it showed points the other way: late-Soviet Russian homicide rates were comparable to or higher than American ones, running near 15 per 100,000 around 1990 — roughly double the US figure — and doubling again to nearly 30 per 100,000 by 2000. The comparison he made from the podium was an unverifiable intuition stated as a fact.

What became of it

PARTLY HELD UP · as of 2026-09

As prophecy it was falsified within thirteen years: the West was not buried, and the hardening East fell apart on its own. As diagnosis, the other half — nothing above the letter of the law, censorship without censors, a press nobody elected — is now quoted by people of every persuasion. Who mostly quote only the first half-page.

Within three days the entire American editorial page had gone at him. Both the New York Times and the Washington Post ran critical editorials within days (I was not able to read either original for this page, so I will not reproduce their arguments). What can be checked directly is the First Lady’s reply: Rosalynn Carter answered his “spiritual exhaustion” charge at the National Press Club — “I’m not a Pollyanna about the mood of the country, but I can tell you flatly—the people of this country are not weak, not cowardly, and not spiritually exhausted.” By September the Crimson reported that editorial pages across the country, especially in the Midwest, had “over-flowed with letters,” most of them siding with her. In 1980 Ronald Berman collected the address together with twelve early responses and six later reflections into a book, Solzhenitsyn at Harvard — a commencement speech that grows its own volume of replies is a measure of the impact.

He never retreated an inch. Forty years on, this passage from chapter 4 of his memoir Between Two Millstones became a National Review cover story:

Before my Harvard speech, I naïvely believed that I had found myself in a society where one can say what one thinks, without having to flatter that society. It turns out that democracy expects to be flattered. When I called out “Live not by lies!” in the Soviet Union, that was fair enough, but when I called out “Live not by lies!” in the United States, I was told to go take a hike.— Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, Between Two Millstones, ch. 4

The prophetic half collapsed cleanly. He told Harvard the West had lost the will to survive, that “a hundredfold Vietnam” loomed over it, that the next war might bury Western civilisation forever. Eleven years later the Berlin Wall came down; thirteen years later the Soviet Union dissolved itself, and NATO never fired a shot. The East he called firmer and stronger went through a doubling of Russian homicide rates around the collapse, and another doubling by 2000. And the Helsinki process he denounced at Belgrade as shameful is now read by historians as one of the few genuinely effective Western instruments of the period: the Basket III human rights provisions handed Eastern European dissidents an internationally recognised yardstick to hold against their own governments — the Soviet Helsinki Watch Groups and Czechoslovakia’s Charter 77 were both built on that foundation. The thing he judged weak turned out to be slow and effective.

The diagnostic half got sharper with age. “Without any censorship in the West, fashionable trends of thought… are fastidiously separated from those that are not” is now practically the opening move in any argument about platforms, algorithms and filter bubbles. “The press is the greatest power — elected by what law, responsible to whom?” became harder to answer, not easier, once that power migrated from newspapers to technology companies. And the beam of the whole speech — no scale above the letter of the law — is the original form of every modern complaint that compliance has replaced ethics.

But the way it gets quoted is the part worth recording. At the fortieth anniversary in 2018 the address was hauled back up on a large scale, overwhelmingly from the American right, and nearly always by way of the decline-in-courage passage. Far fewer went on to the later line refusing to recommend their society as a model for his own; fewer still quoted his wholesale contempt for material prosperity, for television, for “intolerable music,” or the remedy he actually prescribed, which was not a stronger state but “freely accepted and serene self-restraint.” That is not misreading. It is excerpting: cutting a speech that rejected both worlds down to one that rejects only one of them.

And his own last decades left the hardest footnote of all. He returned to Russia in 1994. In 1998 he refused a state award from Boris Yeltsin, saying he could not accept it from a government that had brought the country to its present state of ruin. In June 2007 he accepted a state prize from Vladimir Putin and praised him for the resurrection of Russia; Putin then visited his house outside Moscow to thank him for “all your work for the good of Russia.” After his death in 2008 the journalist Cathy Young wrote the sentence that is hardest to argue with: the man who once demanded the abolition of censorship in a letter to Soviet leaders never protested its revival.

So the verdict can only be “partly held up.” His pathology of a free society was far more accurate than the twenty thousand people in front of him wanted to admit; his prognosis for that disease was wrong in every particular. And the alternative he actually had in mind — the one he did not utter at Harvard, the one Sakharov had publicly warned him about four years earlier — grew into something, in the Russia that outlived him, that this speech never looks at, and that belongs on the shelf right next to it.
