TED2010 · 2010

Values are facts about the well-being of conscious creatures: a neuroscientist tries to fold morality into science

Sam Harris · "Science can answer moral questions"

In February 2010, Sam Harris put two photographs on the TED screen. On the left, the Dalai Lama, who gets up every morning meditating on compassion. On the right, Ted Bundy, who was, Harris said, "very fond of abducting and raping and torturing and killing young women." Then, deadpan: "So, we appear to have a genuine difference of opinion about how to profitably use one's time." The room laughed.

Only then did he make his move. Most Western intellectuals, he said, look at that pair and conclude there is nothing for either man to be really right or wrong about: "He likes chocolate, he likes vanilla." We never do that in science. Up came a photo of the string theorist Edward Witten. If Harris walked into a physics conference and announced that string theory doesn't resonate with him, nothing would happen, because he doesn't understand string theory. "I'm the Ted Bundy of string theory."

That joke is the skeleton of the talk. Harris wants to tear down a division of labor nearly everyone takes for granted: science tells us what is, morality tells us what ought to be. His reply fits in one line: values are a kind of fact, facts about the well-being of conscious creatures. We have no duties to rocks because rocks can't suffer; and since all experience is realized in the brain, the question of which ways of living go better is, in principle, a question for the sciences of mind. He sketched a "moral landscape," peaks of flourishing and valleys of misery. There may be many peaks, the way there are many healthy foods, but the line between food and poison stays clear.

His examples were meant to sting: 21 American states allowed teachers to beat children with wooden boards; in some communities, when a girl is raped, her father's first impulse is to kill her. "What are the chances that represents a peak of human flourishing?" We don't tolerate vast disagreement about how disease spreads, he concluded, and we shouldn't tolerate it about human well-being. Chris Anderson's first words afterward: "So, some combustible material there." Eight months later the talk became a book, The Moral Landscape, subtitled How Science Can Determine Human Values.

What happened next: partly held up. The half that attacks "morality is just taste" mostly holds, and it was never as lonely as Harris implied: the philosopher Kwame Anthony Appiah pointed out that philosophers who favor moral realism outnumber its opponents more than two to one. His paddling question also got an empirical answer: a 2016 meta-analysis covering more than 160,000 children linked spanking to a range of bad outcomes and found no gain in compliance, and by 2026 the number of states allowing school paddling had fallen to 15. The other half, "science determines values," did not get through. The physicist Sean Carroll and the philosopher Russell Blackford both argued that "well-being is the good" is not a scientific finding but a premise loaded in at the start. Harris offered $20,000 to anyone who could change his mind. Answering the winning essay in 2014, he did not concede, but he admitted the subtitle "has become an albatross" and widened "science" to mean our best effort to understand reality at every level. What held is that moral questions have right answers; what didn't is that science is what settles them.

Talk Science can answer moral questions · Venue TED2010, Long Beach · 2010-02-11 · Length about 23 min (including about 5 min of Q&A) · Full text official TED transcript

The moment

About two-thirds of the way in, two faces fill the screen: the Dalai Lama on the left, Ted Bundy on the right. Harris introduces them calmly. One meditates on compassion every morning; the other was "very fond of" abducting, raping, torturing and killing young women.

So, we appear to have a genuine difference of opinion about how to profitably use one's time.— Sam Harris, TED2010

Before the laughter dies, the next slide shows the string theorist Edward Witten. If Harris showed up at a physics conference and declared "String theory is bogus. It doesn't resonate with me," nothing would happen, because he isn't a physicist and doesn't understand string theory:

I'm the Ted Bundy of string theory.— Sam Harris, TED2010

The joke folds the whole argument into a single picture. In physics, an outsider's opinion is allowed not to count. Why, when the subject turns to morality, do a serial killer and the Dalai Lama become one man who likes chocolate and another who likes vanilla?

The speaker, and the year

Sam Harris studied philosophy at Stanford; he later recalled that, "as a student of Richard Rorty's, I drove the man crazy with my realism." The End of Faith (2004) and Letter to a Christian Nation (2006) made him one of the New Atheists. Appiah's review would later call him the head of the movement's "youth wing." By TED2010 he had published two fMRI papers on belief out of UCLA's brain mapping center, and he was finishing a book.

The talk sat at a particular spot. When Harris attacked religion, the commonest comeback was that a scientific worldview has nothing to say about morality, so morality must be left to religion. Stephen Jay Gould had given the idea a famous name, "nonoverlapping magisteria": science governs facts, religion governs meaning and value. Harris came to TED to knock that wall down. The video went online, he wrote later, "just as I was finishing a book on the relationship between science and human values," and it produced "a blizzard of criticism," with thousands of people commenting more or less in real time. As of 2026 the talk shows about 7.58 million views on TED's site.

Glossary

  • Is–ought: David Hume's observation that you cannot get from how the world is to how we ought to act without adding a value premise.
  • Moral realism: the view that there are objective moral facts, so moral judgments can be true or false.
  • Consequentialism: judging right and wrong only by outcomes; utilitarianism is its best-known form. Its rivals are deontology (rules and duties) and virtue ethics (character).

The talk, in full

Harris opens head-on. It is widely believed, he says, that science can help us get what we value but can never tell us what we ought to value. He calls that an illusion, and a dangerous one at this point in history. Then the core:

Values are a certain kind of fact. They are facts about the well-being of conscious creatures.— Sam Harris, TED2010

He starts with rocks. We feel no compassion for rocks because we don't think rocks can suffer; we care more about primates than insects because we think primates are exposed to a greater range of happiness and suffering. That is a factual claim, and we could be wrong about it. Religion is no exception: heaven and hell are still about states of consciousness, and the claim that such states persist after death is itself a factual claim that may or may not be true. Next he lays well-being out as a continuum, from a failed state where mothers cannot feed their children and people are murdered indiscriminately, to a place where a conference like TED is conceivable. Would adding cholera to the water be a good idea? Probably not. Then the brain: experience is realized in the brain, culture changes us by changing our brains, so cultural variation in how people flourish can, in principle, be understood by a maturing science of mind. Out of this comes the "moral landscape," with peaks and valleys that correspond to differences in well-being.

He draws a boundary first: you will not consult a supercomputer about whether to have a second child, or whether you can deduct the full cost of TED as a business expense (laughter).

But if questions affect human well-being then they do have answers, whether or not we can find them.— Sam Harris, TED2010

The first example is domestic. In 21 states, corporal punishment in the classroom is legal: a teacher may beat a child with a wooden board, raising bruises and blisters and even breaking the skin, and hundreds of thousands of children get this every year. "We're not talking about Connecticut." The rationale is explicitly religious, Proverbs' "spare the rod." Is pain and public humiliation a good way to encourage healthy emotional development? Is there any doubt the question has an answer?

Then three analogies, each aimed at an objection. Well-being is undefined? So is health. When the statue on the screen was carved, life expectancy was perhaps 30; now it is about 80 in the developed world. Yet "the distinction between a healthy person and a dead one is about as clear and consequential as any we make in science." Many peaks? There are many healthy foods, and still a clear line between food and poison. Rules have exceptions? "Don't lose your Queen" is a good chess principle, sometimes losing the queen is the only good move, and chess remains "a domain of perfect objectivity."

The second example is the hottest. Some cultures force women to "live in cloth bags" and beat them with steel cable or throw battery acid in their faces if they refuse. The intellectual community, he says, asks "who are we to say" this is wrong; he asks, "who are we not to say this?" He is not talking about voluntary veiling, he adds; women should wear what they want. But what does voluntary mean in a community where, when a girl is raped, her father's first impulse, "rather often," is to murder her out of shame? "Just let that fact detonate in your brain for a minute." Then he swings the camera back to a Western newsstand: we are not on a peak either.

The turn is an irony. The only people who agree with him that moral questions have right answers seem to be religious demagogues, except that their answers come from "a voice in a whirlwind." Then comes the Dalai Lama, Bundy, and the Ted Bundy of string theory: whenever we talk about facts, certain opinions must be excluded. That is what it means to have a domain of expertise.

Does the Taliban have a point of view on physics that is worth considering? No. (Laughter) How is their ignorance any less obvious on the subject of human well-being?— Sam Harris, TED2010

The close: destructive technology cannot be uninvented, so we can no more tolerate vast differences in notions of well-being than vast differences about how disease spreads. "We simply must converge on the answers we give to the most important questions in human life," and to do that we must admit the questions have answers. In the Q&A, Chris Anderson speaks for the other half of the room: some Muslim women describe the veil as a celebration of female specialness. Harris says "I don't think we need an NSF grant to know that compulsory veiling is a bad idea," but that one day we will be able to scan everyone's brain and ask whether people love their daughters just as much in these systems. If the answer is yes, would he change his judgment? Yes, he says, "modulo one obvious fact": you can love someone inside a truly delusional belief system.

Under the iceberg

Where the "brain" on stage comes from. The talk names no study, but Harris's own lab work runs right along this line. A 2008 fMRI study in Annals of Neurology scanned 14 adults as they judged written statements "true," "false," or "undecidable," drawn from seven categories: autobiographical, mathematical, geographical, religious, ethical, semantic and factual. Its conclusion: accepting a statement as true or rejecting it as false appears to rely on "more primitive, hedonic processing in the medial prefrontal cortex and the anterior insula." A 2009 follow-up in PLoS ONE scanned 30 people, 15 committed Christians and 15 nonbelievers: belief was associated with greater signal in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex whether the statement was about God or ordinary facts, and the difference between belief and disbelief "appears to be content-independent." Appiah notes that the book discusses these similarities in how the brain handles moral and nonmoral belief.

Here is the gap. These experiments can show that the brain may use the same machinery to believe a moral statement and a factual one. They cannot show that moral statements are factual statements. Samples of 14 and 30 are small even by fMRI standards. And the Q&A's brain scans asking whether people love their daughters just as much have no counterpart experiment in either paper.

The book's argument is clearer, and leans more on philosophy. The Moral Landscape: How Science Can Determine Human Values (2010) reduces the core to four steps, which Harris spelled out in 2013: morality depends on conscious minds and their capacity for well-being and suffering; minds are natural phenomena, constrained by the laws of the universe; so moral questions have right and wrong answers that fall within the purview of science ("in principle, if not in practice"); so some people and cultures will be wrong about what matters. Missing from the stage is his chosen cornerstone, the "worst possible misery for everyone" argument: imagine every conscious creature in unrelenting agony; everyone must grant that this is bad, and once there is a floor, "better" has meaning. The health, food and chess analogies are its popular version.

"In principle" does most of the work. Answering critics, Harris separated how we verify a proposition from what makes it true: how many breaths did he take last Tuesday? There is no way to find out, but there is a correct number, and "you can bet the farm that it falls between 5 and 5 million."

Sidestepping philosophy was deliberate. In the May 2010 essay "Toward a Science of Morality," he said he had not reached his position by reading moral philosophers but by following the logic of continued progress in the sciences of mind:

I am convinced that every appearance of terms like “metaethics,” “deontology,” “noncognitivism,” “anti-realism,” “emotivism,” and the like, directly increases the amount of boredom in the universe.— Sam Harris, Toward a Science of Morality, 2010-05

In the same essay: "when you scratch the surface on any deontologist, you find a consequentialist just waiting to get out." In other words, the stage claim that all morality comes down to concern for conscious experience is not an empirical discovery. It is a philosophical verdict: consequentialism is the only moral theory that makes sense. The 18 minutes never said so, and that is exactly where the critics went in.

What happened next

PARTLY HELD UP · as of 2026-11

"Moral questions have right answers, relativism fails, many value questions have empirical answers" held, and was never as novel as advertised. "Science can determine human values" did not: well-being-as-the-good is not a scientific finding, and Harris later widened "science" to mean all rational inquiry.

2010: a physicist, then philosophers. About a month after the talk, the Caltech physicist Sean Carroll wrote two blog posts in reply; the May one was titled "You Can't Derive Ought From Is." His three points: there is no single definition of well-being; maximizing it as the goal of morality is a consequentialist stance, not a self-evident one; and there is no simple way to add up well-being across people. His conclusion: "Morality is not part of science, however much we would like it to be." Harris replied that physics too must presuppose the value of logic and evidence. When the book came out in October, Kwame Anthony Appiah wrote in the New York Times Book Review that what Harris endorses is "something very like utilitarianism," with two centuries of familiar problems attached. He also noted that a survey found philosophers more than twice as likely to favor moral realism as anti-realism, so the relativist academy in the talk was partly a straw man.

you can hold that there are moral truths that can be rationally investigated without holding that the experimental sciences provide the right methods for doing so.— Kwame Anthony Appiah, New York Times Book Review, 2010-10

In January 2011 the philosopher Russell Blackford, in a long review in the Journal of Evolution and Technology, accepted many of the book's practical conclusions but said Harris is "no more successful in deriving ought from is than anyone else has ever been." Carroll then answered the breaths analogy: "I know what a breath is; I don't know what a 'unit of well-being is.'"

2013–2014: the prize. Harris offered $2,000 for the best essay of under 1,000 words refuting his central argument, and $20,000 plus a public recantation if it changed his mind. Blackford judged; well over 400 essays came in. The winner, Ryan Born, wrote that the proposed science "cannot derive moral judgments solely from scientific descriptions of the world." Harris replied in June 2014. He did not concede, but he gave ground twice. First, the subtitle:

Most criticisms of The Moral Landscape seem to stumble over its subtitle, “How Science Can Determine Human Values,” and I admit that this wording has become an albatross.— Sam Harris, Clarifying the Landscape, 2014-06

Second, the meaning of "science": he meant "our best effort to understand reality at every level," plumbers tracing a leak included; readers who grant that moral truths exist but won't call finding them science are making "a semantic choice that has no bearing on my argument." On that wide definition he can accept most of what Appiah and Blackford concluded. On the narrow one the talk used, the core of the criticism was never answered.

The empirical question from the stage did get an answer, just not from brain scans. A 2016 meta-analysis by Elizabeth Gershoff and Andrew Grogan-Kaylor pooled five decades of research on more than 160,000 children: parental spanking was significantly linked to 13 of 17 outcomes, all in the harmful direction, and not to better compliance. As for schools, an NPR analysis in August 2026 counted 15 states that allow corporal punishment, with 30 states plus Washington, D.C. banning it outright. The answer behind the drop from 21 to 15 came from developmental psychology and legislation. That backs his claim that such questions have answers, and shows that the answering need not be done by neuroscience.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · Sewall Wright's fitness landscape: in 1932 Wright drew gene combinations as terrain with peaks and valleys, height meaning fitness. The shape is nearly the moral landscape, multiple peaks included. The difference: fitness has a clear metric, offspring left behind, and evolution can only climb locally, so getting stuck on low peaks is normal. Harris's landscape lacks an agreed altimeter and never says how we would know we are standing on a low peak.
  • Buddhism · suffering and its cessation: the Four Noble Truths also ground ethics in the quality of experience. The difference: Buddhism aims to end craving itself, not to maximize well-being, and its verification is introspective, not external measurement.
  • Philosophy · G. E. Moore's open question argument: in Principia Ethica (1903), whatever natural property you define "good" as, a question like "it promotes well-being, but is it good?" still makes sense. It aims straight at Harris's step of equating the good with well-being.
  • Economics · Arrow's impossibility theorem: in 1951 Kenneth Arrow proved that no voting rule can aggregate individual rankings into a collective ranking while meeting a few minimal fairness conditions. It is the mathematical form of Carroll's aggregation objection; the difference is that it concerns ordinal rankings, and cardinally measurable well-being would change the premises.
  • Distributed systems · Byzantine fault tolerance: Lamport and colleagues showed in 1982 that consensus in the presence of traitors requires more than three times as many nodes as traitors. The difference: the protocol never identifies bad nodes by judging whose content is right, whereas Harris's convergence needs to rule the Taliban wrong before excluding them.

Ten takeaways

  1. At TED2010 Sam Harris argued that values are facts about the well-being of conscious creatures, so science can in principle answer moral questions.
  2. We owe nothing to rocks because rocks cannot suffer; how much moral concern a creature gets rests on a factual claim that could be wrong.
  3. The "moral landscape" pictures well-being as terrain: there may be many peaks, but far more ways to miss them.
  4. The health, food and chess analogies answer three objections: an open concept is not empty, many answers is not no answer, exceptions do not mean no objectivity.
  5. The "Ted Bundy of string theory" joke makes the point that factual domains exclude uninformed opinion, and morality should admit expertise too.
  6. Harris's own fMRI work found the same brain region tracking belief in religious and ordinary statements, which says nothing about whether values are facts.
  7. The book's cornerstone is the "worst possible misery for everyone" argument: once there is an agreed floor, "better" and "worse" have meaning.
  8. Sean Carroll, Kwame Anthony Appiah and Russell Blackford converged on one rebuttal: "well-being is the good" is a premise put in, not a scientific finding.
  9. A $20,000 challenge drew over 400 essays; answering the winner, Harris called his subtitle an albatross and widened "science" to all rational inquiry.
  10. The paddling question later got an empirical answer: spanking harms and does not improve compliance, and paddling states fell from 21 to 15.
