TED · 2008

She watched her own left hemisphere go quiet, and her first thought was: this is so cool

Jill Bolte Taylor · My stroke of insight

The woman on stage says: "I have brought for you a real human brain." Then she actually lifts it out of the tray, spinal cord dangling.

The official transcript records one line here: (Groaning, laughter) — the gasp comes first, the laugh second. She holds it for a while, explaining how the two hemispheres sit apart, and then an assistant comes up and carries the tray away. "Excuse me. Thank you. It's been a joy," she says. The assistant answers: "It has been."

That is the register of the whole talk: something horrifying, delivered brightly.

On the morning of December 10, 1996, Jill Bolte Taylor, a thirty-seven-year-old brain scientist, woke with a pain behind her left eye — "the kind of caustic pain that you get when you bite into ice cream." She climbed onto her exercise machine as usual and noticed her hands "look like primitive claws grasping onto the bar." In the bathroom she lost her balance, propped herself against the wall, looked down at her arm and could no longer find the edge of her own body: the atoms of her arm had blended with the atoms of the wall.

Then comes the line the talk is remembered for. Her left-hemisphere brain chatter went silent, "just like someone took a remote control and pushed the mute button."

She says it was beautiful there. The job gone, the stress gone, thirty-seven years of emotional baggage cleared in one stroke. She named the place La La Land.

What follows is the funniest and most frightening stretch of the talk. Her right arm goes completely limp, she realises she is having a stroke, and the sentence that surfaces is: "Wow! This is so cool!" — how many brain scientists get to study their own brain from the inside out? The next one: "But I'm a very busy woman! I don't have time for a stroke!"

The phone call for help took her forty-five minutes. She could not read her own business card, only pixels. So she matched the shape of the squiggles on the card to the shape of the squiggles on the phone pad, one at a time. She kept drifting out and coming back with no memory of which digits she had pressed, so she used her paralysed arm like a stump to cover the numbers she had already dialled. When the call connected, her colleague said "Woo woo woo woo," and she thought: he sounds like a Golden Retriever. She said, clearly in her mind, "This is Jill! I need help!" What came out was "Woo woo woo woo woo."

Through all forty-five minutes, the haemorrhage in her left hemisphere kept growing.

She lived. Two and a half weeks later surgeons removed a golf-ball-sized clot; eight years later she had fully recovered. And the conclusion she carried out of it is what the talk is really selling: that voice in the left hemisphere saying "I am, I am" is the wall between you and the world, while the right hemisphere lives in the present and feels connected to everything. So people can "purposely choose to step to the right of their left hemispheres" and find that peace.

That sentence is why thirty million people have watched this talk.

It is also the only part of it that did not hold up. A 2013 imaging study of 1,011 people put it flatly: the data "are not consistent with a whole-brain phenotype of greater 'left-brained' or greater 'right-brained' network strength across individuals." A 2014 essay in PLoS Biology said the left-brain/right-brain polarities "owe more to the power of myth than to the scientific evidence."

The sharpest blow comes from Taylor herself. In 2021 she published a new book that opens: for half a century we have been trained to believe the right hemisphere is the emotional brain and the left houses rational thinking, but it is common knowledge in neuroscience that emotional limbic tissue is evenly divided between the two hemispheres.

Her stroke was real, and her account of those forty-five minutes is still cited as a case in the peer-reviewed literature. But the left-brain/right-brain diagram she used to explain it is one she no longer uses — while the version from the stage is still sitting in thirty million memories.

The Moment

She has just finished describing how she had a stroke inside her own left hemisphere. Then she stops and says: "If you've ever seen a human brain, it's obvious that the two hemispheres are completely separate from one another. And I have brought for you a real human brain."

And then she actually picks it up.

The official TED transcript leaves a stage direction here: (Groaning, laughter). The order matters — the room gasps first and only then works out that it is allowed to laugh. It is a preserved human brain taken from a donor, spinal cord hanging off the back. She turns it in her hands: this is the front, this is the back, this is how it would be positioned inside my head.

A short while later an assistant walks on and takes the tray away. "Excuse me. Thank you. It's been a joy," she says to him. The assistant answers: "It has been."

The room laughs again.

Those forty seconds sign the contract for the whole talk: over the next eighteen minutes you will hear someone lose, step by step, the ability to read, to write, to speak and to recall her own life — and the person telling it will keep making you laugh. The brain is not a prop, it is collateral. It says that the woman on this stage understands anatomically what is about to happen, which is why she is allowed to be light about it.

The Speaker, and That Year

She studies the brain because of a brother diagnosed with schizophrenia. That is the first sentence of the talk. What she wanted to understand, she says, is why she can take her dreams, connect them to reality and make them come true, while her brother cannot connect his to a common and shared reality, so they become delusion instead.

So she moved from Indiana to Boston and joined Dr. Francine Benes's lab in the Harvard Department of Psychiatry. By day she mapped "the microcircuitry of the brain: which cells are communicating with which cells, with which chemicals, and then in what quantities." Evenings and weekends she travelled as an advocate for NAMI, the National Alliance on Mental Illness.

Hold on to the exact shape of that résumé, because it has almost nothing to do with the subject of the talk. There are three papers on PubMed carrying her name, all of them microanatomy of monoamine systems. In a 1998 Neuroreport paper she is first author: lesion the dorsal raphe nucleus of neonatal rats with a toxin, then count how much denser the dopamine fibres are in the adult prefrontal cortex. In a 1997 Synapse paper the team sectioned postmortem tissue from 15 normal controls and 10 schizophrenic cases and counted tyrosine-hydroxylase-positive fibres in layer II of the anterior cingulate. Not one of them is about the cerebral hemispheres. She is a genuine neuroanatomist; her expertise simply sits at a different scale, in a different part of the brain.

On the morning of December 10, 1996, she was thirty-seven. It took eight years to recover fully; four years after that, in 2008, Viking published her book My Stroke of Insight, 183 pages. She recorded this talk on February 27 of the same year, and it went online on March 12. As this page is written, the TED page shows 30,572,104 plays.

Which means she did not walk on stage fresh from a stroke. She is describing something that happened twelve years earlier, after she had already written a book about it and thought about it for twelve years.

Glossary

  • Arteriovenous malformation (AVM): a congenital tangle in which arteries connect straight to veins with no capillary bed in between to absorb the pressure. Eventually it can rupture. This was the actual cause of her stroke — present from birth, and a different thing entirely from the common stroke of later life.
  • Corpus callosum: the bundle of nerve fibres joining the two hemispheres, the largest bridge inside the brain.
  • Lateralization: a function being unevenly divided between left and right. Language really is strongly lateralized to the left; whether a whole person is "left-brained" is a different question.
  • Resting-state functional connectivity: you lie in a scanner doing nothing while the scan looks for brain regions whose activity rises and falls together — synchrony implying they form a network.
  • Default mode network (DMN): the set of regions most active when a person is daydreaming, thinking about themselves, or retrieving memories.
  • Neuromyth: a popular claim simplified or distorted out of a real neuroscience finding — a species that survives especially well in education.

The Talk, in Full

The structure is clean: build a frame, then throw your own body at it.

The frame. She starts from the real brain and offers an analogy: "For those of you who understand computers, our right hemisphere functions like a parallel processor, while our left hemisphere functions like a serial processor." The two talk through the corpus callosum, which she says is made of "some 300 million axonal fibers"; otherwise, she says, they are completely separate. Then comes the line that authorises everything after it:

Because they process information differently, each of our hemispheres think about different things, they care about different things, and, dare I say, they have very different personalities.— Jill Bolte Taylor, My stroke of insight, 2008

She lays the two "personalities" out. The right hemisphere is all present tense: it thinks in pictures, learns kinesthetically through the movement of the body, and takes in every sensory stream at once until it explodes into an enormous collage of what this moment looks, smells, tastes, feels and sounds like. By now she has stopped sounding like a science explainer: "I am an energy-being connected to the energy all around me through the consciousness of my right hemisphere." The left hemisphere is the opposite: linear, methodical, concerned only with past and future. It picks that collage apart into details and details about details, files them, links them to everything ever learned and projects them forward. It thinks in language — the voice reminding you to pick up bananas on the way home. But the important thing it says, she argues, is something else: "I am. I am."

And as soon as my left hemisphere says to me "I am," I become separate. I become a single solid individual, separate from the energy flow around me and separate from you.— Jill Bolte Taylor, My stroke of insight, 2008

Then she drops the hinge: "And this was the portion of my brain that I lost on the morning of my stroke." Frame built, protagonist on.

The collision. What follows is a dense first-person chronicle in which nearly every beat carries a physical object. The caustic pain behind the left eye, "the kind you get when you bite into ice cream." She climbs onto her cardio glider and sees her hands "look like primitive claws grasping onto the bar," then looks at her body: "Whoa, I'm a weird-looking thing." Crossing the living room, everything inside her has slowed way down, every step rigid and deliberate. In the bathroom she loses her balance, props herself on the wall, looks at her arm — and cannot find the boundary of her own body. The atoms and molecules of her arm have blended with those of the wall, and all she can detect is energy.

And in that moment, my left hemisphere brain chatter went totally silent. Just like someone took a remote control and pushed the mute button. Total silence.— Jill Bolte Taylor, My stroke of insight, 2008

She says she was shocked at first and then immediately captivated by the magnificence of the energy around her. With no body boundary left she felt enormous and expansive, at one with all the energy that was — "and it was beautiful there." She gives the place a name: La La Land. The talk now starts oscillating: the left hemisphere comes back online, shouts "Hey! We've got a problem!", she tenses, and then drifts back out. The job and every stress attached to it, gone. Every relationship and every stress attached to those, gone. She turns to the audience: "And imagine what it would feel like to lose 37 years of emotional baggage!" The room laughs. This is the finest piece of craft in the talk — she has told the story of a haemorrhage in progress as an enviable holiday.

Then the right arm goes completely paralysed and she knows: I'm having a stroke. And the next thought is the talk's most famous laugh — "Wow! This is so cool!" How many brain scientists have the opportunity to study their own brain from the inside out? Followed immediately by: "But I'm a very busy woman! I don't have time for a stroke!" Her plan at the time: I can't stop the stroke, so I'll do this for a week or two and then get back to my routine.

The call for help is the longest and most vivid stretch. She cannot remember her work number, only that a business card sits in her office. She pulls out a three-inch stack and cannot read the top one — "even though I could see clearly in my mind's eye what my business card looked like, I couldn't tell if this was my card or not, because all I could see were pixels." All she can do is wait for what she calls a wave of clarity, reattach to reality for a second and rule the card out. It took forty-five minutes to get one inch down that stack — "in the meantime, for 45 minutes, the hemorrhage is getting bigger in my left hemisphere." She no longer understands numbers or what a telephone is, but it is the only plan she has: phone pad here, business card there, matching the shape of the squiggles on the card to the shape of the squiggles on the pad. She keeps drifting back to La La Land and returning with no memory of which digits she pressed, so she wields her paralysed arm like a stump to cover the numbers as she goes. The call connects; her colleague says "Woo woo woo woo" and she thinks: he sounds like a Golden Retriever. She says, clear in her mind, "This is Jill! I need help!" — and what comes out is "Woo woo woo woo woo." "I didn't know that I couldn't speak or understand language until I tried."

An ambulance takes her to Massachusetts General. She curls into a fetal ball, and "just like a balloon with the last bit of air," she feels her spirit surrender — in that moment she knows she is no longer the choreographer of her life. She wakes that afternoon shocked to be alive, with every sense turned against her: light burns her brain like wildfire, sounds are so loud and chaotic she cannot pick a voice out of the background. Unable to locate her body in space, she feels enormous and expansive again, "like a genie just liberated from her bottle," her spirit soaring "like a great whale gliding through the sea of silent euphoria." Then the word: Nirvana. I found Nirvana.

The talk turns on the next sentence: "But I'm still alive! I'm still alive, and I have found Nirvana. And if I have found Nirvana and I'm still alive, then everyone who is alive can find Nirvana." That single inference is the foundation of her entire later career.

The rest is almost brusque: two and a half weeks later surgeons removed a golf-ball-sized clot pressing on her language centres; a photograph of her with her mother; "It took me eight years to completely recover." Eight years, one sentence. Then she welds frame and experience together:

We are the life-force power of the universe, with manual dexterity and two cognitive minds. And we have the power to choose, moment by moment, who and how we want to be in the world.— Jill Bolte Taylor, My stroke of insight, 2008

She demonstrates it live: right now I can step into the consciousness of my right hemisphere and be the life-force power of the universe, the fifty trillion molecular geniuses that make up my form, at one with all that is — or I can step into my left hemisphere and become a single individual, a solid, separate from the flow and separate from you: "I am Dr. Jill Bolte Taylor: intellectual, neuroanatomist." "These are the 'we' inside of me. Which would you choose? Which do you choose? And when?" And for the last line she turns TED's own slogan into dialogue: "And I thought that was an idea worth spreading."

Under the Iceberg

First thing left off the stage: this was not an ordinary stroke. In eighteen minutes what she says is "a blood vessel exploded in the left half of my brain." The actual cause was the rupture of a congenital arteriovenous malformation — a term she never uses once. The difference is large. The common stroke is a clot or an embolus blocking a vessel, mostly in older people; an AVM is an abnormal tangle present from birth, and it tends to rupture in otherwise healthy adults in their twenties and thirties. She was thirty-seven. Whether an experience grown out of that rare lesion generalises to "everyone who is alive can find Nirvana" is a jump that happens on stage while its footings stay off it.

Second: her authority is real, but it points somewhere else. When she says on stage that she dedicated her career to research into the severe mental illnesses, that is entirely true — it was research into microcircuitry. The three papers above star serotonin fibres in the rat dorsal raphe, dopamine terminals in layer II of the anterior cingulate, and postmortem slides from 15 controls and 10 patients. Several disciplines separate that work from "the two hemispheres have different personalities." Calling herself a brain scientist is honest; but an audience transfers that authority automatically onto whatever she is talking about, and on this particular subject she has no more experimental data than any well-read person in the room.

Third: that "300 million." She says the corpus callosum is made of some 300 million axonal fibres; the classic count (Tomasch, 1954) is about 180 to 200 million myelinated fibres, with the later literature stretching the range up to 300 million. She took the top of it. Not fabricated — but it is a number rounded in the direction of "two hemispheres, two independent machines," when what the corpus callosum actually demonstrates is the opposite: the two sides talking to each other without pause.

Fourth, and sharpest: in the clinical literature, emotional lateralization runs the other way. The pattern Guido Gainotti described in Cortex in 1972 (vol. 8, pp. 41–55) is that left-hemisphere-damaged patients typically show "catastrophic reactions" — collapsing, weeping, despairing in their frustrated attempts at speech — while patients with severe right-hemisphere lesions typically show an "indifference reaction": denying or not caring about their own disability, joking in a fatuous, ironic way. When Gainotti revisited the models in The Neuroscientist in 2019, his conclusion was that recent work supports an updated version of general right-hemisphere dominance for emotions of every kind.

Which means: the euphoria produced by her left-hemisphere haemorrhage runs in the minority direction, not the typical outcome for that kind of damage. That does not make her experience less real — a case is a case. It does mean the experience cannot be run backwards as evidence that the left hemisphere handles anxious separateness and the right handles joyful union.

Fifth: the academy read her case carefully, and did not read out of it what she wanted. In 2009 Alain Morin, of Mount Royal College, published an analysis of her case in Consciousness and Cognition, framing it as a sample of self-awareness deficits following loss of inner speech: with the talking voice in her head gone, her corporeal awareness, sense of individuality, retrieval of autobiographical memories and self-conscious emotions all broke down. That is a markedly different account from hers — Morin's protagonist is inner speech, not right-hemisphere energy.

The same issue carried two commentaries. Robert Mitchell, of Eastern Kentucky University, wrote that identifying inner speech with self-awareness is problematic, and that Taylor's own description of herself before, during and after the stroke "is at times confusing and difficult to comprehend conceptually"; her deficits look piecemeal rather than global, and "she describes self-awareness that exists independent of inner speech." In other words, the people who took her case most seriously could not even agree on what she had lost.

What Became of It

PARTLY HELD UP · as of 2026-09

Her first-person record held: a rare and unusually detailed case, still cited in peer-reviewed work. The diagram she used to explain it — left = the separated self, right = unified peace — did not. A large imaging study in 2013 ruled out any whole-brain left/right type, and the person who dismantled the frame in 2021 was Taylor herself. The problem is that what thirty million plays left behind is the 2008 version.

2008–2009 | the case enters the literature. The talk goes online in March, the book appears the same year. A year later Morin's analysis runs in Consciousness and Cognition alongside two commentaries (Mitchell, and Henry Schlinger of California State University). The field's handling is clear: accept the phenomenology as data, set the author's own theoretical explanation aside and start again.

2012 | it becomes one of the most stubborn neuromyths in the classroom. Dekker, Lee, Howard-Jones and Jolles surveyed 242 primary and secondary teachers with an interest in the neuroscience of learning (137 in the UK, 105 in the Netherlands) for Frontiers in Psychology. One statement to judge read: "Differences in hemispheric dominance (left brain, right brain) can help explain individual differences amongst learners." 91% of UK teachers agreed; 86% of Dutch teachers agreed — among the highest endorsement rates on the whole list. On average these teachers believed 49% of the neuromyths presented. She did not invent this idea; it is far older than she is. But her talk is the most-watched vehicle it has ever had.

2013 | a thousand and eleven brains say no. Nielsen, Zielinski, Ferguson, Lainhart and Anderson analysed resting-state functional connectivity MRI from 1,011 subjects aged 7 to 29 for PLoS ONE, carving the grey matter into 7,266 regions at 5-mm resolution and computing lateralization for every pair. The conclusion leaves no room: "our data are not consistent with a whole-brain phenotype of greater 'left-brained' or greater 'right-brained' network strength across individuals." Lateralization is a local property, not a global one.

One detail deserves to be said in her favour. Among the left-lateralized hubs the study found, alongside Broca's and Wernicke's areas, were components of the default mode network — medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate, temporoparietal junction — the very web that lights up when a person ruminates and spins an autobiography. "The voice in your head that keeps narrating 'I' leans left" is not at odds with her intuition. What is at odds is the large step she took next: treating a whole hemisphere as a personality, and then inviting people to pick one.

2013 | named directly. Robert Klitzman, a psychiatrist at Columbia, named the talk in Psychology Today, writing that "her explanations of the differences between the right and left sides of the brain are inaccurate, and promote several myths," and that simplifying the brain into binaries — with one half implicitly better than the other — ignores critical intricacies.

2014 | the review essay closes it out. Michael Corballis, in PLoS Biology: "Polarities of left and right brain are broadly invoked in art, business, education, literary theory, and culture, but owe more to the power of myth than to the scientific evidence." He also marks the real boundary of the split-brain work: it did reveal "the left to be specialized for language and the right for emotional and nonverbal functions," but the speculation that grew on top about complementary functions was "most of it exaggerated or ill-founded." On the most popular claim of all — creativity belongs to the right brain — his answer is a single sentence: "Brain imaging shows, though, that creative thought activates a widespread network, favoring neither hemisphere." With a pair of numbers attached: 95–99% of right-handers are left-brained for language, and so are about 70% of left-handers.

2021 | she takes the frame apart herself. Taylor published Whole Brain Living, proposing Four Characters — Left Thinking, Left Emotion, Right Emotion, Right Thinking — one thinking module and one emotional module in each hemisphere. The first paragraph introducing the book on her own site reads:

For half a century we have been trained to believe that our right brain hemisphere is our emotional brain, while our left brain houses our rational thinking. It is common knowledge in the world of neuroscience that our emotional limbic tissue is evenly divided between our two hemispheres, which means that each hemisphere has both an emotional module and a thinking module of cells.— Jill Bolte Taylor, introducing Whole Brain Living, 2021

She does not say "I got it wrong in 2008." But what that passage takes apart is exactly the premise the 2008 talk rests on — her line about the hemispheres having very different personalities, and the gesture thirty million people remember: stepping to the right of your left hemisphere. On her own 2021 account, the left side has emotion too and the right side thinks as well, so there is nowhere to step.

So the talk's fate forks. As a case history it has aged well: someone trained in neuroanatomy retained that much detail through four hours in which a clot was pressing on her language centres, and first-person material of that kind is genuinely scarce. As a theory about you and me it has been replaced by its own author — it is just that nobody sends a correction notice to thirty million playbacks.
