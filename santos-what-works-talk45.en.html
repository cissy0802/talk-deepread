Yale / Coursera · 2018 · short course 3/3

Kindness, company, sleep and WOOP: the direction mostly held, the effects are smaller than the lecture says, and several headline studies failed to replicate

Laurie Santos · The Science of Well-Being (3/3: what actually works)

At the last Q&A of the happiness course, a student says his sleep app shows he averages five hours a night. He knows he should get seven. But at one in the morning there is still reading to finish and an email to send, and going to bed with those undone makes him anxious. Getting seven hours, he says, would mean turning his whole life over, and the thought alone is daunting. Laurie Santos does not reassure him. Yes, she says, that's the point: this is meant to be a "big life shift."

These are the last two lectures of Yale's happiness course (the previous issue covered how the mind tricks us into wanting the wrong things). This time she lists the things we don't want but should, because they really do make us happier. Pick work that uses your "signature strengths" and puts you in "flow." Do kind things: spending five dollars on someone else beats spending it on yourself, and twenty dollars is no better than five. Talk to strangers: Chicago commuters told to chat with the person next to them got off the train in the best mood, though everyone had predicted the chat would be worst. Value time over money. Meditate, because we spend nearly half our waking time with our minds elsewhere, and a wandering mind is usually less happy. Exercise and sleep, which she sells as a free, legal, side-effect-free "happy pill."

The final lecture returns to the course's old problem: knowing isn't doing. She offers two tools. Change your environment (move the candy jar farther away and you eat less). And Gabriele Oettingen's WOOP: Wish, best Outcome, inner Obstacle, an if-then Plan. The obstacle must come after the outcome.

Check the studies against the papers and the direction mostly holds, but the lecture is often fuller than the paper. The first "spending on others makes you happier" experiment had 46 people; in Dunn's own large preregistered replication in 2020, one test held, one failed and one found a small effect. Her Uganda study was university students recalling a purchase, not money handed out in a village to buy medicine. The exercise group's ninety percent "recovered" is really 8 percent relapse among those who had already remitted. Eight weeks of meditation growing gray matter did not replicate in a 2022 trial of more than two hundred people. And Brian Wansink, whom she cites on environment design, was found by Cornell in 2018 to have committed academic misconduct and resigned.

So the verdict is partly held. Kindness, social connection, exercise and WOOP all have meta-analyses behind them, but the effects run from 0.2 to 0.35 standard deviations: a nudge, not a cure. Santos was right to tell that student to rearrange his life. The research just promises less than she did in the lecture.

The moment

In the last Q&A, a student asks two things. The first is about motivational posters: two weeks after you put one up you stop seeing it, so do you have to keep buying new ones? The second is about sleep. His sleep app says he averages five hours a night:

I know that I should be getting seven hours, but I I can't I feel really anxious if I like go to bed and just ignore all that.— a student, The Science of Well-Being, video 31

Doing it, he says, would mean a total overhaul of his life, and the idea is daunting. Someone laughs: "spoken like a second semester senior." Santos's answer does not hedge. You're right, it is an overhaul. What the course has been recommending (time affluence, half an hour a day thinking about nothing but your breath, hanging out with friends) is supposed to crowd out grades and résumés: "this is meant to be a big life shift, right?" That line is where the whole course lands. A dozen lectures have shown that we want the wrong things; here she asks students to actually take their hours back from them.

The speaker, and the year

Same living room, same lecturer. As the first issue explained, Laurie Santos studies cognition in monkeys and dogs and was head of Yale's Silliman residential college. This course began as a pilot recorded in her own living room in front of students from her college, then became the most-enrolled course in Yale's history and a public Coursera course.

These are the pilot's fourth and fifth lectures, with three interviews recorded for the online version: Elizabeth Dunn of the University of British Columbia (how to spend money happily), Nicholas Epley of the University of Chicago (how we misread other minds) and Gabriele Oettingen of New York University (the inventor of WOOP). The homework is a public pledge to practice one habit that week, and Santos is also running college-wide "wellness weeks." Small clues date the lectures: the students went skiing the day before, it is midterm season, and Dunn and her friends are sponsoring a Syrian refugee family settling in Canada.

The sources are more varied than in the previous two issues. Those came almost entirely from one circle, Gilbert and Wilson. This time it is the whole positive-psychology toolkit: Seligman's strengths, Csikszentmihalyi's flow, Lyubomirsky's kindness exercises, Dunn and Norton's spending experiments, Killingsworth and Gilbert on mind wandering, Oettingen and Gollwitzer on goals, plus sleep and exercise medicine, and a food-marketing researcher who was then at the height of his fame, Brian Wansink.

Glossary

  • Signature strengths: from Seligman's 24 "character strengths," the few a person scores highest on and feels are most essentially their own.
  • Flow: Csikszentmihalyi's term for absorbed, time-losing engagement, which appears when both challenge and skill are high and matched.
  • Prosocial spending: spending money on other people, as gifts or donations.
  • Time affluence: the subjective sense of having enough time, as opposed to material affluence.
  • Default mode network: a set of brain regions that becomes active when we are not doing a task, linked to remembering the past, imagining the future and thinking about other people.
  • Mental contrasting / implementation intentions: the first means imagining a wish fulfilled, then the obstacle in the way; the second is Gollwitzer's "if X, then I will do Y" plan. Together they are called MCII; WOOP is the everyday version.

The talk, in full

The fourth lecture opens with a recap. We mostly want the wrong things (miswanting), because of the mind's "annoying features"; last week covered how to fight two of them, with savoring, gratitude and resetting reference points. The hardest one is left: the "I want this" signal the brain hands us is itself unreliable. So today's question is what we should want but don't yet. She sorts the answers into two kinds: the right part of things we already want, and things we never thought of.

Work first. Everyone needs a job, and earlier lectures showed that a higher salary buys little happiness. So what should you look for? First, whether the job lets you use your signature strengths. She lays out Marty Seligman's framework: traits that are recognized across cultures, fulfilling in themselves, don't diminish others and can be measured. There are 24 of them, such as love of learning, creativity, gratitude and love, and the students have all taken the VIA character strengths survey and received a ranking. Seligman's experiment is simple: identify your top strengths, then for one week use one of them in a new way every day. The daily change, she notes, is itself a defense against hedonic adaptation. The control group just wrote about their day. At one, three and six months, she says, the strengths group had higher happiness scores and fewer depressive symptoms. In the workplace, the more strengths people use, the more productive and satisfied they are, with positive emotion at work as the link. Another study found a sweet spot at around four of your top seven strengths, which made people most likely to see their job as a "calling" rather than a job.

The second is flow. She guesses she has pronounced Csikszentmihalyi's name right, then describes the state: total absorption, a challenge you can handle, losing track of time until you notice you haven't eaten in two hours. Low challenge means boredom; high challenge without enough skill means anxiety (Yale students live in that cell, she says, and often only feel they lack the skill); both at the max is flow. Then comes the most counterintuitive data in this section. In an experience-sampling study from the 1980s, workers were paged at random and asked how they felt. At work they more often reported high challenge, efficacy and confidence, the marks of flow; at leisure, scrolling through Netflix for example, they reported apathy and boredom. Yet the same people said at work that they would rather be at leisure, and at leisure that they wanted to keep going. Her conclusion: your homework and your job are probably not that bad, and your chosen leisure may be duller than you think.

Then the second kind, things we never thought to want. The first is kindness. She admits it sounds like "a cheesy like Hallmark card," the sort of thing your grandmother forwards on Facebook in bad fonts, but the data are solid. Very happy people want to do kind things, and do them, more than very unhappy people. There are two kinds of causal experiment. In one, people simply notice and count the kind acts they have done, without doing any more, and their happiness score rises by half a point to a point. In the other, by Sonja Lyubomirsky, people perform five random acts of kindness in a week, either spread across days or all on one day, while a control group keeps a diary. The control group's happiness drops, the spread-out group barely changes, and the one-day group rises most.

Elizabeth Dunn and Mike Norton (co-authors of Happy Money) stopped people on campus, measured their happiness, and handed them an envelope with five or twenty dollars to spend by five that afternoon, half on themselves and half on someone else. Called that evening, the people who spent on others were happier, and the amount made no difference. People asked to predict got both wrong: they expected spending on themselves, and spending twenty dollars, to feel better. Santos rates the result highly:

And this is one of the most robust effects I think in the field of psychology right now.— Laurie Santos, video 20

She adds a cross-cultural follow-up: the same experiment in a rural town in Uganda, where twenty dollars could buy a family's HIV medication for a month, produced an effect of the same size as in Canada. So kindness, including kindness with money, is something we don't realize will make us happy, and it does.

In her interview, Dunn adds two qualifications that the lecture leaves out. First, in her own words: "So, that study was pretty small." Much smaller than the studies she runs now, too small to detect any difference between five and twenty dollars. Second, giving to charity does not automatically make people happier; it works best when you feel connected to others and can see the impact, which is how she and her friends chose to sponsor a refugee family. She also warns against compressing her work into "money can't buy happiness":

Money can buy happiness like people who have more money are happier.— Elizabeth Dunn, video 21

Time matters just as much; her own use of the research is paying a house cleaner to eliminate the worst part of her week.

The second is social connection. Santos reads from Yale student Marina Keegan's essay "The Opposite of Loneliness": before graduating, what she feared losing most was not the right job, city or spouse but that web of people. Data from Ed Diener and Seligman agree: very happy people have more close friends and spend less time alone. Then comes Nick Epley's experiment, which Epley describes himself in the interview. Riding the commuter train every morning from Chicago's South Side to Hyde Park, he watched passengers sit next to people from their own neighborhood for 45 minutes and "treat each other like a lampshade." He gave commuters a five-dollar Starbucks gift card and randomly assigned them to talk to their neighbor, enjoy their solitude, or do whatever they normally do. Afterwards, the talkers were in the best mood, the solitude group the worst, and productivity didn't differ. A separate group only predicted, and predicted the opposite:

So, not only were their expectations miscalibrated, they were precisely backwards— Nicholas Epley, video 23

Why the mistake? People don't think the conversation itself will be unpleasant; they think the other person won't want to talk. In a waiting-room version, the person being talked to also ended up happier; in a museum lobby, simply making eye contact and smiling at passers-by lifted people's mood. He also tells a story about Chicago's Metra commuter rail. When he told a Metra official his results, she said she believed them completely, and then said Metra was about to add quiet cars to every line, "because we asked people what they wanted, and this is what they said they wanted." Metra did once have chatty bar cars, she said, but they were dropped because they got too crowded.

The mechanism Santos offers: just having someone else there makes an experience richer. In a study by Erica Boothby and Margaret Clark, eating the same chocolate silently at the same time as a stranger raised people's liking ratings by almost a point.

The third is time affluence. A Google image search for "affluence" brings up nothing but rich people and a golden hamburger. Psychologists also talk about another kind of wealth: having time. People who say they are more like Tina, who would give up money for free time, are happier than those more like Maggie, who would work more hours to earn more. Cassie Mogilner's café study supplies a mechanism. People first did a word-unscrambling task with words about either time or money, then were secretly observed in the café. Those primed with time spent almost twice as long socializing as those primed with money, who mostly worked; on the way out, the time group was almost half a point happier than controls on a five-point scale. Part of why valuing time makes people happier, she concludes, is that they spend the extra time with other people.

The fourth she calls "mind control," meaning control of your own mind, not other people's. Killingsworth and Gilbert, using smartphone experience sampling, found that people's minds wander 46.9 percent of the time, in almost every activity except sex, and that unless the wandering goes somewhere pleasant, people are less happy than when focused. She paraphrases Gilbert: the ability to think about what isn't happening is a cognitive achievement that comes at an emotional cost. Then a bit of neuroscience. Within a fraction of a second of stopping a task, the brain switches to a "default mode network," the same regions used to recall the past, imagine the future and guess what others think. That is what lets humans leave the present:

We can be proud that we're like the only species that gets out of our own heads.— Laurie Santos, video 25

The price is unhappiness. The remedy is meditation, which she defines broadly: turning attention from distracting thoughts to a single focus, whether the breath, body sensations or compassion. The evidence comes in a stream. Yale's Hedy Kober and colleagues scanned meditators with ten thousand-plus hours of practice and found key default-network regions quieter during meditation, and at rest as well. Barbara Fredrickson's loving-kindness intervention raised positive emotion week by week. A Harvard study reported increased gray matter after an eight-week mindfulness-based stress reduction course (MBSR, about half an hour a day). Two weeks of mindfulness classes raised GRE scores. A Stanford study found that a few minutes of loving-kindness toward one stranger made people feel closer even to other strangers. If you add only one practice, she says, meditation is a good choice. In the Q&A she adds that the type barely matters as long as it keeps your mind in the present and you do it daily; she finds breath counting boring, prefers choiceless noticing, and likes loving-kindness because it is "cheesy in the right way."

The fifth is exercise and sleep, which she pitches in an infomercial voice as a "happy pill": happier, better grades, better looks, no side effects, legal and free. For exercise she cites Babyak and colleagues: patients with major depression were assigned to exercise (30 minutes, three times a week), the antidepressant sertraline (Zoloft), or both. She says that at ten months about 90 percent of the exercise group had recovered, versus just over half on medication, and that exercise alone even beat the combination. For sleep, three studies. Dinges and colleagues restricted people to about five hours a night for several nights, and their mood fell while their physical and emotional complaints soared. Walker and colleagues found that a night's sleep improved a motor skill far more than the same time awake. Wagner and colleagues found that people who had slept were more likely to discover a hidden rule in a task, more than three times as likely, she says. Then she reads a list of what short sleep does to you, and concludes:

All the reasons you guys took this course, you can achieve just by sleeping a couple extra hours a night.— Laurie Santos, video 26

In the Q&A, asked what happens when a stranger snubs you, she says it happens far less than we imagine; introverts can start small, say by asking a dining-hall worker how their day is going.

The fifth lecture returns to the course's old problem: knowing doesn't help. Out comes the "G.I. Joe fallacy" again:

We think that knowing is half the battle, but it's not.— Laurie Santos, video 28

The first tool is changing the situation. Here she cites Brian Wansink of Cornell's Food Lab, who in Slim by Design "makes a strong case that willpower is completely overrated." Secretaries ate about 48 percent more candy when the jar was on their desk than two meters away, and 25 percent less when it was in a drawer; people with cereal, chips and soda visible on their kitchen counters weighed about ten kilograms more. So move temptations away and out of sight, put good cues in view (a gratitude journal by the bed, a meditation reminder on the phone), and recruit your suitemates to sleep early and meditate with you.

The second tool is setting goals differently, in three steps. Make the goal specific down to time, place and count, because specificity forces you to work out how. Imagining the outcome isn't enough; spend as long on the obstacles, which is mental contrasting. Picture only the summit and you sit there content; picture only the climb and you never leave home. In a study of women trying to eat more fruit and vegetables, the mental-contrasting group was still eating more two years later. Third, Peter Gollwitzer's implementation intentions: if I see the pizza in the dining hall, I'll turn and grab the orange; when I grab the doorknob to leave, think keys. It sounds silly, but it works on our automatic responses. For hard goals made over winter break, students with an if-then plan were about three times as likely to succeed. Put the pieces together and you get Oettingen's WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan. She cites Angela Duckworth and colleagues, who taught it to disadvantaged fifth graders to use each morning: average grades rose from C+ to B-, conduct improved and absences fell.

In her interview, Oettingen describes WOOP as a tool "like, you know, a bicycle or a hammer." You need five quiet, uninterrupted minutes. Name your most important wish in three or four words; imagine the best outcome and let your mind drift; then "switch gears" and ask what in you stands in the way, with no excuses, digging deeper; finally form the plan "if obstacle then I will action." It can also tell you to let a wish go:

So it's really a cleaning up tool.— Gabriele Oettingen, video 30

The order matters, "because our research shows that once you switch the O's it doesn't work anymore": think first about Saturday's party and you think about what to wear and who will be there; think first about Wednesday's exam and the party reveals itself as the obstacle. In the last Q&A, Santos comes back to the five-hour student with a final point: if there isn't enough time, take a knife to some extracurriculars.

Like there just isn't enough time in life to do all the cool things that there are to do at Yale.— Laurie Santos, video 31

Beneath the surface

"One of the most robust effects" started with 46 people. Dunn, Aknin and Norton's 2008 Science paper had three parts: a national survey of 632 Americans (the more of their income people spent on others, the happier they were), 16 employees who received a bonus, and the envelope experiment. The experiment had N = 46 in a 2 × 2 design (five or twenty dollars × self or others), with an effect size of ηp² = 0.10. A further 109 students made predictions: 69 thought personal spending would make them happier, and 94 thought twenty dollars would beat five. This is the study Dunn calls "pretty small."

The Uganda study didn't hand out money for medicine. Santos describes giving people in a rural town a meaningful sum, twenty dollars that could buy a family's HIV medication. In the original (Aknin and colleagues, an NBER working paper in 2010, published in JPSP in 2013), this experiment had students at Mbarara University and Makerere University, alongside Canadian students, randomly asked to recall a time they spent ten thousand Ugandan shillings (or twenty Canadian dollars) on themselves or on others, and then rate their happiness. The effect was d = 0.20, in the same direction in both countries. Almost 20 percent of the Ugandan students' prosocial memories were purchases made in response to a negative event, which may be where the "medicine" detail comes from. The cross-cultural consistency is real, but it was a memory task, not a lifesaving sum handed over to spend.

The exercise group's ninety percent uses the wrong denominator. Babyak and colleagues' 2000 study enrolled 156 patients with major depression aged 50 and over, and treatment lasted four months. At four months the three groups had improved about equally. The difference came in the next six months: among patients who had remitted, 8 percent of the exercise group relapsed, against 38 percent on medication and 31 percent on both. Ninety percent is roughly 100 minus 8, but only among those who had remitted. Patients were recruited through advertisements and were already interested in exercise, and the authors could not explain why the combination did worse than exercise alone.

The sleep and meditation numbers are a little inflated too. The abstract of Wagner and colleagues' 2004 Nature paper says "more than twice as many" subjects found the hidden rule after sleep, not more than three times. The meditation study Kober co-authored (Brewer and colleagues, PNAS 2011) compared 12 meditators with 12 controls; the meditators averaged about 10,565 hours of practice, and the authors themselves call the sample "moderately small" and in need of replication. The gray-matter study (Hölzel and colleagues, 2011) had 16 people in the course and 17 on a waitlist, practicing about 27 minutes a day on average.

The time-affluence segment contradicts itself. Santos first says more people in the survey valued time, "about 70% 30," then, in the next breath, that over two-thirds value money over time. Hershfield, Mogilner and Barnea's 2016 paper, pooling five studies and 4,415 people, found that 64 percent chose money; only the sample asked in person at a train station mostly chose time.

Most of it is still about bad predictions. The spending experiment, the train experiment and the flow pager study share a structure: people predict A will beat B, and B beats A. This continues the previous two issues. Santos's whole course is really one argument: affective forecasting is unreliable, so arrange your life by the data, not by intuition. This issue pushes that argument furthest. It doesn't stop at saying the things you want won't help; it hands you a list of what you should want instead. How reliable that list is depends on the weight of each study behind it.

What happened next

Partly held · as of 2026-10

Kindness, social connection, exercise and WOOP all point the right way, backed by meta-analyses or large studies, but the effects are 0.2 to 0.35 standard deviations, far smaller than the lecture's tone; the prosocial-spending effect comes and goes in preregistered replications; eight weeks of meditation changing brain structure did not replicate; and Wansink, the main source for the environment segment, was found to have committed misconduct.

From 2014, the stranger studies held. Epley and Juliana Schroeder published "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude" in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General in 2014, and Epley says trains, buses, cabs and waiting rooms gave consistent results. In 2022 Gillian Sandstrom, Boothby and Gus Cooney had 286 students at a US and a UK university do a five-day "scavenger hunt"; the group told to talk to strangers reported less awkwardness, more confidence and less expected rejection, effects that lasted at least a week. This is the most solid item in this issue.

2018: Wansink was found to have committed misconduct. Outside researchers found extensive data and statistical problems in his papers. In September 2018 the JAMA journals retracted six of his papers at once, bringing his total to 13; Cornell found misconduct (misreported data, problematic statistics, failure to preserve records), and he announced his resignation. I found no retraction or correction notice for the candy-jar and kitchen-counter studies Santos cites, but they come from the same lab, and the kitchen study was correlational to begin with: "people with cereal out weigh ten kilograms more" is not "cereal makes you ten kilograms heavier." There is independent evidence that situations shape behavior, but this segment's headline evidence can no longer serve as evidence.

2018: the size of the kindness effect. Curry and colleagues meta-analyzed 27 studies of whether performing kind acts makes the actor happier. The overall effect was δ = 0.28, small to medium; Oxford's press release put it at less than one point on a 0–10 happiness scale. The direction is right, but it is milder than "much more happiness bang for your buck."

2020: the preregistered replication of prosocial spending. Aknin, Dunn and colleagues published three high-powered preregistered replications in JPSP. With 712 students buying something for a stranger in need, the effect held. With 1,950 adults recalling a purchase, it did not. With the procedure changed to raise engagement and 5,199 participants, it held but the differences were small. The authors conclude the effect is real but its size depends on method. That lands squarely between Santos's "one of the most robust" and Dunn's own "pretty small."

2021: a meta-analysis of WOOP. Wang, Wang and Gai pooled 24 studies and 15,907 people and found an overall MCII effect of g = 0.336, small to medium; they warn of publication bias, so the true effect may be smaller, and interventions delivered in person (0.465) beat those delivered on paper (0.277). Oettingen says almost everyone can use it, and the data say it works, but as a small, steady push.

2022: eight weeks of meditation did not change brain structure. Tammi Kral and colleagues in Richard Davidson's group at the University of Wisconsin pooled two randomized trials in Science Advances: more than two hundred healthy adults with no meditation experience, randomly assigned to MBSR, a matched "health enhancement" program (exercise, music, nutrition) or a waitlist. There were no structural differences between groups, including in regions earlier MBSR studies had reported, and the MBSR group's self-reported gains in mindfulness also appeared in the health program. The authors note that longer practice might change structure, which their study cannot rule out. So the evidence that meditation calms the mind and reduces wandering stands; "eight weeks grows gray matter" should not be repeated.

Exercise holds up best. A 2024 network meta-analysis by Noetel and colleagues in The BMJ (later corrected once) found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training effective against depression, more so at higher intensity, though the authors rated most of the evidence low or very low in certainty. That exercise helps with depression is now mainstream; that it beats medication is not a consensus.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · mettā: the loving-kindness meditation in Santos's lecture and the Stanford study comes from one of Theravada Buddhism's four "immeasurables": wishing "may you be safe and happy" first to yourself, then to loved ones, strangers and enemies. The Stanford finding that practicing toward one stranger brings others closer too matches the tradition's step-by-step widening toward all beings. Unlike in purpose: in Buddhism mettā loosens the line between self and other, with happiness as a by-product; the course uses it to raise one's own happiness score, which puts it back inside the self.
  • Daoism · Cook Ding: the butcher in the Zhuangzi meets the ox "with spirit, not with the eyes," and his blade stays sharp for nineteen years, much like flow: skill and difficulty interlock and the self steps back. Unlike in that Csikszentmihalyi wants the challenge raised step by step, while Zhuangzi wants less force, following the grain, and at a hard knot he slows down.
  • Evolutionary biology · Trivers's reciprocal altruism: Robert Trivers showed in 1971 that helping non-relatives can be favored by selection over repeated encounters. The warm glow of kindness may be that mechanism's emotional reward. Unlike in level: Trivers explains why the tendency evolved, not how helping feels now, and Dunn's participants felt happier helping strangers who would never repay them.
  • Stoicism · Seneca's On the Shortness of Life: Seneca says people guard their money but give their time away freely; life isn't short, we waste it. That fits time affluence. Unlike in where the time goes: Seneca wants it reclaimed for philosophy and oneself, while Mogilner's data say extra time pays off when spent with others.
  • Computing · event-driven programming: an implementation intention is like registering an event handler in the brain: when the trigger "see pizza" fires, the callback "grab the orange" runs, with no fresh decision from the main program. Unlike in that human handlers decay and need re-registering by doing WOOP again.

Ten takeaways

  1. When choosing work, look past salary to whether it uses your top character strengths and can put you in flow.
  2. Experience-sampling studies find people are in flow more often at work than at leisure, yet keep saying they would rather be at leisure.
  3. Doing five kind acts in a single day raises the doer's happiness more than spreading them across a week.
  4. In Dunn's envelope experiment, people who spent the money on others were happier that evening, and five dollars worked as well as twenty.
  5. Commuters predict that talking to a stranger will be the worst option; those who actually talk are happiest, and so are the people they talk to.
  6. People who value time over money are happier, partly because they spend the extra time socializing.
  7. Minds wander about 47 percent of the time, and unless the wandering is pleasant, people are less happy than when focused.
  8. WOOP runs through the wish, the best outcome, the inner obstacle and an if-then plan, and the outcome must come before the obstacle.
  9. The spending-on-others effect came and went in a 2020 preregistered replication, and "eight weeks of meditation adds gray matter" failed to replicate in a 2022 trial of over two hundred people.
  10. Meta-analyses put kindness interventions and WOOP at about 0.3 standard deviations: useful, but nothing like the "happy pill" of the lecture.
