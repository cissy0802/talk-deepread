Procrastination is a small pain, sleep is a brain wash: the methods held up, the brain pictures behind them are wobbling

Barbara Oakley · Learning How to Learn (3/3: Procrastination, Memory and Sleep)

The slide shows a few small circles standing for brain cells, with specks floating in the gaps between them: metabolic waste piled up during the waking day. Barbara Oakley clicks the remote. The circles shrink, the gaps widen, fluid rushes through and carries the specks away. She clicks again, because, she says, she just has so much fun doing this. The Google engineers in the room laugh.

This is the last part of Learning How to Learn. The first two parts covered how the brain thinks and how it packs knowledge into chunks. This one is about time: how things move from working memory into long-term memory, why you keep putting things off, and why the all-nighter before an exam is a losing trade.

Procrastination first. Her account: when you look at something you would rather not do, the part of your brain that processes pain lights up. You have two options. Push through, and the pain soon fades. Or turn your attention to something else, and you feel better right away. Doing that once is no big deal. Doing it as a habit starts to look like an addiction. Her remedy is the Pomodoro Technique: set a timer for 25 minutes, turn off every notification, work with focus, then reward yourself with a few minutes of fun. The trick is to watch the clock, not the task. You do not promise to finish your homework in those 25 minutes. You promise to focus for 25 minutes.

Then memory. Working memory is a rather poor blackboard; long-term memory is a vast warehouse. Getting things into the warehouse takes practice, and spaced practice at that: repeating something 20 times in one evening works far worse than the same number of repetitions spread over several days. Her image is a brick wall, where each layer of mortar needs time to set. Part of that setting time is sleep. Citing a mouse study, she says that when you learn something and then sleep, new synaptic connections form. So do not cram; learn a little, sleep, learn a little more. Exercise goes on the list too, because running helps new neurons in the hippocampus survive.

She closes by turning to people who think they are not good enough. If your working memory is poor, other ideas keep slipping in, so you are more creative. If you think slowly, you are a hiker in the woods who sees the rabbit trails the race car driver misses. Feel like an impostor? The feeling is so common that knowing how common it is is the best cure.

What happened to all this? The methods are solid. Spacing is backed by a meta-analysis of 317 experiments. Taking breaks on a fixed schedule, tested in 2023 with 87 university students, left them less tired than breaking whenever they liked, with the same amount of work done. The brain science she attached to the methods is wobbling, though. The pain centers come from a 28-person brain-imaging study of math anxiety that measured brain activity, not pain. The sleep animation was challenged head-on in 2024 by a mouse study from another lab that found clearance slows during sleep, and the two sides are still arguing. Whether adults grow new neurons at all is unsettled, and when randomized trials of exercise and cognition are pooled and corrected for publication bias, the effect shrinks to almost nothing. As for the claim that a poor working memory makes you more creative, we could not find the study she had in mind. So the verdict is partly holds: following the advice is fine, but several bricks in the "why" have come loose.

Course Learning How to Learn (Coursera, with Terrence Sejnowski) · This part Procrastination, memory and sleep · Year 2014 · Texts Talks at Google, third-party transcript (second half) + TEDxOaklandUniversity subtitles (Amara) + auto-captions of four course videos

The moment

The slide shows a row of small circles, which Oakley says are brain cells. While you are awake, metabolites come out of the cells and sit in the junctions between them; they are, she says, "essentially toxins in your brain," which is why the longer you stay up, the foggier you get. Then she asks everyone to watch very carefully and clicks the remote. The circles shrink, the gaps open up, fluid washes past and the specks are gone.

You go to sleep, they shrink. I’ll do that again, because I just have so much fun doing this.— Barbara Oakley, Talks at Google

And she plays it again. The animation illustrates a 2013 mouse study in Science that was fresh and much talked about at the time. In this part of the course it plays two roles. It is Oakley's most vivid reason not to stay up late or cram. A decade later, it is also the most contested brick in the whole part. Both stories need telling.

The speaker, and the year

Barbara Oakley's story was told in the first two parts: she fell off the math bandwagon in third grade, joined the army to learn Russian, worked as a translator on Soviet trawlers in the Bering Sea, left the military at 26, rebuilt her brain to study engineering, and became an engineering professor at Oakland University. In 2014 she and the Salk Institute neuroscientist Terrence Sejnowski launched Learning How to Learn on Coursera. Part 1 covered focused and diffuse thinking; part 2 covered chunking and illusions of competence.

This part collects the course's material on time: procrastination, memory, sleep, and a few words for slow learners and people with poor memories. One detail is worth keeping in mind from the start. The mouse paper she uses on stage to show that exercise helps learning lists Sejnowski among its authors. Some of the neuroscience in this course is its co-teacher's own research. This page draws on four texts: the second half of her long talk at Google (procrastination, Pomodoro, sleep, exercise, spaced repetition, slow thinkers, impostor feelings); the TEDx passages on procrastination and slow thinking; two course videos on memory and sleep; and two interview-style videos, one with Sejnowski on neuromodulators and one in which Oakley interviews the self-learner Scott Young. We have no text for the course's videos on habits, so this page does not cover them.

Glossary

  • Working memory / long-term memory: the first is the handful of things you are holding in mind right now, small and quick to fade; the second is knowledge and skill stored for years, huge, but you have to practice to find things in it.
  • Spaced repetition: spreading the same number of practice sessions over days or longer instead of doing them all at once.
  • Pomodoro Technique: setting a timer for about 25 minutes of focused work, then taking a few minutes off. Pomodoro is Italian for tomato.
  • Glymphatic system: a hypothesis proposed around 2012 that cerebrospinal fluid flows through brain tissue and flushes out metabolic waste, more so during sleep.
  • Adult neurogenesis: the birth of new neurons in the adult brain. Clear in the mouse hippocampus, disputed in humans.
  • Reverse inference: seeing a brain region light up and concluding that the person is experiencing whatever that region "usually" does. Most regions do many jobs, so this step is shaky.

The talk, in full

At Google, she comes at procrastination by anticipating an objection. Having explained that you need to switch back and forth between focused and diffuse modes, and that this takes time, she says she knows what you are really thinking: that you are a procrastinator and do not have time. She adds a generous aside: you can be a really effective human being and still procrastinate about some things. So it is not a character flaw; it is something you can learn about.

Her explanation of procrastination is the most vivid mechanism in this part:

Studies have shown that if you look at something you don’t like, the pain centers of your brain actually activate.— Barbara Oakley, Talks at Google

Look at a textbook for a subject you dislike, she says, and you feel a twinge, and if you were in a scanner we could see it. Then there are two paths. The first is to work through it, and the pain gradually disappears. The two versions differ here: at Google she says "like 20 minutes or so"; at TEDx she says research has shown "within a few minutes" it will disappear. The second path is to turn your attention to something more pleasant, and you feel better immediately. The audience laughs. She goes on:

So, you do this once, you do this twice; it's just not that big a deal. But you do this very often, and it's actually like an addiction.— Barbara Oakley, TEDxOaklandUniversity, 2014

The chain of reasoning matters here. Procrastination is not laziness; it is short-term pain relief, and because the relief works every time, the habit is reinforced every time. That chain shapes her remedy: if the problem is the pain of starting, find a way around that moment.

The remedy is the Pomodoro Technique. At Google she gives its origin: Francesco Cirillo developed it in the 1980s, named for his tomato-shaped kitchen timer. You set it for 25 minutes (she says different lengths suit different people), turn off every alarm and instant message, work with as much focused attention as you can, and then reward yourself, by surfing the web or "whatever floats your boat." At TEDx she sets the bar even lower:

Anybody can do 25 minutes, virtually anyone.— Barbara Oakley, TEDxOaklandUniversity, 2014

She tells a small story about her own Pomodoros: partway through she wonders whether she is really focusing as hard as she can, then realizes she obviously is not, because she just got distracted wondering whether she was focusing. She lets the thought drift by and gets back to work. That, she says, is the exercise itself.

The key to the whole method, in her words:

Don’t focus on the task. Only focus on the time.— Barbara Oakley, Talks at Google

Why? Because "I must complete this task" is the source of the pain. Promise only 25 minutes of focus and you slip past it into the flow of the work. The TEDx version is more concrete: do not sit down and say you will finish your entire homework set in these 25 minutes; just say you will work with focused attention for 25 minutes. She also warns against the opposite mistake: do not decide to do 20 Pomodoros today and try to beat yourself into productivity. Ease into it.

The break is not optional. She ties it back to the thread of part 1: some learning happens while you relax, in diffuse mode, so you are working even when you are not focusing, and the Pomodoro also trains your ability to relax without guilt. Sejnowski's course video on neuromodulators adds the other half of why the reward matters. Dopamine, he explains, deals in predicting future rewards, not just immediate ones, which is what lets it push you toward something unpleasant now that pays off later; when you promise yourself a treat after a study session, he says, you are tapping into your dopamine system.

From procrastination she turns to sleep. Everyone has heard that sleep matters before a big test; she says it matters in many more ways. The first is the animation from the opening: toxins build up between cells while you are awake and cloud your judgment; in sleep the cells shrink and fluid washes the toxins out. It is housekeeping. The course video on sleep puts it more starkly:

sleep which can sometimes seem like such a waste of time is actually your brain's way of keeping itself clean and healthy— Barbara Oakley, course video "The Importance of Sleep in Learning" (auto-captions)

The video goes on: taking a test without enough sleep means thinking with a brain full of metabolic toxins, like driving a car with sugar in the gas tank, and too little sleep over too long is associated with headaches, depression, heart disease, diabetes and dying earlier.

The second way is synapses. At Google she shows a figure from what she calls a wonderful paper by Guang Yang of NYU Langone: the same neuron before learning and sleep, and after learning and sleep, with the second covered in little triangles, each a new synaptic connection. Her conclusion: when you learn something and go to sleep, that is when new connections form, so do not cram at the last minute. You want "many short learning periods, sleep, learning, sleep," building the neural scaffold bit by bit.

The course video adds two more layers. During sleep, it says, the brain tidies up what you have been learning, erasing the less important parts and strengthening what you need, and rehearses the tougher parts; sleep also makes a remarkable difference in solving hard problems, as if switching off the conscious you in the prefrontal cortex lets other regions talk to each other more easily. And there is dreaming: go over material just before sleep and you are more likely to dream about it; set it in mind that you want to dream about it and your chances rise further; and dreaming about what you are studying, she says, can substantially enhance your understanding.

Third comes exercise. She criticizes schools for cutting recess, then shows a figure from a mouse study: mice trained to tell two symbols apart, with blue dots for old neurons and red for the new neurons born every day in the hippocampus, which she says happens in all of us and is "absolutely essential" for learning and remembering. There are two ways to help them survive: new environments, which is why travel helps, and, "just as powerful as learning," exercise, even simple walking. Then she points to a name on the paper: Terrence Sejnowski, her co-teacher, who at 65 climbs down "like a mountain goat" every day or every few days to run on the beach. She is convinced, she says, that this is part of how he keeps his intellectual edge.

Then memory itself. The course video on memory lays out the frame for this part: working memory is centered in the prefrontal cortex; researchers used to think it held about seven items, but it is now widely believed to hold about four chunks. It is like a blackboard, but "not a very good blackboard": you keep repeating things so they do not fade, which is why you repeat a phone number until you can write it down, and shut your eyes to keep other things from crowding in. Long-term memory is a warehouse spread across the brain, big enough for billions of items, so big that items bury each other, which is why you cannot find things without practice.

How do you move something from the blackboard into the warehouse? At Google she bends an old saying:

Practice makes, in some sense, permanent.— Barbara Oakley, Talks at Google

Practice makes perfect becomes practice makes permanent: the more you practice, the broader and more deeply embedded the neural pathway. Skip practice, and the "metabolic vampires" from part 2 suck the pattern away: you understood the professor, you may even have had a flash of insight, but you walk away for a few days and it is gone.

Practice also needs rhythm, which is spaced repetition. At Google she suggests Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and again Friday, with new synapses growing each time you learn a little and sleep on it. The course video is more precise:

if you try to glue things into your memory by repeating something 20 times in one evening for example it won't stick nearly as well as if you practice it the same number of times over several days— Barbara Oakley, course video "Introduction to Memory" (auto-captions)

Her image is a brick wall: give the mortar time to set between layers and the wall is solid; skip that and you get a jumble. The mortar is the time synaptic connections need to form and strengthen. Sleep, spacing and the wall are, for her, one and the same thing.

With the methods laid out, she gives her last stretch to people who feel their brains are not good enough. First, poor memory. Research has shown, she says, that if your working memory is poor and your four slots are weak, other stuff keeps slipping in, which is why you cannot hold ideas well, "but because the other stuff is slipping in, you're actually more creative." She extends this to attention deficit disorder (ADHD): research has shown, she says, that a wandering attention means "much more potential for being creative," at the price of working harder than others.

Then slowness. Some people watch colleagues with race car brains and feel left behind. She brings in one of her heroes, the father of modern neuroscience, Santiago Ramón y Cajal, who said he was no genius and succeeded through hard work and persistence. In her retelling, Cajal said the race car brains often race ahead and jump to conclusions, while he could see their mistakes and was more flexible when data contradicted him. Which gives this part its best-known image:

But you’re the hiker, and your experience is completely different.— Barbara Oakley, Talks at Google

The race car driver gets there first, but you can smell the pine forest, reach out and touch the needles, see the rabbit trails they missed. The TEDx version adds the cost: you may have to work harder to grasp the material, but in many cases the trade-off is well worth it, because you gain solid mastery. At Google she also cites the Fields medalist Maryam Mirzakhani, who, she says, was told as a young person that she thought too slowly to be a mathematician. In the Q&A, someone asks how to read Nietzsche and Heidegger faster. Her answer is a flat no: research suggests speed-reading techniques are somewhat spurious, reading anything difficult deeply simply takes time, so have some compassion for yourself.

Then the impostor syndrome: the feeling that you are the fake in the room and "they're going to find out what the real truth is," felt by people at Google and by professors alike. Her remedy is a single step: know how common it is. Next time you think "I'm really not as good as they say I am," recognize it as the impostor syndrome talking.

The course video in which Oakley interviews Scott Young adds a witness for these passages. Young had worked through MIT's four-year computer science curriculum in one year on his own and was then learning four languages in a year. When people wrote to say he must have a gift for languages, he recalled that his first attempt at a foreign language, French, took about as much time as he was now spending, and he got a D. His conclusion:

being intelligent within that subject is often a factor of just how much exposure you've had to it— Scott Young, course interview video (auto-captions)

So "I'm bad at math" is not a verdict, just a sign you need more time; the better you get, the more interesting it becomes.

Both the Google and TEDx versions end on the same thought. At Google she says we are always told to follow our passion, but passion grows around what we are good at, and some things take much longer to get good at. The TEDx version is the shortest:

Don't just follow your passions; broaden your passions, and your life will be enriched beyond measure.— Barbara Oakley, TEDxOaklandUniversity, 2014

Compressed into one line: procrastination is dodging the pain of starting → the Pomodoro watches the clock, not the task, to slip past that moment, and the break counts as learning → memory needs practice, spaced out, like letting mortar set → sleep clears waste and grows new synapses along the way → exercise keeps new neurons alive → a poor memory, a slow mind and impostor feelings are not verdicts. The to-do list is short: 25 minutes of focus at a time; spread the same practice over several days; do not pull all-nighters; walk more.

Beneath the surface

The "pain centers" come from an experiment on math anxiety. She does not name it, but the match is Ian Lyons and Sian Beilock's 2012 paper in PLOS ONE, titled "When Math Hurts." Fourteen highly math-anxious and fourteen low math-anxious people lay in a scanner; a cue told them whether a math or a word problem was coming, and then they solved it. The more math-anxious they were, the stronger the activity in the posterior insula and mid-cingulate cortex when the "math is next" cue appeared, regions that often show up in pain research. Three things did not make it on stage. First, the participants were people with serious math anxiety, not ordinary people looking at anything they dislike. Second, the authors state that the link appeared only during anticipation, not while doing the math, so their claim is that anticipating math is painful, not that math itself hurts. Third, pain was never measured; it was inferred backward from brain regions, which the authors acknowledge is reverse inference, and insula activity could also reflect detecting a threat to the body. Oddly, the second point is the one that best supports her remedy: the pain comes before you start and is gone once you do. But neither "20 minutes" nor "a few minutes" appears in this paper; it never measured how long any pain lasted.

The Pomodoro itself has no experiment behind it. She is clear that it is Cirillo's 1980s invention, a productivity method, not a study. The reasons she gives on stage (watching the clock sidesteps the pain; diffuse mode works during the break) are her own assembly of mechanisms from earlier in the talk.

Sleep washing the brain: mice, and anesthesia counted too. The animation matches Lulu Xie, Maiken Nedergaard and colleagues' 2013 paper in Science. In live mice, they found that natural sleep or anesthesia was associated with a 60% increase in the space between cells, a sharp rise in exchange between cerebrospinal and interstitial fluid, and faster clearance of beta-amyloid. The on-stage "you go to sleep, they shrink" is, in the paper, "natural sleep or anesthesia," with the same effect. It is an animal experiment, not a measurement in people.

New synapses: the mouse motor cortex. The Guang Yang paper she names is Guang Yang, Wen-Biao Gan and colleagues in Science, 2014. After mice learned a motor task (running on a rotating rod) and then slept, new dendritic spines formed on a subset of branches of layer V pyramidal neurons; different tasks produced spines on different branches; neurons active during learning reactivated in the following non-REM sleep, and disrupting that reactivation stopped the new spines from forming. It is an elegant study, but it is about mice learning movements. Getting from there to "learn a little calculus, sleep, learn a little more" crosses two gaps, species and task.

Dreams and insight: two human studies, both narrower than the video. The most cited source for sleep making a "remarkable difference" to problem solving is Ullrich Wagner, Jan Born and colleagues in Nature, 2004: people practiced a number task with a hidden rule, and after 8 hours of sleep more than twice as many discovered the rule as after staying awake; without initial practice, sleep did nothing. That is exactly her point about planting the seed in focused mode first. The dreaming claim matches Erin Wamsley, Robert Stickgold and colleagues in Current Biology, 2010: people learned a virtual maze, and those who dreamed about it during an afternoon nap improved more on a retest five hours later, while thinking about the maze while awake did not predict improvement. That is a correlation, not causal evidence that dreams "enhance understanding." For "set it in mind that you want to dream about it," we found no study she could be relying on.

Exercise and new neurons: Sejnowski's own mouse paper. The paper she points to is Henriette van Praag, Brian Christie, Terrence Sejnowski and Fred Gage in PNAS, 1999: mice with a running wheel had more newborn cells in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus, did better in the water maze, and showed stronger long-term potentiation. It is a classic of animal research. "Being born every day in all of us" is the longest leap in this part; the next section covers what happened to it.

On spacing she is right, and it can be said more precisely. Nicholas Cepeda, Hal Pashler and colleagues in Psychological Bulletin, 2006, pooled 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles: spaced practice beats massed practice, with almost no dissent in the literature. What she does not mention is the pattern: the best gap depends on how long you want to remember. The longer the horizon, the wider the gap should be. "Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday" suits next week's exam, not next year.

Two studies she mentions in passing: one stronger than her claim, one narrower. On speed reading, Keith Rayner and colleagues' 2016 review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest agrees with her: speed trades off against accuracy, doubling or tripling reading speed without losing comprehension is unlikely, and the real route to faster reading is reading more and building vocabulary. In the Q&A she also says that college students who took almost no math are far more likely to default on home mortgages. The match is Kristopher Gerardi and colleagues in PNAS, 2013, but that study measured US subprime borrowers' ability to do basic calculations, not how many math courses they had taken.

What happened next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-10

The practices hold up: spaced repetition rests on hundreds of experiments, scheduled breaks now have a direct test, and speed reading really is unreliable. The mechanisms she attached to them are wobbling: sleep clearing toxins is still disputed even in mice, adult human neurogenesis is unsettled, the effect of exercise on cognition nearly vanishes after correction, and we found nothing behind the claim that a poor memory makes you more creative.

2006–2007: two cornerstones on the methods side. The spacing meta-analysis above nailed down spaced practice. A year later Piers Steel, in Psychological Bulletin, pooled 691 correlations on the causes of procrastination; among the strongest and most consistent predictors were how aversive the task is, how far off the reward is, self-efficacy and impulsiveness. That points the same way as Oakley's "it starts with dislike," but Steel describes a motivational calculation, people systematically discounting distant rewards, not a brain region lighting up.

2018: new neurons in humans, two papers in the same year, in opposite directions. Shawn Sorrells, Arturo Alvarez-Buylla and colleagues reported in Nature that new neurons in the human hippocampus drop sharply in the first year of life, with only a few isolated ones by ages 7 and 13, and none detected in 17 post-mortem samples from healthy adults or 12 surgical samples from people with epilepsy. Weeks later Maura Boldrini and colleagues reported the opposite in Cell Stem Cell: healthy people aged 14 to 79 had thousands of immature neurons in the dentate gyrus, with no decline with age. Much of the disagreement comes down to how tissue is handled and which markers are used, and it is still unresolved. Oakley's "being born every day in all of us" was the mainstream view in 2014; today it needs a question mark.

2023: the evidence that exercise makes you smarter is thinner than it looks. Luis Ciria, Daniel Sanabria and colleagues published an umbrella review in Nature Human Behaviour, restricted to randomized controlled trials. Most of the 24 meta-analyses reported a positive effect, but the authors found underpowered trials, selective inclusion and publication bias. Pooling all the primary trials gave d = 0.22; accounting for active controls and baseline differences brought it to 0.13; correcting for publication bias left 0.05, with a confidence interval crossing zero. Their advice is caution about claims that regular exercise improves cognition in healthy people until better causal evidence arrives. That does not make walking useless; it may help you wake up, sleep better, stay healthy. But the TEDx line that exercise within a few days increases our ability to learn and remember does not stand up today.

2023: the Pomodoro finally gets tested. Felicitas Biwer, Anique de Bruin and colleagues, in the British Journal of Educational Psychology, had 87 students at a Dutch university try it for one day of real self-study: one group took breaks whenever they liked, one took 6 minutes after every 24 (the researchers call it the Pomodoro), and one took 3 minutes after every 12. Those who chose their own breaks studied longer and rested longer, but were more tired and distracted, with lower concentration and motivation; the three groups completed the same amount of work. The authors conclude that fixed breaks had mood benefits and seemed to have efficiency benefits (the same work in less time). It is the first piece of advice in this part to be tested directly, and the result leans her way, though the sample is small and it ran for a single day.

2024–2025: the brain-washing animation gets pushed back. A team at Imperial College London led by Nicholas Franks and William Wisden (first author Miao) reported in Nature Neuroscience that molecules injected into mouse brain tissue moved the same way whether the animals were asleep, awake or anesthetized, and that clearance from the brain was "markedly reduced, not increased" during sleep and anesthesia. Nedergaard called the work misleading, and her group published a Matters Arising in the same journal in March 2025, arguing that molecules spreading within the brain is not the same as being flushed out of it; Franks and Wisden replied that the objections were misunderstandings. In January 2025 Nedergaard's group published new evidence in Cell that slow norepinephrine oscillations during non-REM sleep make blood vessels constrict and dilate, pumping cerebrospinal fluid into the brain. Both sides' experiments are in mice; there is no direct measurement in humans. Sleep's importance for memory is not at stake (the Yang and Wagner studies stand on their own), but the picture of cells shrinking in sleep so fluid can flush the toxins has gone from textbook illustration to open question.

The passages for people who feel inadequate: mixed evidence. ADHD and creativity: Martine Hoogman and colleagues reviewed 31 behavioral studies in Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews in 2020. Most found better divergent thinking in people with high ADHD traits who fall short of a diagnosis, but not in those with the disorder; creative achievement was high in both groups, and there was no advantage in convergent thinking. So "a wandering mind has more creative potential" is half right, and stretching it to "ADHD makes you more creative" goes too far. For poor working memory making you more creative, we could not find the study she refers to, so all we can report is that she said research shows it. Impostor syndrome: Dena Bravata and colleagues' 2020 systematic review covered 62 studies and 14,161 people; prevalence ranged from 9% to 82% depending on the scale and cutoff, it was common in men and women and across ages, and often came with depression and anxiety. "It's very common" holds up. But the same review notes that no published study has evaluated any treatment, so "knowing how common it is is the best remedy" is comfort, not a tested therapy.

We found no independent academic critique of this part of the course. Judged by whether each piece of advice has research behind it, this part has the same shape as the first two: the practices are almost all sound, while the layer that dresses them up as neuroscience often runs ahead of the evidence.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · The second arrow: in the Sallatha Sutta of the Samyutta Nikaya, the Buddha says an ordinary person struck by one arrow (bodily pain) is struck again by a second (resisting and fretting over the pain), while a trained disciple feels only the first. Oakley's procrastination mechanism is nearly the same picture: the twinge on seeing the task is the first arrow; turning away to your phone to escape it is the second, the one that drags you deeper. Where they differ is the way out: the Pomodoro sidesteps the first arrow by moving attention from the task to the timer; the Buddha's way is to face it and see it clearly, neither fleeing nor adding drama. One is an engineering detour, the other trains the mind itself.
  • Confucianism · "Learn and practice it in due time": the opening line of the Analects is often read as "review what you learn at regular times," which is spaced repetition. Zhu Xi glossed the word for practice as a young bird flapping its wings again and again, close to her "practice makes permanent." The difference lies in "time": some commentators read it as "at the proper moment," not at even intervals, and the practice Confucius meant was more the rehearsal of rites and music than memorization.
  • Machine learning · Experience replay: DeepMind's 2015 Atari-playing DQN stored past experiences in a buffer and replayed random samples during training, with the paper citing hippocampal replay as inspiration. It resembles Guang Yang's finding that neurons replay daytime activity in sleep. The difference is selection: DQN samples at random to break correlations between consecutive experiences; sleep replay is selective, favoring recent and important experiences.
  • Distributed systems · Garbage-collection pauses: runtimes like Java periodically "stop the world" to reclaim memory nobody uses, so the system can keep running for a long time. Oakley's sleep picture is exactly this model: pile up garbage by day, halt and sweep by night. The difference is what we know: what a garbage collector does in its pause is written in code; whether sleep sweeps at all, or sweeps more slowly, is exactly what has been argued about since 2024.

Ten takeaways

  1. Barbara Oakley explains procrastination as short-term pain relief: dodging an unwanted task feels better at once, and doing it often works like an addiction.
  2. The trick of the Pomodoro Technique is to watch the clock, not the task: promise 25 minutes of focus, not a finished job.
  3. The breaks in a Pomodoro count as learning, because diffuse mode keeps working in the background while you relax.
  4. The same number of practice sessions spread over several days sticks far better than one evening of repetition, and the best gap grows with how long you want to remember.
  5. The source of the "pain centers" claim is a 28-person brain-imaging study of math anxiety; the pain-related activity appeared only during anticipation, and pain was never measured.
  6. After mice learn a motor task, neurons reactivating during sleep grow new dendritic spines on specific dendritic branches.
  7. A 2024 mouse experiment found brain clearance markedly slower during sleep, directly contradicting the 2013 finding that sleep washes the brain.
  8. Whether the adult human hippocampus still makes new neurons was answered in opposite ways by two 2018 papers and remains unsettled.
  9. Restricted to randomized trials and corrected for publication bias, the effect of exercise on cognition in healthy people is close to zero.
  10. Impostor feelings are common across all kinds of people, but no published study has yet tested how to treat them.
