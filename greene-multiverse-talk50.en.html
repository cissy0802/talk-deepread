One absurdly small number, and a whole bubble bath of universes

Brian Greene · "Is our universe the only universe?"

Brian Greene was holding his four-year-old daughter, Sophia, and told her, "I love you more than anything in the universe." She turned to him and asked:

"Daddy, universe or multiverse?"— Brian Greene, TED, 2012

The room laughed. This was TED in February 2012; Greene is a string theorist at Columbia and the author of The Elegant Universe. Once the laughter died down, he set out to persuade a room full of people to take seriously the idea that our universe is one of countless many.

His way in was a single number. A few months earlier the Nobel Prize in physics had gone to two teams of astronomers who set out to measure how fast cosmic expansion was slowing down and found instead that it is speeding up, like an apple tossed upward that climbs faster and faster. The thing doing the pushing is called dark energy: a uniform, invisible "mist" that, by Einstein's equations, produces repulsive gravity. The trouble is that the amount of it is "spectacularly small," and no one can derive that number from the laws of physics.

Step one is string theory. Particles are vibrating strings, and the theory needs extra, curled-up dimensions of space whose shape dictates how strings vibrate, and therefore how much dark energy there is. But the list of candidate shapes grew from about five to roughly 10 to the 500. Some physicists gave up on string theory. Others flipped the problem: maybe every shape is real, each its own universe. Then "why this number?" is the wrong question; the right one is "why are we in this universe?" With much more dark energy, matter can't clump into galaxies; with much less, the universe collapses early. No galaxies, no us. Kepler, Greene says, spent decades trying to explain why Earth sits 93 million miles from the Sun. There's no deep answer: there are many planets, and we live on the one that is neither too hot nor too cold.

Step two is inflation. The "fuel" behind the Big Bang is almost impossible to use up, so there are many Big Bangs, and ours is "but one bubble in a grand cosmic bubble bath of universes." One test: look for marks left in the cosmic microwave background by other bubbles colliding with ours. He closes on the far future, when accelerating expansion will carry every distant galaxy out of sight and astronomers will see only static darkness. We live, he says, in an era when deep truths are still within reach.

Fourteen years on, the story has stalled halfway. The most direct test had in fact already been run a year before the talk: in 2010 a team searched WMAP satellite data for bubble collisions and found none. Alan Guth, who proposed inflation, concedes that in eternal inflation "anything that can happen will happen" infinitely many times, which makes probabilities very hard to define, and the "why are we in this one?" argument depends on probabilities. Since 2018 some string theorists have questioned whether string theory can produce stable universes with constant positive dark energy at all, which is the foundation of the 10 to the 500. In 2024 and 2025, data from the DESI survey began to hint that dark energy is weakening; that is not yet established. The multiverse has been neither confirmed nor ruled out. The verdict: unresolved.

The moment

On the slide, a smoky white haze fills the space between galaxies. Greene explains that this is dark energy; it's invisible, "but I've made it smokey white here so you can see it." Then comes the number the whole room is meant to remember, and he gives it a single sentence:

Expressed in the relevant unit, it is spectacularly small.— Brian Greene, TED, 2012

He never reads out how many zeros it has, and the transcript doesn't either. But everything that follows (string theory, 10 to the 500, bubble universes) exists to answer it. A few minutes later comes the boldest line of the talk:

Which means we have been asking the wrong question.— Brian Greene, TED, 2012

The speaker, and the year

Brian Greene is a physicist at Columbia University who works on how string theory's extra dimensions curl up (compactification) and what that means for cosmology. He is also one of the best explainers of physics alive: The Elegant Universe and The Fabric of the Cosmos both became PBS NOVA specials, and with Tracy Day he co-founded the World Science Festival. When the count of candidate shapes passes into the thousands he adds, "since after all, graduate students need something to do," and the room laughs.

The timing was handed to him. In October 2011 the Nobel Prize in physics went to Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess "for the discovery of the accelerating expansion of the Universe through observations of distant supernovae." Earlier that year Greene had published The Hidden Reality, a whole book on varieties of parallel universes. The talk compresses that book into 21 minutes and picks the version with the most physicists behind it and the most controversy around it: the string landscape plus eternal inflation. In 2012 that combination was, for some leading theorists, a serious working hypothesis and, for others, a sign of physics giving up on prediction. Greene admits up front that the idea is speculative, but asks the audience to take it seriously, "as it just might be right."

Glossary

  • Cosmological constant: dark energy whose density never changes, written Λ.
  • Landscape: the set of all "vacua" string theory allows (a shape for the extra dimensions plus values for the fields inside them), often put at around 10 to the 500.
  • Eternal inflation: the ultra-fast expansion of the early universe never stops in some regions, which keep spawning new "bubble universes."
  • Anthropic reasoning: explaining the parameters we observe by the requirement that observers can exist at all, in other words a selection effect created by observers themselves.
  • σ (sigma): a unit of statistical significance; the bigger it is, the less the result looks like a fluke.

The talk, in full

Greene lays out the map at once. Part one: the Nobel result and the mystery it exposed. Part two: a solution from string theory, which is where the multiverse enters. Part three: inflation, which pulls the pieces together.

Part one starts in 1929, when Hubble found that distant galaxies are all rushing away and space is expanding. Even so, everyone was certain the expansion must be slowing, the way gravity slows an apple thrown upward. In the 1990s two teams set out to measure how fast it was slowing and found it speeding up. The answer comes from Einstein: in general relativity, if space is filled with a uniform invisible energy, the gravity it generates is repulsive. That is dark energy. The mystery is the amount, spectacularly small; we want the number to emerge from the laws of physics, and no one has managed it. He heads off the "why should I care?" objection: some details are windows onto uncharted reality, and the only approach that has made headway on this number invokes other universes.

Part two gives three facts about string theory. What it is: look inside particles, far below anything we can probe, and you find tiny vibrating filaments of energy; like violin strings sounding different notes, different vibration patterns are electrons, quarks, photons. Its cost: the math is inconsistent unless there are extra dimensions of space, crumpled too small to detect. The key link: the shape of those dimensions constrains how strings vibrate, and "in string theory, vibration determines everything," including the amount of dark energy. Know the shape, compute the dark energy.

But all we have is a list of candidates. First about five, small enough to check one by one; then hundreds and thousands, "since after all, graduate students need something to do"; then millions and billions, up to about 10 to the 500. Some concluded string theory would never make testable predictions. Others turned the issue on its head: maybe every shape is equally real, each a universe of its own. Then the laws of physics can't explain one number for dark energy, because there are many numbers; the question is why we find ourselves in a universe with the value we measure. And that can be answered. With much more dark energy, matter is blown apart whenever it tries to clump; with much less, everything collapses quickly. Either way, no galaxies, no stars, no us. His summary is almost a grin:

And that would be that. Mystery solved, multiverse found.— Brian Greene, TED, 2012

For anyone unsatisfied, he brings in Kepler, who worked for decades to explain why the Sun is 93 million miles from Earth and failed, because there are many planets at all kinds of distances. The right question is why we're on a planet at this distance: closer is too hot, farther too cold. For planets this is plainly the right reasoning; for universes, "it may also be." He then names the crack himself: we know other planets exist, while other universes are so far speculation. So he needs a mechanism that actually makes universes.

Part three opens with a joke:

The Big Bang leaves out something pretty important, the Bang.— Brian Greene, TED, 2012

Inflationary cosmology supplied the fuel for the Bang, based on a quantum field. The only detail that matters: it is so efficient it is virtually impossible to use up, so the Big Bang is likely not a one-time event, and "our universe becoming but one bubble in a grand cosmic bubble bath of universes." Meld that with string theory: each bubble has differently shaped extra dimensions and different physics, and we're in this one because only here are conditions right for our form of life. He calls it "compelling but highly controversial." Can it be tested? Inflation's predicted pattern of hot and cold spots has been observed; and if another universe ever hit ours, it would leave an extra subtle pattern in the temperature of the sky "that we might one day be able to detect."

The ending is the quietest part. Accelerating expansion will carry distant galaxies away so far and so fast that their light can never cross the gap. Astronomers of the far future will see only endless, static black stillness and conclude the universe is static, with a single oasis of matter: "a picture of the cosmos that we definitively know to be wrong." Even with records from our era, would they believe ancient texts or their own state-of-the-art observations? Greene suspects the latter. His last lines:

Sometimes nature guards her secrets with the unbreakable grip of physical law. Sometimes the true nature of reality beckons from just beyond the horizon.— Brian Greene, TED, 2012

There's an unspoken tension here: he has just argued for an ensemble of universes we will very likely never see, and closes with a story about truth slipping beyond view.

Beneath the iceberg

How small is "spectacularly small"? Steven Weinberg's 2000 review spells it out: some calculable contributions to the vacuum energy exceed what observation allows by about 120 orders of magnitude, so cancelling them would have to be accurate to 120 decimal places. On Sean Carroll's Mindscape podcast in 2019, Greene put it as "off by a factor of 10 to the 120."

The anthropic argument predates the landscape, and it needs probabilities. In 1987 Weinberg used "galaxies must be able to form" to put an upper bound on dark energy. In the 2000 review he notes that this alone allows about 200 times today's matter density, "a big improvement over missing by 120 orders of magnitude, but not good enough." Add a "principle of mediocrity" (we should find ourselves in a universe typical of those that allow life) and the chance of seeing dark energy this small comes out at 5% to 12%, "a little low, but not implausibly so." So an anthropic explanation is more than "otherwise we wouldn't be here": it needs a probability distribution over universes. The talk skips that step entirely.

Kepler is in the same review: Kepler tried to derive planetary distances from nested Platonic solids, and the anthropic account of Earth's liquid-water orbit, Weinberg writes, makes sense "if and only if" there is a very large number of big bangs. Greene's analogy tracks this almost point for point; the "if and only if" became "it may also be."

"Shape" is a simplification. Raphael Bousso and Joseph Polchinski showed in 2000 that fluxes in the extra dimensions (think of field lines wrapped around them) contribute to dark energy in discrete steps; one flux gives steps far too large, but with order 100 fluxes the allowed values become dense enough to include the observed one, a "discretuum." The 2003 KKLT paper (Kachru, Kallosh, Linde, Trivedi) gave a recipe for string vacua with positive dark energy, but it needs anti-D3 branes to lift the energy up, and the result is only metastable, with a lifetime far longer than 1010 years. The talk's "a shape for each universe" is, in the papers, shape plus fluxes plus branes, and our universe may be only temporarily stable. Even the 10 to the 500 is soft: in 2019 Greene called it "a number that's thrown around that's not as meaningful as it might sound."

"Impossible to use up" hides the measure problem. Alan Guth's 2007 review concedes the price: in eternal inflation "anything that can happen will happen; in fact, it will happen an infinite number of times." Asking how common a kind of universe is means computing infinity divided by infinity; the answer depends on how you cut it off, and the most naive cutoff yields what he calls the youngness paradox. Anthropic explanations need exactly that probability, and eternal inflation makes it hard to define. This is the biggest unpaid bill behind "mystery solved."

The ending and the test both have a backstory. Greene doesn't name it, but the "future astronomers will think the universe is static" scenario is the subject of a 2007 paper by Lawrence Krauss and Robert Scherrer, "The Return of a Static Universe and the End of Cosmology." As for bubble collisions, in December 2010 Stephen Feeney, Matthew Johnson, Daniel Mortlock and Hiranya Peiris published the "First Observational Tests of Eternal Inflation": WMAP's seven-year data do not warrant adding bubble collisions, with fewer than 1.6 detectable collisions on the full sky at 68% confidence. When the audience heard "one day," that road had already been walked once, empty-handed. In a 2011 Physics World interview Greene called falsifiability "the key question" for the multiverse and allowed that some versions "stand outside science." On stage that caveat shrank to one word at the start: "speculative."

What happened next

UNRESOLVED · as of 2026-11

The multiverse has been neither confirmed nor falsified. The one direct test (bubble collisions) has found no signal; the link "string theory has many stable vacua with positive dark energy" has faced serious challenge since 2018; and 2024–2025 data hint that dark energy itself may not be constant. None of the three is settled.

2013. After the Planck satellite results, Anna Ijjas, Paul Steinhardt (one of inflation's early architects) and Abraham Loeb published "Inflationary paradigm in trouble after Planck2013." The "multiverse-unpredictability problem," they wrote, "has been known for three decades before Planck2013 and, thus far, lacks a solution"; the most natural measures, weighting by volume and counting bubbles, fail. It is the measure problem Guth concedes, with a harsher conclusion from critics: a theory that can produce anything cannot say what we should see.

2014 and 2017. George Ellis and Joe Silk published "Defend the integrity of physics" in Nature, subtitled: attempts to exempt speculative theories of the Universe from experimental verification undermine science. The text is paywalled; this page read only the title, subtitle and reference list (which includes philosopher Richard Dawid's work on non-empirical theory assessment) and does not restate the argument. In 2017 Ijjas, Steinhardt and Loeb challenged inflation in Scientific American, and more than thirty physicists led by Guth and Linde signed a rebuttal; this page did not read either text and records only that the exchange happened.

2018. Georges Obied, Hirosi Ooguri, Lev Spodyneiko and Cumrun Vafa proposed a version of the "swampland" conjecture: in any consistent theory of quantum gravity, an inequality forbids metastable vacua with positive dark energy, motivated by how "notoriously difficult" it has been "to construct a meta-stable de Sitter (dS) vacuum in string theory in a controlled approximation." If right, a KKLT-style landscape lies outside what string theory allows. On Mindscape in 2019 Greene was candid: the KKLT authors "have not backed down," but if the challenge holds "it would call into question over a decade, maybe close to two decades, of research"; perhaps dark energy slowly decays instead, solutions that are "somewhat easier to accommodate"; an observational verdict might take "a 100 or 500 years," "but maybe we'll get lucky."

2024–2025. Combined with the microwave background and supernovae, first-year data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) preferred dark energy that changes over time, at 2.5 to 3.9σ away from the standard model. The second data release in March 2025 (over 14 million galaxies and quasars) pushed that to 2.8 to 4.2σ, with careful wording: "Unless there is an unknown systematic error associated with one or more datasets." In Quanta's coverage Vafa said of string theory, "The theory kind of demands that it change"; while Bousso, co-author of the 2000 discretuum paper, said, "For three sigma, even four, I'm not going to lose any sleep."

If dark energy really is changing, the "number to be explained" at the start of the talk isn't a constant, and the question has to be reposed; many string theorists would read it as support for the swampland, which cuts against the landscape. But the multiverse wouldn't die with it: eternal inflation and the string landscape are separable. As of today, the observational parts of the talk all stand, every link of the theoretical chain is still contested, and the one direct test he offered has found no signal, before the talk or since.

Crossovers

  • Statistics · Abraham Wald's bombers: in World War II Wald was asked where to add armour to returning bombers. The answer was where there were no bullet holes, because planes hit there never came back. Anthropic reasoning is the same survivorship selection: the dark energy we see is a sample filtered by "observers can exist." Where it breaks: Wald knew the missing planes existed and could count the sorties; no one can count the universes without observers, so the analogy presupposes exactly what the multiverse needs to prove.
  • Buddhism · Indra's net: the Avatamsaka Sutra describes a net over Indra's palace with a jewel at every knot, each reflecting all the others, another way of replacing one world with countless ones. Where it breaks: Indra's net is about mutual reflection and interdependence; inflation's bubbles are causally cut off and never see one another, barring the odd collision.
  • Philosophy of science · Popper and Dawid: Popper drew the line of science at falsifiability; Richard Dawid's 2013 book String Theory and the Scientific Method argues that when experiment is out of reach, arguments such as "no other theory does the job" can still count in a theory's favour. Much of the multiverse fight is these two standards colliding.
  • Distributed systems · network partitions and split brain: after a network split, an isolated group of nodes sees only itself and may conclude it is the whole cluster. Greene's future astronomers are partitioned by the cosmic horizon and confidently reach a wrong global conclusion. Where it breaks: partitions heal; the cosmic horizon doesn't.

Ten takeaways

  1. In the 1990s two astronomy teams set out to measure how fast cosmic expansion was slowing and found it accelerating, earning the 2011 Nobel Prize in physics.
  2. Dark energy's observed value is spectacularly small: Weinberg estimated some calculable vacuum-energy contributions exceed the limit by about 120 orders of magnitude.
  3. String theory's extra dimensions allow roughly 10 to the 500 candidate configurations, each with different particle properties and dark energy.
  4. The multiverse move swaps "why is dark energy this number?" for "why are we in a universe whose dark energy lets galaxies form?"
  5. Anthropic explanations need a probability distribution over universes; with a principle of mediocrity, Weinberg got a 5% to 12% chance of seeing dark energy this small.
  6. The talk's Kepler analogy already appears in Weinberg's 2000 review, which stresses it works if and only if there are very many big bangs.
  7. In the Bousso–Polchinski and KKLT papers, fluxes and branes in the extra dimensions tune dark energy, and our universe may be merely metastable.
  8. Eternal inflation yields infinitely many bubble universes, and Guth concedes that counting their proportions runs into the infinity-over-infinity measure problem.
  9. A 2010 search of WMAP data for bubble-universe collisions found no signal, and the 2018 swampland conjecture questions whether string theory allows stable positive dark energy.
  10. Anthropic reasoning is the same survivorship selection as Wald's bomber analysis, except no one can count the universes without observers.
