TED2012 · 2012

Each of us is more than the worst thing we have ever done

Bryan Stevenson · "We need to talk about an injustice"

A 52-year-old lawyer stands on the TED stage and confesses something he "probably shouldn't": he has never had a drop of alcohol. The room applauds. He says right away that it isn't virtue. The reason goes back to when he was eight or nine.

That morning his grandmother took him out back alone. She said she had been watching him, that she thought he was special, that he could do anything he wanted. Then she asked for three promises: always love your mom; always do the right thing, even when the right thing is the hard thing; never drink alcohol. He promised. At 14 or 15, out in the woods, his brother offered him a beer, and he said no. His brother asked if he was still hung up on that talk. "Oh, Mama tells all the grandkids that they're special." The audience roared. "I was devastated."

But he kept the promise. Bryan Stevenson uses the story to make a point about identity: once you decide who you are, you can do things other people think make no sense. Then he turns the word on a whole country. Who does America think it is?

The numbers come next. In 1972 there were 300,000 people in American jails and prisons; now there are 2.3 million, the highest rate of incarceration in the world. One in three Black men between 18 and 30 is in jail, in prison, on probation or on parole. The United States, he says, is the only country that sentences 13-year-olds to die in prison. On death row, for every nine people executed, one innocent person has been exonerated and freed. We would never let planes fly, he says, if one in every nine crashed.

He turns the death penalty question around. The question isn't whether people deserve to die for their crimes. It's whether we deserve to kill. A German scholar once told him that, given Germany's history, it could never again systematically execute people. Yet in the American South, he says, defendants whose victims are white are many times more likely to be sentenced to death, in the very states where lynching victims lie buried.

Then come the three "very simple things" his work has taught him. Each of us is more than the worst thing we've ever done. In too many places, the opposite of poverty is not wealth but justice. And you judge a society by how it treats the poor, the condemned, the incarcerated. He ends with words a Black courthouse janitor once said to him: keep your eyes on the prize, hold on. The audience stood. Afterwards TED attendees gave about a million dollars to his campaign on juvenile sentencing.

What happened next: partly upheld. Less than four months later, he won Miller v. Alabama in the Supreme Court, 5 to 4: mandatory life without parole for juveniles is unconstitutional. By 2026, 28 states and the District of Columbia had banned the sentence outright, and nearly 1,300 people had been released, though a 2021 case, Jones, narrowed the door again. His numbers have to be taken one at a time. The "11 times" and "22 times" for Southern death sentences are raw ratios; after controlling for 39 variables, the figure is 4.3 times. In the Q&A he said violent crime had stayed "relatively stable" and that the prison boom came mainly from the drug war; later data and research don't support either claim. Some of his numbers held up and some were stated too broadly. His central line has only become more pressing. Most Americans behind bars are not there for drugs, so cutting mass incarceration means deciding how to treat people convicted of violence.

Talk We need to talk about an injustice · Venue TED2012, Long Beach · March 2012 · Length about 23 min (including a closing Q&A with Chris Anderson) · Transcript official TED transcript

The moment

He once wrote a motion late at night, when he was up too late and "wasn't thinking real straight." Its heading:

"Motion to try my poor, 14-year-old Black male client like a privileged, white, 75-year-old corporate executive."— Bryan Stevenson, "We need to talk about an injustice," 2012

Laughter, then applause and cheers. He goes on: the motion alleged prosecutorial, police and judicial misconduct, and had "a crazy line in there about how there's no conduct in this county, it's all misconduct." The next morning he woke and wondered whether he had dreamed it. "And to my horror, not only had I written it, but I had sent it to court."

Under the joke sits a hard question. A judge had certified a 14-year-old to stand trial as an adult. "How can a judge turn you into something that you're not?"

The speaker, and the year

Bryan Stevenson grew up in a poor, racially segregated settlement in southern Delaware. His grandmother's parents had been born into slavery in Virginia. Harvard Law School left him feeling lost, until in December 1983 an internship took him to a small Atlanta office defending people on death row, and he walked onto death row for the first time. He went on to found the Equal Justice Initiative (EJI) in Montgomery, Alabama, which represents the condemned, the poor and children prosecuted as adults. "I spend most of my time in jails, in prisons, on death row," he tells the audience.

The timing of March 2012 was unusual. Three weeks later, on March 20, he would stand before the U.S. Supreme Court for two boys who had been 14 when they committed murder and were serving life without parole. One of the cases was Miller v. Alabama. The TED audience was drawn from technology, design and entertainment, and he named the contrast outright: he asked them to think about the poor and the disadvantaged, "those who will never get to TED." Afterwards Chris Anderson came on stage to ask what people could do "other than writing a check." According to later reports, TED attendees gave about one million dollars to his juvenile sentencing campaign.

The talk, in full

He opens with TED itself. TED has an identity, and words spoken there travel. A teacher's words can be meaningful, but a compassionate teacher's words can be especially meaningful. So he will talk about the power of identity, a lesson he learned from his grandmother.

The grandmother takes the first quarter of the talk, delivered like stand-up. She was "the end of every argument in our family," and "the beginning of a lot of arguments." She hugged so hard you could barely breathe, then an hour later asked, "Bryan, do you still feel me hugging you?" Then come the talk out back, the three promises, and the brother's reveal. With the laugh landed, he shows his hand:

"I don't say that because I think that's virtuous; I say that because there is power in identity."— Bryan Stevenson, 2012

Then the laughter settles. His grandfather was in prison during Prohibition, and his uncles died of alcohol-related diseases. The promises she asked for were the costs she had seen.

He turns to criminal justice and lays down the numbers layer by layer: 300,000 people locked up in 1972, 2.3 million now, seven million more on probation and parole; one in three Black men aged 18 to 30 under correctional control; 34 percent of Black men in Alabama permanently stripped of the vote; the only country that sentences 13-year-olds to die in prison. Then wealth:

"We have a system of justice in this country that treats you much better if you're rich and guilty than if you're poor and innocent. Wealth, not culpability, shapes outcomes."— Bryan Stevenson, 2012

With the death penalty comes the key turn of the talk. We usually ask whether people deserve to die for the crimes they commit. The other question is: Do we deserve to kill? It moves the question from the defendant's identity back to ours, and closes the loop with the opening. What supports it is error: for every nine people executed, one innocent person has been exonerated and released. "In aviation, we would never let people fly on airplanes if, for every nine planes that took off, one would crash." The audience laughs. Somehow, he says, we insulate ourselves from the problem.

Then history. The era of terror from the end of Reconstruction to World War II, with its lynchings and bombings, shaped generations. Older people ask him to tell audiences to stop saying the country faced terrorism for the first time after 9/11: "we grew up with that." South Africa and Rwanda went through truth and reconciliation; America did not. Then the quietest moment of the talk. A German scholar told him that with Germany's history, it could never systematically execute human beings. He imagined Germany executing people, disproportionately Jewish people. "I couldn't bear it." And yet in the states of the Old South, he says, you are 11 times more likely to get the death penalty if the victim is white than if the victim is Black, and 22 times more likely if the defendant is Black and the victim white, in the very states where lynching victims lie buried.

He begins to close. He quotes Václav Havel: under oppression what people needed most was hope, "an orientation of the spirit." He warns that this kind of identity "will get to you." He tells of reciting EJI's long list of goals to Rosa Parks, who replied, "That's going to make you tired, tired, tired." Johnnie Carr, who had organized the Montgomery bus boycott, put her finger in his face: "That's why you've got to be brave, brave, brave." Then the three sentences:

"I've come to understand and to believe that each of us is more than the worst thing we've ever done."— Bryan Stevenson, 2012

"Even if you kill someone, you're not just a killer," and so the law must respect a basic human dignity. Second: "in too many places, the opposite of poverty is justice." Third:

"Ultimately, you judge the character of a society not by how they treat their rich and the powerful and the privileged, but by how they treat the poor, the condemned, the incarcerated."— Bryan Stevenson, 2012

The ending returns to the crazy motion. On the courthouse steps, an older Black janitor hugged him and whispered, "I'm so proud of you." Inside, the room filled with police, assistant prosecutors and clerks, all angry that someone was talking about race and poverty. The janitor finally came in and sat behind him. At the break a deputy sheriff rushed over: "Jimmy, what are you doing in this courtroom?" The old man stood up:

"I came into this courtroom to tell this young man, 'Keep your eyes on the prize, hold on.'"— Bryan Stevenson, 2012

He hands that line straight to the audience. In the Q&A he adds two points we'll come back to. California would soon vote on redirecting the billion dollars it planned to spend on the death penalty over five years to law enforcement. And the prison boom came not from violent crime but from "this misguided war on drugs."

Below the waterline

Where "11 times" and "22 times" come from. The talk cites no source, but the figures match the Baldus study, cited in the Supreme Court's 1987 decision in McCleskey v. Kemp. It covered more than 2,000 murder cases in 1970s Georgia. Defendants charged with killing white victims received death sentences in 11% of cases, and those charged with killing Black victims in 1%. Black defendants with white victims received death sentences in 22% of cases, Black defendants with Black victims in 1%. The two multiples in the talk are these raw ratios.

The stage version leaves out three layers. First, this was one state in one decade; on stage it became "the states of the Old South." Second, the raw ratios don't account for how serious each case was. Baldus examined 230 possible non-racial explanations. In one model, after controlling for 39 of them, defendants charged with killing white victims were 4.3 times as likely to be sentenced to death. That is still large, but it isn't 11. Third, in the same model the defendant's own race carried a factor of only 1.1, and the raw numbers even showed white defendants sentenced to death more often overall (7%) than Black defendants (4%). The robust finding is that the victim's race set the price of a life. That fits his argument about whose lives count even better, but the version on stage is broader than the original result.

The Court accepted the statistics and rejected the appeal, 5 to 4. Justice Powell wrote: "Apparent disparities in sentencing are an inevitable part of our criminal justice system." In dissent, Justice Brennan noted that the majority feared the claim would open every sentence to challenge: "Taken on its face, such a statement seems to suggest a fear of too much justice."

The denominator of "one in nine." On stage he says that for every nine executions, one person has been exonerated, and then: "one out of nine people, innocent." Those are different claims. The first compares exonerations to executions. The second sounds as if one in nine of the executed was innocent, but the people exonerated are exactly the ones who were not executed. A closer estimate of how many death sentences are wrong comes from Samuel Gross and colleagues in PNAS (2014). Using survival analysis, they estimated that if everyone sentenced to death stayed under that sentence indefinitely, at least 4.1% would be exonerated, which they call a conservative figure. The denominator is all death sentences: roughly one in 25, measured with a different ruler from the airplane image.

The grandmother, and the larger word in his book. Two years later his memoir Just Mercy (2014) opens with her again, with small differences from the stage. The talk gives her 10 children; the book says nine. The book also admits that when she asked whether he still felt her hug, "I said no a lot because it made me happy to be wrapped in her formidable arms." Above all, the book adds a line of hers:

"You can't understand most of the important things from a distance, Bryan. You have to get close."— Bryan Stevenson, Just Mercy, 2014 (excerpt in the Atlanta Journal-Constitution)

Proximity to the condemned, he writes, is what brought him back from feeling lost. At TED the key word was identity; in the book it became proximity. Later, in commencement speeches, he would repeat four things: get proximate to suffering, change the narrative, stay hopeful, be willing to do uncomfortable things. The TED talk already holds all four; they just hadn't been made into a list yet.

The era of terror, counted. On stage he said "we don't really know very much about it." In 2015 EJI's report Lynching in America documented 4,075 racial terror lynchings in 12 Southern states between 1877 and 1950, at least 800 more than the most comprehensive earlier count.

What happened next

PARTLY UPHELD · as of 2026-11

The moral core held, and so, mostly, did the fight over juvenile sentencing; the death-penalty error figures got worse, not better. But the racial multiples on stage were stated too broadly, and two claims from the Q&A, about violent crime and the drug war, have been contradicted by data and research.

June 2012: the fastest payoff. Less than four months after the talk, the Supreme Court held 5 to 4 in Miller v. Alabama that mandatory life without parole for people under 18 at the time of their crimes violates the Eighth Amendment. Justice Kagan predicted such sentences would become "uncommon." But the ruling barred only mandatory sentences, so judges could still impose them case by case. In 2016 Montgomery v. Louisiana (6 to 3) made Miller retroactive. In 2021 Jones v. Mississippi (6 to 3) pulled back: a sentencer need not make a separate finding of "permanent incorrigibility." The states went further. A January 2026 report from the Campaign for the Fair Sentencing of Youth counts 28 states and D.C. with outright bans and about an 85% drop since Miller in the number of people serving the sentence, and says nearly 1,300 have come home.

The death penalty: the error rate hasn't improved, and the fight over "exoneration" goes on. A 2021 report from the Death Penalty Information Center (DPIC) put the ratio at one exoneration for every 8.3 executions, worse than "one in nine"; by the end of 2024 the total had reached 200. On the other side, Justice Scalia, concurring in Kansas v. Marsh (2006), wrote that "mischaracterization of reversible error as actual innocence is endemic in abolitionist rhetoric" and said DPIC's list included "some dubious candidates." That is why the Gross estimate of 4.1% matters: it sidesteps the argument over who counts. The California vote he mentioned, Proposition 34 in November 2012, failed by about 48% to 52%.

Mass incarceration: down, though not as far as he hoped. The prison population peaked in 2009. By the Prison Policy Initiative's 2025 count, "nearly 2 million" people are locked up, with 671,000 more on parole and 2.9 million on probation. The U.S. still has "the highest incarceration rate of any independent democracy on earth."

Two Q&A claims that didn't hold. He said "the violent crime rate has remained relatively stable." Pew Research Center's summary shows the FBI's violent crime rate fell 49% from 1993 to 2022, and the Bureau of Justice Statistics victimization survey shows a 71% drop. The other claim, that the boom came from the war on drugs, was taken apart by Fordham law professor John Pfaff in Locked In (2017). State prison growth was driven mainly by local prosecutors filing more felony charges, and drug offenders are a minority of prisoners; the Prison Policy Initiative likewise finds four out of five people behind bars are held for something other than a drug offense. As Pfaff told Reason: "By focusing on drugs, we're delaying this conversation on violence."

Here is the twist. Pfaff overturned an offhand answer, and in doing so made the core of the talk stronger. If most prisoners were convicted of violence, reforms that free only "nonviolent drug offenders" can never end mass incarceration, and sooner or later the country has to decide whether someone who has killed is still a person. That is exactly what "more than the worst thing we've ever done" answers. In April 2018 EJI opened the National Memorial for Peace and Justice in Montgomery, with the names of more than 4,000 lynching victims. He had said on stage that America never did truth and reconciliation; he went and built a piece of it himself.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · Angulimala: In the Pali Canon (Majjhima Nikaya 86), the serial killer Angulimala meets the Buddha, becomes a monk and attains arahantship. Buddhism has told this story of a man not defined by his worst acts for over two thousand years. Where it differs: even as a monk he is beaten bloody on his alms round, so change does not cancel karma; and Buddhism holds there is no fixed self at all, while Stevenson needs a person who carries dignity, so the law has someone to respect.
  • Psychology · fundamental attribution error: Named by Lee Ross in 1977, it is our tendency to explain others' behavior by character and underweight circumstance. Calling someone "a killer" turns one act into a whole person. Where it differs: the attribution error is a bias that experiments can correct; Stevenson's claim is moral, and holds even when the attribution is accurate.
  • Business thinking · one-way doors: Jeff Bezos's shareholder letter for 2015 separates reversible "two-way door" decisions from irreversible "one-way doors" that must be made slowly and carefully. Execution is the ultimate one-way door, and the exoneration ratio suggests that door is not being reviewed carefully enough. Where it differs: Bezos's point is to speed up reversible decisions, and justice has no fast half.
  • Political philosophy · Rawls's difference principle: In A Theory of Justice (1971), Rawls holds that inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged. Testing institutions by their worst-off members is the same vantage as judging a society by its prisoners. Where it differs: Rawls is about distributing primary goods, Stevenson about punishment and dignity, and offenders are not automatically the "least advantaged" in Rawls's scheme.

Ten takeaways

  1. Bryan Stevenson's story of promising his grandmother never to drink shows that a settled identity lets people do what others think makes no sense.
  2. The TED talk shifts identity from the person to the nation: how America treats prisoners determines what kind of country America is.
  3. The death penalty question can be reversed: not whether offenders deserve to die, but whether we deserve to kill.
  4. "One exonerated for every nine executed" is a ratio of exonerations to executions, not a claim that one in nine executed people was innocent.
  5. Gross and colleagues estimated in 2014 that at least 4.1% of U.S. death sentences are wrongful convictions, a conservative figure.
  6. The Baldus study's raw figures were 11% versus 1%; after 39 controls, killing a white victim made a death sentence 4.3 times as likely.
  7. In the Baldus data, the victim's race drove the price of a life; the defendant's own race had only a small effect.
  8. Miller v. Alabama banned only mandatory life without parole for juveniles, and Jones v. Mississippi in 2021 widened judges' discretion again.
  9. John Pfaff's work shows prosecutors' felony charging drove the prison boom and drug offenders are a minority, so reform must address violent offenses.
  10. The Buddhist story of Angulimala, a killer turned saint, also says a person is more than their worst act, without erasing the consequences.
