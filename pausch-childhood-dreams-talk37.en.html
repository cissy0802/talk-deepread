Carnegie Mellon · 2007

The brick walls are there to stop the other people: the methods in the Last Lecture, and the half it left unsaid

Randy Pausch · Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams

On September 18, 2007, an auditorium at Carnegie Mellon was full. Randy Pausch walked on stage and started with the bad news. His CAT scans showed about ten tumors in his liver, and the doctors had given him three to six months of good health. “That was a month ago, so you can do the math.” Then he got down on the floor and did push-ups. Anyone who wanted to cry or pity him, he said when he got up, could come down and do a few of those first.

He was a computer science professor who worked on virtual reality, with three children. For the next hour and more he did not talk about cancer, or about his wife and kids. He talked about six childhood dreams: being in zero gravity, playing in the NFL, writing an article for the World Book Encyclopedia, being Captain Kirk, winning the giant stuffed animals at amusement parks, and becoming a Disney Imagineer. He got most of them. The ones he missed gave him something else.

Every dream hit a wall. Disney sent him “some of the damned nicest go-to-hell letters I have ever gotten.” Carnegie Mellon’s PhD program turned him down at first. Out of that came the lecture’s most quoted passage:

The brick walls are not there to keep us out. The brick walls are there to give us a chance to show how badly we want something.— Randy Pausch, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

The other word that kept coming back was “head fake,” the feint from basketball. You send kids to play football not so they learn football, but so they learn teamwork, perseverance and sportsmanship. Alice, the programming software he built, was a head fake too: kids think they are making movies and games, and they are actually learning to program.

At the end he revealed two head fakes. The first: the talk was not about how to achieve your dreams but about how to lead your life, and if you lead it the right way, “The dreams will come to you.” The second: “The talk’s not for you, it’s for my kids.”

The lecture became a phenomenon. Within eight months more than six million people had watched it online, and the book of the same name, published in April 2008, went to number one. Pausch died on July 25, 2008, a little over eleven months after the doctors gave him “three to six months of good health.”

So did the methods hold up? Partly. The head fake has one real experiment behind it. His student Caitlin Kelleher gave 88 middle-school girls one of two versions of Alice. Girls with the storytelling version spent 42% more of their time programming, but they learned the same amount, and their interest in studying computer science was no different. The brick-wall line is a story told from the far side of the wall: nearly every wall he got over had someone behind it making a phone call for him. He said so in the same lecture, “You can’t get there alone,” but that line traveled less. In 2012 his wife Jai published a memoir about the twenty-three months of caregiving: the exhaustion, the resentment, the marriage counseling, and a husband who, off stage, could be irritable and stubborn. The message in a bottle for his children did reach them. It just wasn’t the whole story.

The Moment

A colleague finished the introduction and the room rose to its feet. Pausch’s first words: “Make me earn it.”

Then he explained that the lecture series used to be called the Last Lecture: if you had one last lecture to give before you died, what would it be? “I thought, damn, I finally nailed the venue and they renamed it.” After the laughter, he did what his father taught him and introduced the elephant in the room. About ten tumors in his liver; three to six months of good health left; “That was a month ago, so you can do the math.”

We cannot change the cards we are dealt, just how we play the hand.— Randy Pausch, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

He was not in denial, he said; the family had just moved to Virginia because it would be a better place for them “down the road.” And right now he was in such good health that it was “the greatest thing of cognitive dissonance you will ever see”: “I am in better shape than most of you.” He got down on the floor and did push-ups. When he stood up: “So anybody who wants to cry or pity me can down and do a few of those, and then you may pity me.”

The Speaker, and the Year

Pausch was born in 1960, studied at Brown, took his PhD at Carnegie Mellon, got tenure a year early at the University of Virginia, and came back to Carnegie Mellon in 1997. He worked on virtual reality and human-computer interaction, but he was best known for three teaching projects: a course in which students from across the campus built virtual worlds in teams, a professional master’s program in entertainment technology he co-founded with a drama professor, and Alice, software that teaches children to program. The colleague who introduced him noted his lunch of choice: turkey sandwiches on white bread with mayo.

The talk was not booked because of the cancer. His symptoms appeared in late summer 2006; in September he had Whipple surgery, followed by chemotherapy and radiation through May 2007. In August he wrote on his health-update page that he was scheduled to speak at Carnegie Mellon on September 18. On August 15, his doctors told him the cancer had spread to his liver and spleen and that he likely had “three to six months of good health left.” He kept the date.

The room was full of colleagues, students, and the mentors and old friends he would name from the stage; Jeffrey Zaslow, a Wall Street Journal columnist who was there, later put it at 400 people. Zaslow’s editors had told him a phone interview would do. He went anyway. So the lecture had two audiences: the people in the room, and the three children for whom the recording was meant.

The Talk, Unpacked

He started by ruling out three subjects. Not cancer: “If you have any herbal supplements or remedies, please stay away from me.” Not his wife and kids: “I’m good, but I’m not good enough to talk about that without tearing up.” Not religion, though he announced a deathbed conversion, paused, and said: “I just bought a Macintosh.” What was left came in three parts: his childhood dreams, how he had helped enable the dreams of others, and lessons learned.

Part one is the list. When he was eight or nine, men were landing on the moon on TV and “anything’s possible”; he said “the inspiration and the permission to dream is huge.” Six dreams: zero gravity, the NFL, a World Book article, being Captain Kirk, winning big stuffed animals, and being a Disney Imagineer.

Zero gravity gave him his first method: have specific dreams. He never wanted to be an astronaut. As a boy he wore glasses and was told astronauts couldn’t, and anyway “I just wanted the floating.” NASA had a training plane, the Vomit Comet, that flew parabolic arcs with about 25 seconds of near-weightlessness at the top of each one, and college teams could compete for a flight. His team won, and he hit his first brick wall: faculty advisors could not fly with the teams. He read the rules carefully and found that students could bring a local journalist. So he called NASA to ask where to fax his resignation as faculty advisor and his application as a journalist. “That’s a little transparent, don’t you think?” the man said. Yes, said Pausch, but our project is virtual reality, and we’re bringing a pile of VR headsets that all the students from all the teams will try and all the real journalists will get to film. He got the fax number. That gave him a second method: have something to bring to the table, “because that will make you more welcome.”

The NFL was the dream he didn’t reach, and he said he probably got more from it than from any of the ones he did. He signed up at nine, the smallest kid in the league. His coach, Jim Graham, was six-foot-four, had played linebacker at Penn State, and was so old school “he thought the forward pass was a trick play.” He came to the first practice with no footballs. When a kid asked, he asked back how many men were on the field (twenty-two) and how many were touching the ball at any time (one). “Right, so we’re going to work on what those other twenty-one guys are doing.” Fundamentals, fundamentals, fundamentals. At another practice Graham rode him the whole time, and afterwards an assistant coach came over to say that was a good thing:

when you’re screwing up and nobody’s saying anything to you anymore, that means they gave up.— Randy Pausch, quoting an assistant coach, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

His conclusion: “Your critics are your ones telling you they still love you and care.” Another coach, Setliff, taught him enthusiasm by sending players into absurdly wrong positions for a single play, the short guys all at receiver; because it was only one play, everyone went all out. He pulled a football out of his pocket and said that when he was working on a hard problem, people would see him wandering the halls with one. Then a line he had picked up at Electronic Arts: “experience is what you get when you didn’t get what you wanted.”

This is also where he first named the idea that runs through the whole talk: the head fake, or indirect learning. When we send kids to play football or soccer or swim, we don’t actually want them to learn football. We want them to learn teamwork, sportsmanship, perseverance. “And you should keep your eye out for them because they’re everywhere.”

The World Book dream was a punchline. He had become an authority on virtual reality, “but not like a really important one,” so he was at the level of people the World Book would badger, and he wrote the entry under V. Having been selected, “I now believe that Wikipedia is a perfectly fine source for your information because I know what the quality control is for real encyclopedias. They let me in.” Captain Kirk carried a lesson in leadership: Kirk wasn’t the smartest man on the ship (Spock was smart, McCoy was the doctor, Scotty the engineer), so what skill set let him run it? Later William Shatner, writing a book about which Star Trek technologies had come true, visited Pausch’s lab, and they built him a virtual version of the old bridge and put it on red alert. “It’s really cool to meet your boyhood idol, but it’s even cooler when he comes to you to see what cool stuff you’re doing in your lab.” For the stuffed animals, he said that in an age of digitally manipulated images people might doubt the photos, so he had a row of giant bears brought on stage and invited anyone who wanted “a little piece of me” to take one afterwards.

The hardest dream was Imagineering. At eight, on a cross-country family trip to Disneyland, he didn’t say “I want to experience this.” He said “I want to make stuff like this.” After his PhD he applied and got those “damned nicest go-to-hell letters.” Then came the passage that would travel furthest:

But remember, the brick walls are there for a reason. The brick walls are not there to keep us out. The brick walls are there to give us a chance to show how badly we want something. Because the brick walls are there to stop the people who don’t want it badly enough. They’re there to stop the other people.— Randy Pausch, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

What followed was really a story about how to get over walls. In 1991, at Virginia, his group built “Virtual Reality on Five Dollars a Day”: when everyone thought VR took half a million dollars, they hacked together a working system for about five thousand. At the conference, Tom Furness, one of the big names in VR, stood up to ask a question. Pausch said he would love to answer it, “but first, will you have lunch with me tomorrow?” There was humility in that moment, he said, and also an ask the other person couldn’t refuse. A few years later Imagineering was building a secret Aladdin magic-carpet VR ride. Pausch, who was about to brief the Secretary of Defense on virtual reality with Fred Brooks, used that as his reason to call for materials and was put through to the team lead, Jon Snoddy. Before their lunch he spent “something like 80 hours” asking VR experts what they would ask if they had access to this project, then memorized the list, because he couldn’t walk in reading out “Question 72.” The lunch lasted two hours, and at the end he made the ask: I have a sabbatical coming up. Snoddy: “what’s that?”

This time the wall was a person. A dean at Virginia, whom he called Dean Wormer, saw that Disney would own his intellectual property and killed the deal, and wouldn’t even let him take unpaid leave to go. “It’s very important to know when you’re in a pissing match,” Pausch said, and to get out of it fast. He asked whether this wasn’t really a call for the Dean of Sponsored Research; the dean agreed; Pausch was in Gene Block’s office in “a big ball of dust.” Both men said “I don’t know,” but Block said it as: I don’t have much information, but one of my star faculty members is here and he’s excited, so tell me more. “Some brick walls are made of flesh.” One of his actual jobs on the Aladdin project was cleaning the caps that touched guests’ heads inside the headsets. After six months Disney asked him to stay; he said no, one of the few times he surprised his father, and mentioned the bottle of Maalox in his desk drawer. He became a one-day-a-week consultant for about ten years.

Part two: enabling the dreams of others. It began around 1993 with an undergraduate, Tommy Burnett, whose dream was to work on the next Star Wars film. Pausch told him they probably weren’t going to make those movies; Tommy said they were. He went on to work on all three. Pausch, a self-described efficiency freak, wanted to do this in bulk, so at Carnegie Mellon he created Building Virtual Worlds: 50 students from all departments, random teams of four, two-week projects, teams reshuffled after each one, five projects a semester. Two rules: no shooting violence and no pornography. “And you’d be amazed how many 19-year-old boys are completely out of ideas when you take those off the table.”

The first assignment came back so far beyond his expectations that he called his mentor Andy van Dam. Van Dam said: go back tomorrow, look them in the eye and say, “Guys, that was pretty good, but I know you can do better.” The reasoning: you obviously don’t know where the bar should be, and you’ll only do them a disservice by putting it anywhere. The end-of-term show filled the auditorium he was standing in. His favorite moment in ten years was a world that crashed on stage, and a student dressed as a ninja who drew his sword, cried “I am dishonored!” and dropped, to be dragged off by his teammates. He said he could tell whether a world was good just by body language before the presentation: if the team stood close together, it was good. When it was time to hand the course on, his advice was to find somebody better than you to hand it to.

After the course came the Entertainment Technology Center (ETC), which he founded with drama professor Don Marinelli: a two-year professional master’s whose curriculum showed a single “Project Course” each semester. He split the credit 70/30 in Marinelli’s favor. Here he described one concrete practice: peer feedback. Every two weeks teammates rated each other; by the end of the semester each student had 15 data points and got a bar chart ranking how easy they were to work with compared with their peers. “That is the best gift an educator can give is to get somebody to become self reflective.” Beyond that was the thing he wanted to be infinitely scalable, Alice:

The best way to teach somebody something is to have them think they’re learning something else.— Randy Pausch, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

He gave the numbers: downloaded well over a million times, eight textbooks, used at ten percent of U.S. colleges. The next version would come out in 2008, teach Java, and use characters from The Sims. “I, like Moses, get to see the promised land, but I won’t get to set foot in it.”

Part three: lessons learned. First, parents: his mother at 70 driving a race car on an amusement-park track, his father on a roller coaster at 80. After his father died, the family found among his things a Bronze Star for valor from the Battle of the Bulge; in 50 years of marriage his mother had never known. In high school Pausch painted his bedroom walls with a submarine, an elevator and the quadratic formula, and his parents let him. “If your kids want to paint their bedroom, as a favor to me let them do it.” Then his mentor. As a sophomore teaching assistant he was, he said, an arrogant young man, and van Dam put an arm around his shoulders and took him for a walk:

Randy, it’s such a shame that people perceive you as so arrogant. Because it’s going to limit what you’re going to be able to accomplish in life.— Randy Pausch, quoting Andy van Dam, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

“What a hell of a way to word ‘you’re being a jerk.’” Van Dam didn’t say what he was; he said how people saw him and what it would cost. It was also van Dam who told him to get a PhD: you’re such a good salesman that any company that gets you will use you as a salesman, so you might as well sell something worthwhile, like education.

Then students. Caitlin Kelleher asked why Alice was fun. Because, he answered, he liked making the little toy soldiers move at his command. She turned Alice into a storytelling tool and showed that middle-school girls would learn to write software if it was presented as storytelling. He gave her the “all-time best head fake award.” Then shorter items: decide whether you’re a Tigger or an Eeyore; loyalty is a two-way street (as an untenured professor he vouched for an undergraduate a dean had it in for, and that student, Dennis Cosgrove, became Alice’s designer); and a colleague named Syl’s advice to young women about men who are interested in them: ignore what they say and pay attention only to what they do.

“Never give up” came with two rejections. Brown put him on the wait list; he called every day until they found it annoying enough to let him in. Carnegie Mellon’s PhD program rejected him. He dropped the letter on van Dam’s desk, van Dam reached for the phone to fix it, and Pausch said no, that’s not the way I was raised. After visits to other schools left him ready to get a job instead, van Dam made the call anyway, in Dutch. At eight the next morning, department head Nico Habermann looked at him and asked: “Randy, why are we here?”

Then the key question of the section: how do you get people to help you? “You can’t get there alone.” His answer: tell the truth, be earnest.

I’ll take an earnest person over a hip person every day, because hip is short term. Earnest is long term.— Randy Pausch, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

Apologize when you screw up; focus on other people, not yourself. For a concrete example of focusing on someone else, he had a giant birthday cake wheeled out: the day before had been his wife’s birthday, and she hadn’t had a proper one, so 500 people sang. Jai came on stage in tears and blew out the candle. Then a run of short lines. Don’t bail: “The best of the gold’s at the bottom of barrels of crap” (48 hours into his sabbatical at EA he learned the company was about to give USC eight million dollars to build a program just like his, and he stayed). Get a feedback loop and listen to it: “The hard part is the listening to it.” Show gratitude: when he got tenure he took his whole research team to Disney World for a week. Don’t complain, just work harder: a photo of Jackie Robinson, with the claim that “It was in his contract not to complain, even when the fans spit on him.” Be good at something. Work hard (his secret to early tenure: call his office any Friday night at ten). Find the best in everybody; wait long enough and people will show you their good side. Be prepared: “Luck is truly where preparation meets opportunity.”

Then he asked whether they had figured out the head fake.

It’s not about how to achieve your dreams. It’s about how to lead your life. If you lead your life the right way, the karma will take care of itself. The dreams will come to you.— Randy Pausch, Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams, 2007

“Have you figured out the second head fake? The talk’s not for you, it’s for my kids. Thank you all, good night.”

Below the Waterline

The methods in this lecture come from different places: some have experiments behind them, some are a lifetime of experience, some are borrowed sayings. Taken one by one, the gap between the stage version and what lies underneath varies a lot.

The head fake has an experiment, but it shows less than the stage version claims. The Kelleher dissertation he praised (2006, with Pausch chairing the committee) produced a main experiment published at CHI, the top human-computer interaction conference, in 2007. Eighty-eight girls from local Girl Scout troops, average age 12.6, were randomly assigned to Storytelling Alice (43) or Generic Alice (45) for a one-time, four-hour workshop, with two hours and fifteen minutes on their assigned version. The storytelling group spent 42% more of their time editing programs. In the last few minutes, when nobody expected them to work, 51% of the storytelling girls were sneaking extra time on their programs, against 16% of the generic group, and 88% of the storytelling girls chose to take their version home. But the two groups scored the same on the programming quiz; the paper itself says storytelling “did not negatively impact” learning. Nor did interest in pursuing computer science differ (p = .33). The authors list their own limits: a short study, volunteer participants, girls only. Strictly, the experiment supports “wrap it differently and kids will spend more time on it,” not “the best way to teach somebody something.”

The evidence for Alice in college courses is thinner. The study usually cited is Moskal, Lurie and Cooper (2004). Among incoming computing majors with no programming experience and weak math, “at risk,” the 25 who took an Alice course first averaged a 3.0 in their first programming course and 88% went on to the second; the 30 who didn’t averaged 1.9, and 47% continued. But students chose for themselves, there was no random assignment, and class time wasn’t controlled; the authors call it a proof of concept with small samples. As for “ten percent of U.S. colleges,” every figure we found comes from Carnegie Mellon or University of Virginia press material, and the 2012 one says it is “based on textbook sales.”

The feedback loop points the right way, but feedback doesn’t always help. Kluger and DeNisi’s 1996 meta-analysis pooled 607 effect sizes: on average feedback improved performance (d = 0.41), yet more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse. What mattered was where feedback pulled attention. Feedback aimed at the task (what the right answer is, how much you’ve improved) helped; feedback aimed at the self (praise, discouragement, threats to self-esteem) often hurt. Pausch’s “how easy to work with” ranking is precisely a chart about you as a person. It made some students self-reflective; by this meta-analysis, it may simply have stung others.

The brick-wall line is only half supported by his own evidence. Line up the walls he described getting over. Carnegie Mellon: van Dam phoned the department head. Disney: the excuse of a Secretary of Defense briefing, and Snoddy’s “we’ll work it out.” Dean Wormer: another dean who said “tell me more.” Each time he wanted it badly, and each time someone with power opened the door. Saying the walls stop “the people who don’t want it badly enough” is a view from the far side. On persistence itself, psychology’s closest measure is grit. Credé and colleagues’ 2017 meta-analysis (88 independent samples, about 67,000 people) found grit only modestly correlated with academic performance (ρ = .18), the perseverance facet somewhat more (.26), and grit correlated .84 with conscientiousness, so it may not be a new trait at all.

The borrowed lines have different origins from the ones he gave. He said he learned “experience is what you get when you didn’t get what you wanted” at EA. Quote Investigator traces “Experience is what you get while you are looking for something else” to a 1923 newspaper and the “don’t get what you want” form to 1938, author unknown. “Luck is where preparation meets opportunity” is often credited to Seneca, but it first appears in an American youth magazine in 1912; the Seneca attribution dates only from 1999. Jackie Robinson’s “contract” is also a simplification. SABR’s account describes a promise made at an August 1945 meeting with Dodgers president Branch Rickey, who wanted a player with “the guts not to fight back,” and Rickey released Robinson from that promise in 1949. None of the sources we read says it was written into his contract.

His longer texts. On November 27, 2007, he went back to Virginia to give a talk on “Time Management,” the one he had thought he would be famous for, all concrete practice: plan each day, prioritize the to-do list, turn money into time. The book The Last Lecture was written by Zaslow from Pausch’s words, spoken into a cellphone headset over 53 bike rides. Pausch later wrote on his homepage: “The lecture really was for my kids, but if others are finding value in it, that is wonderful. But rest assured; I’m hardly unique.”

What Happened Next

PARTLY HOLDS · as of 2026-10

The head fake has an experiment behind it, but the experiment shows kids will spend more time, not that they learn more. The brick walls are a survivor’s story: nearly every wall he got over, someone opened a door for him. As a message in a bottle for his children, it worked.

Autumn 2007: a lecture that should have passed quietly. Days later Zaslow wrote it up in his Wall Street Journal column, with a video of excerpts. He later told Editor & Publisher it was “the perfect storm … Fifteen years ago, a column would have come and gone.” The video spread, and Pausch appeared on Oprah. In November about a dozen publishers emailed him, and Jai was “quite emphatic” that he do it, since they had always talked about his “writing the manual” for her and the kids.

2008: he spent the time he had left. On March 13, just out of hospital after having 1.4 liters of fluid drained from a lung, he flew to Washington to testify at a public witness hearing of the House appropriations subcommittee that funds health research, arguing for pancreatic cancer funding. The Congressional Record preserves his words: “We don’t have advocates for this disease because they don’t live long enough.” The book came out April 8; ten days later he wrote that it was a number one best seller and Hyperion was about to have two million copies in print, “but all I cared about was the first 3 copies.” In May he was named to the Time 100, by which point more than six million people had watched the lecture. He died at home on July 25, aged 47, a little over eleven months after he was told “three to six months.” By then the book had been translated into 30 languages, with nearly three million copies in print.

2009–2026: what he left behind. The Randy Pausch Memorial Footbridge, announced at the lecture, was dedicated on October 30, 2009, joining the computer science building to the arts building, with an actual brick wall at the computer science end. Alice 3, with Sims characters donated by EA and a Java mode, appeared in beta in 2009; in May 2026 Alice marked its thirtieth year with version 3.9.1. But the vision of millions of kids having fun while learning something hard was realized at scale mostly by another tool: MIT’s Scratch, whose statistics page showed 135 million registered users in 2024. That is a comparison of scale; we found no head-to-head study. In late 2012 Congress passed the Recalcitrant Cancer Research Act (law in 2013), and the CEO of the patient group PanCAN later wrote that Pausch’s testimony “helped set the foundation” for it.

Criticism was scarce; the weightiest came from home. In May 2008 Janice Harayda, a former Plain Dealer book editor, noted that the book came from Mitch Albom’s publisher and agent, had a format like Tuesdays With Morrie, and that Pausch “loves clichés.” We found no published critique of the brick-wall line on survivorship grounds; the analysis above is our own reading of the transcript. The more important testimony came from Jai. In the book, Pausch wrote that when he asked her what she had learned, “Turns out, she could write a book titled Forget the Last Lecture: Here is the Real Story.” In 2012 she published her memoir, Dream New Dreams, and told Kirkus:

In our society, caregivers are expected to be stoical and shoulder the burden without support, but I think this is unrealistic.— Jai Pausch, Kirkus interview, 2012

Both of them, she said, were resentful of each other at different times: “Without the counselor I am not sure even if Randy survived that our marriage would have.” One review summed up the Randy of her book as “irritable, argumentative, self-absorbed, controlling,” while noting she doesn’t tarnish his memory. None of this overturns any method from the stage, but it fills in what lay outside the Tigger-or-Eeyore slide: a man who chooses to be Tigger has someone beside him carrying the load.

So the brick walls should be read alongside the line from the same lecture, “You can’t get there alone.” The part of the lecture that holds up best is the one he said wasn’t the point: leaving his children a record of who their father was.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Socioemotional selectivity theory (Laura Carstensen): Stanford’s Carstensen found that when people see their remaining time as limited, their goals shift from acquiring new knowledge and experiences toward emotionally meaningful relationships, in older adults and in seriously ill young people alike. Pausch’s second head fake is nearly a textbook case: a talk ostensibly for 400 people, addressed to three children. Where it differs: the theory predicts people shrink their social circles as time runs short, yet Pausch spent his last months on television, writing a book and testifying to Congress. Perhaps, for him, what he left his kids had to be seen by everyone first.
  • Stoicism · Epictetus’s dichotomy of control: the Enchiridion opens by dividing things into what is up to us and what is not; Pausch’s line about the cards we are dealt has the same structure. The difference: a Stoic would file childhood dreams among the externals not worth clinging to.
  • Evolutionary biology · Exaptation: Gould and Vrba (1982) named traits that evolved for one function and were later co-opted for another, as feathers may have served for warmth before flight. Sending kids to football so they learn teamwork is the same kind of co-opting. The difference: evolution has no designer, and a head fake has a teacher behind it.
  • Buddhism · Maranasati, mindfulness of death: Theravada practice treats recollecting death as a discipline for seeing what matters and wasting less time. The push-ups and “you can do the math” work in much the same way. The difference: mindfulness of death leads toward letting go, while Pausch was talking about how to get the things you want, one by one.

Ten takeaways

  1. Randy Pausch gave the Last Lecture in September 2007, a month after doctors said his cancer had spread and he had three to six months of good health.
  2. Dreams should be specific: Pausch wanted the floating, not the astronaut job, which led him to NASA’s training plane.
  3. To get through a door, bring something to the table: VR headsets for every team got Pausch aboard as a “journalist.”
  4. Critics who still bother with you haven’t given up on you; screwing up while nobody says anything is the worst place to be.
  5. A head fake is indirect learning: people think they are learning one thing, such as making movies, while learning another, such as programming.
  6. In the Storytelling Alice experiment, girls spent 42% more time programming with the story version but learned the same amount.
  7. A feedback meta-analysis found feedback helps on average but worsened performance in over a third of cases, especially feedback aimed at the self.
  8. The claim that brick walls stop people who don’t want it badly enough is a view from the far side: almost every wall Pausch crossed, someone opened a door.
  9. “Luck is where preparation meets opportunity” and “experience is what you get when you didn’t get what you wanted” are anonymous old sayings, not Seneca’s or EA’s.
  10. People who feel time running out turn toward meaningful relationships; the Last Lecture’s real recipients were Pausch’s three children.
