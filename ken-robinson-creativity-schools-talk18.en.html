The argument fits in one sentence; the other fifteen minutes are performance

Ken Robinson · Do schools kill creativity?

On 25 February 2006, four days into the TED conference, Ken Robinson spent about four minutes warming up the room and then compressed everything he had come to say into a single sentence: creativity is now as important in education as literacy, and deserves the same status. The room applauded. He waited for it to die down and said, "That was it, by the way. Thank you very much." Pause. "So, 15 minutes left."

He barely argued anything in those fifteen minutes. He told stories. A six-year-old girl is drawing God; the teacher says nobody knows what God looks like; the girl says, "They will in a minute." His four-year-old son plays Joseph in the Nativity; three four-year-old kings walk in with gifts, get their order muddled, and the third one delivers the line that brings the house down. He riffs on the fact that Shakespeare had a father, was once seven, was once told to go to bed.

The argument threaded between the stories is clear enough. Small children will take a chance because they aren't frightened of being wrong — and "if you're not prepared to be wrong, you'll never come up with anything original." Schools make being wrong the worst thing you can do, and so "we are educating people out of their creative capacities." He offers a ranking he says holds worldwide: mathematics and languages at the top, humanities next, arts at the bottom — and inside the arts, art and music above drama and dance. He says the ranking is a nineteenth-century industrial artefact, and that it has turned schooling into "a protracted process of university entrance." He closes with the choreographer Gillian Lynne: in the 1930s her school wrote to her parents suspecting a learning disorder; the specialist left her alone in his office with the radio on, then told her mother, "Mrs. Lynne, Gillian isn't sick. She's a dancer. Take her to a dance school." She went on to choreograph Cats and The Phantom of the Opera.

The talk became the most-played in TED's history — 80,571,996 plays on TED's own site as of this writing.

What happened afterwards is more complicated than the talk. The version on that stage is not the same as the 243-page report Robinson himself wrote. In All Our Futures, the 1999 report he chaired for the UK government, one of the opening questions reads: "Is this a return to the progressive teaching ideas of the 1960s?" The answer is one word: "No." The report also states that "high standards in creative achievement require just as much rigour as traditional academic work," and that genuine creative achievement "draws from skills, knowledge and understanding. These are sometimes taught best through formal instruction." None of those qualifiers survive into the fifteen minutes. The report's claim about a hierarchy of subjects is bounded too — it refers specifically to England's National Curriculum after 1988, with a citation attached. On stage it became "every education system on earth."

Harder still: the one brain claim in the talk that can be checked directly — that the corpus callosum is "thicker in women," hence women's better multitasking — had been judged untenable by a meta-analysis of 49 studies nine years before he said it. Robinson didn't actually lean on it as evidence; what he said was, "There's a raft of research, but I know it from my personal life."

So the verdict is misused. His central plea — stop measuring every child with one ruler — holds up, and was even tested in the small: Creative Partnerships, the programme his report produced, reached more than five thousand English schools and over a million children between 2002 and 2011, and an independent evaluation found real but small gains in attainment before the funding was cut. What eighty million people actually remember is the version with every qualifier stripped out — the version his own 243 pages answer with "No."

The moment

Four minutes in, Ken Robinson has already landed several jokes at the conference's expense — the last four days have been wonderful, he says, "In fact, I'm leaving"; admit at a dinner party that you work in education and you can watch the blood run from your neighbour's face. Then he stops setting up punchlines and compresses the entire talk into one sentence:

My contention is that creativity now is as important in education as literacy, and we should treat it with the same status.— Ken Robinson, Do schools kill creativity?, TED2006

The room applauds. He says thank you. It applauds again. He waits it out, and says:

That was it, by the way. Thank you very much. (Laughter) So, 15 minutes left. (Laughter) "Well, I was born ... "— Ken Robinson, Do schools kill creativity?, TED2006

It's a joke, and it is also the most honest thing in the talk about the talk. The argument really does fit in one sentence. The remaining fifteen minutes are not argument; they are the work of giving that sentence pictures, laughs, and a shape you can still repeat at a dinner party three months later. That this became the most-played talk in TED's history — 80,571,996 plays on TED's own counter as of September 2026 — is not because it proved anything. It is because it did that job extraordinarily well. Which is the question this page keeps returning to: when an argument travels this way, which version of it arrives?

The man, and that year

Ken Robinson (born Liverpool, 4 March 1950; died in London of cancer on 21 August 2020, aged 70) was not a schoolteacher, a fact his critics would later reach for first. His route was a different one: director of the Arts in Schools Project from 1985 to 1989, Professor of Arts Education at the University of Warwick from 1989 to 2001, knighted in 2003 for services to the arts. So when he teases academics on stage for treating the body as "a form of transport for their heads" and then adds "and I used to be one, so there" — that isn't just self-deprecating rhetoric. He had left the job five years earlier.

What matters most: by February 2006 he had already made this argument officially, at far greater length and with far more care. In 1998 the UK government asked him to chair the National Advisory Committee on Creative and Cultural Education (NACCCE). In May 1999 the committee delivered All Our Futures: Creativity, Culture and Education — 243 pages, roughly 85,500 words, with citations, data and policy recommendations. And it was not a paper exercise: in 2002 Arts Council England launched Creative Partnerships in response to it, funded by the culture department and the education department, pairing schools with working artists. In other words, on the day he walked onto that stage he had a government report behind him and a national programme already four years old.

And the audience was not made of educators. TED2006 drew the technology, design and business elite — he refers to Al Gore speaking about ecology the previous night, and to a young performer named Sirena in the hall. It was his first TED, in a venue holding around a thousand people. He had not come to present the report. He had come to translate it into nineteen minutes. What that translation dropped is the whole of section four below.

Glossary

  • Corpus callosum: the bundle of nerve fibres joining the brain's two hemispheres. Its rearmost portion is the splenium, which is what the dispute on this page is actually about.
  • Meta-analysis: pooling the data from every study on a question and recomputing the answer. A single study can get a result by chance; a meta-analysis is much harder to fool.
  • Effect size (d): how big a difference is, measured in standard deviations. d = 0.2 is small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large; d near zero means the two groups are essentially indistinguishable. It is a separate question from statistical significance — with a big enough sample, even a trivial difference turns significant.
  • Key stage: England's school phases. Key stage 1 is roughly ages 5–7, 2 is 7–11, 3 is 11–14, 4 is 14–16 (ending in GCSE exams).
  • TTCT: the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking, a standardised test used since the 1960s to score "creativity." The argument in section five is fought entirely over its historical norming data.

The talk, in full

He starts with the room. Three themes have run through the four days: the sheer range of human creativity on display; the fact that nobody has any idea what happens next; and the extraordinary capacities children have. He twists the second into a number — "children starting school this year will be retiring in 2065" — and notes that nobody present, for all the expertise on parade, can say what the world looks like in five years, "and yet, we're meant to be educating them for it." This is the talk's one piece of straightforward load-bearing logic: if the future is unpredictable, betting everything on a single narrow ability is a bad strategy.

Then the one-sentence thesis, the applause, and the "15 minutes left" joke. Then he starts telling stories, and the order is designed.

Two children first, both short, both funny. The six-year-old drawing God: the teacher says nobody knows what God looks like, and the girl answers, "They will in a minute." Then his four-year-old son as Joseph in the Nativity — three four-year-old kings walk in with tea towels on their heads, get out of sequence, and the third one says, "Frank sent this." (Frankincense, heard by a four-year-old.) The work these two stories are doing arrives in the next paragraph:

What these things have in common is that kids will take a chance. If they don't know, they'll have a go. […] What we do know is, if you're not prepared to be wrong, you'll never come up with anything original […] And by the time they get to be adults, most kids have lost that capacity.— Ken Robinson, Do schools kill creativity?, TED2006

He aims the blame squarely at mechanisms rather than people: "We stigmatize mistakes. And we're now running national education systems where mistakes are the worst thing you can make. And the result is that we are educating people out of their creative capacities." He then paraphrases Picasso — all children are born artists, the problem is remaining an artist as you grow up — and delivers the line the talk is most quoted for: "we don't grow into creativity, we grow out of it. Or rather, we get educated out of it."

Then a stretch of pure performance with almost no argument in it: he mentions having lived near the birthplace of Shakespeare's father, and is "struck by a new thought" — you never picture Shakespeare having a father, or being seven, or sitting in somebody's English class getting "Must try harder," or being told by his dad to put the pencil down and go to bed. None of it has any evidential value, and it is the densest concentration of laughter in the talk — which is precisely how the fifteen minutes get filled.

Laughter spent, he puts forward the talk's real substantive claim, the hierarchy of subjects:

Every education system on earth has the same hierarchy of subjects. Every one. Doesn't matter where you go. […] At the top are mathematics and languages, then the humanities. At the bottom are the arts. Everywhere on earth.— Ken Robinson, Do schools kill creativity?, TED2006

There is a hierarchy inside the arts too: art and music usually outrank drama and dance. "There isn't an education system on the planet that teaches dance every day to children the way we teach them mathematics. Why? Why not?" Then the summary line that needs no explaining: as children grow up, "we start to educate them progressively from the waist up. And then we focus on their heads. And slightly to one side."

Next, the alien: if you visited public education from outside and asked who really succeeds by it, who collects all the brownie points, you would be forced to conclude that "the whole purpose of public education throughout the world is to produce university professors." Hanging off that is his undignified portrait of academics (living up in their heads, treating the body as transport, writhing off the beat in the disco on the last night of a residential conference) — turning the knife on his own former identity is how he keeps the criticism from reading as cruelty.

Then the historical explanation, the densest stretch of actual argument in the talk. There were essentially no public systems of education before the nineteenth century; "they all came into being to meet the needs of industrialism"; so the hierarchy rests on two ideas. First, that the subjects most useful for work belong at the top — which is why you were steered benignly away from the things you liked, on the grounds you'd never get a job doing that. Advice he calls "benign" and "profoundly mistaken." Second, academic ability has come to dominate our whole view of intelligence, "because the universities design the system in their image," turning public education into "a protracted process of university entrance." The consequence: many highly talented, creative people believe they are not, because what they were good at wasn't valued or was actively stigmatised. He adds a figure attributed to UNESCO — over the next thirty years more people worldwide will graduate than since the beginning of history — and a phrase: academic inflation.

Then: we know three things about intelligence. It is diverse (we think visually, in sound, kinesthetically, in abstract terms, in movement); it is dynamic (the brain isn't divided into compartments, and creativity most often comes from the interaction of different disciplinary ways of seeing); and it is distinct. Here he also gives his own definition of creativity: "the process of having original ideas that have value."

The "dynamic" point carries an aside that is the subject of section four: "By the way, there's a shaft of nerves that joins the two halves of the brain, called the corpus callosum. It's thicker in women." — followed by the routine about his wife cooking while phoning, talking to the kids, painting the ceiling and "doing open-heart surgery over here," and one crucial admission: "There's a raft of research, but I know it from my personal life."

Last comes the choreographer Gillian Lynne, who gets the longest stretch in the talk — she is the sole evidence for "intelligence is distinct," and the emotional landing point. In the 1930s her school wrote to her parents: "We think Gillian has a learning disorder." She couldn't concentrate; she fidgeted. "I think now they'd say she had ADHD. Wouldn't you? But this was the 1930s, and ADHD hadn't been invented at this point. It wasn't an available condition." Aged eight she was taken to a specialist, and sat in an oak-panelled room on her hands for twenty minutes while the man and her mother catalogued everything wrong with her at school. At the end the doctor said he needed to speak to her mother privately, and asked her to wait. On the way out he turned on the radio on his desk, and told the mother: "Just stand and watch her."

"Mrs. Lynne, Gillian isn't sick. She's a dancer. Take her to a dance school."— Ken Robinson relaying Gillian Lynne's account, Do schools kill creativity?, TED2006

Robinson says he asked what happened. She said the room was "full of people like me — people who couldn't sit still, people who had to move to think." She auditioned for the Royal Ballet School, became a soloist, and went on to choreograph Cats and The Phantom of the Opera. Then the closing blade: "Somebody else might have put her on medication and told her to calm down." Applause.

He ends by returning to Al Gore's ecology talk from the night before: we need a new conception of human ecology, because "our education system has mined our minds in the way that we strip-mine the earth for a particular commodity." He quotes Jonas Salk on what would happen if insects, and then humans, disappeared from the earth, and lands on the children: "our task is to educate their whole being, so they can face this future. […] we may not see this future, but they will. And our job is to help them make something of it."

Under the iceberg

The 243-page report is narrower than the nineteen minutes at every point that matters. All Our Futures (NACCCE, May 1999) was written under Robinson's own chairmanship. It isn't background material; it is the full version of this talk. And nearly all the difference between them is qualifiers.

One: half the definition is gone. On stage, creativity is "the process of having original ideas that have value." The report's definition is: "Imaginative activity fashioned so as to produce outcomes that are both original and of value." It spells out four characteristics of creative processes: they always involve thinking or behaving imaginatively; that imaginative activity is purposeful, directed at an objective; the process must generate something original; and the outcome must be of value in relation to the objective. The stage version keeps only the last two. What got dropped are exactly the two conditions that separate creativity from idle noodling.

Two: the report explicitly denies being a licence to let children roam. Its opening Q&A contains an item that reads as though written for the critics who would arrive a decade later:

5. Is this a return to the progressive teaching ideas of the 1960s? No. We are advocating a new balance between learning knowledge and skills and having the freedom to innovate and experiment.— NACCCE, All Our Futures, 1999, p. 15

The same page states that "high standards in creative achievement require just as much rigour as traditional academic work." Paragraph 163 goes further: "Genuine creative achievement, for example, draws from skills, knowledge and understanding. These are sometimes taught best through formal instruction. But formal instruction alone will not encourage creativity and may even stifle it" — and it asks readers to "move beyond divisive stereotypes of traditional or progressive teaching, and to see the need for elements of both." Even the most abusable line, that everyone is creative, is conditional in the original: all people are capable of creative achievement in some area, "provided the conditions are right and they have acquired the relevant knowledge and skills." In nineteen minutes, the words knowledge, skills, rigour and formal instruction do not appear once.

Three: "every education system on earth" only became global on stage. Handling the same point, the report's paragraph 132 is far more bounded: "In the existing National Curriculum there is an explicit hierarchy of subjects" — meaning England's statutory curriculum after 1988, and the legal distinction between core and foundation subjects — and it notes that successive revisions since 1988 have "progressively reduced" the requirements for the arts and humanities. It attaches evidence: a longitudinal study (Pollard et al., 1994) indicating that primary schools reduced provision for art while increasing time for science and technology. A dated, citation-backed observation about one country's statutory curriculum became an uncited assertion about the entire planet. That isn't a difference of detail: the first claim can be tested and refuted, the second can only be believed or not.

Four: the one hard fact in the talk that can be checked directly was already nine years dead when he said it. The claim that the corpus callosum is thicker in women, hence better multitasking, has a clear origin: a 1982 paper by de Lacoste-Utamsing and Holloway in Science, presented as the first reliable sex difference in human brain morphology. Bishop and Wahlsten's 1997 meta-analysis took that paper apart. The original sample was 28 brains, and the paper reported only 14 of them (nine male, five female). Total corpus callosum area was almost identical between the sexes (704.3 mm² for men, 708.3 mm² for women). What the argument was built on was the splenium: 218.3 mm² in women against 186.1 mm² in men — which works out to t = −1.85, two-tailed p = 0.0895, not significant by any conventional standard.

Then the pooled result across 49 independent studies from 1982 to 1994. For splenium area: 23 studies, combined effect size d = 0.04, 95% confidence interval −0.08 to 0.16 — straddling zero. The authors calculate that detecting a difference that small with 80% power would need 9,872 subjects per group; the 1982 paper used 14 brains. For contrast, the same table gives brain weight a combined effect size of d = 1.20 (men heavier — that difference is real, and large).

Their concluding sentence: "The widespread belief that women have a larger splenium than men and consequently think differently is untenable." That was published in 1997. Robinson said it on stage in 2006.

And the same meta-analysis contains a passage that reads like a description of this talk's own fate. The authors note that Science refused to publish failures to replicate the 1982 paper; that by February 1993 the 1982 article had been cited over 127 times, while one 1991 failure to replicate had never been cited at all. If journalists favour reporting sex differences and ignore findings of no difference, they write, "this could easily propagate a myth bolstered by apparent scientific legitimacy."

In fairness, Robinson did not present it as evidence — he said outright, "There's a raft of research, but I know it from my personal life." But that is exactly the point: a refuted finding survives precisely by being mentioned in passing, as something other than evidence. It occupies about fifteen seconds on stage, and nobody checks a citation in fifteen seconds.

What became of it

MISUSED · as of 2026-09

The central plea holds, and was even tested in the small. But what eighty million people remember is a version with every qualifier stripped out — a version his own 243-page report answers with "No." And the statistic most often cited to prove that schools kill creativity did not survive a re-analysis of 330,000 people.

2002–2011: the idea genuinely got a large-scale trial. Creative Partnerships, the programme his report produced, was launched by Arts Council England with money from the culture and education departments. By the time it ended it had run more than eight thousand projects in over five thousand English schools, reaching more than a million children and over ninety thousand teachers. That is worth pausing on: in education policy, an idea rarely gets field-tested at this scale at all.

2011: the independent evaluation came back "real, but small." The National Foundation for Educational Research (NFER), assessing the 2008–9 and 2009–10 school years, found several significant positive effects — speaking and listening at Key stage 1, average points score and English at Key stage 3, points score at Key stage 4 — while stating plainly that "the differences between the groups were relatively small." It offered a conversion: in the 2009 pupil-level analysis, the effect was equivalent to 13 per cent of participating pupils achieving, on average, one level higher in their Key stage 3 average points score than their background characteristics predicted. Its conclusion: Creative Partnerships was making "a small but valuable contribution to improving attainment at key stages 1, 3 and 4."

That "small but valuable" is the most worth remembering figure on this page. It refutes the claim that none of this was ever more than rhetoric, and it equally refutes the urgency on stage about having to rethink fundamental principles. In the same year, following the 2010 coalition government, the culture department and Arts Council England cut the funding, and activity in schools ended in the summer of 2011. The years the policy was dying are the years the talk was climbing to the top of TED's all-time list.

2013–2017: Britain's knowledge-first teachers made the talk their target. On 12 October 2013 the teacher Joe Kirby published "What Sir Ken Got Wrong," calling him "profoundly, spectacularly wrong" and going point by point: talent is not innate, multiple intelligences don't hold up, literacy and numeracy are preconditions for other learning, the thing actually preventing learning is disruption rather than conformity, and subject knowledge enables creativity rather than opposing it. On 6 April 2017, Carl Hendrick put it harder in TES, calling Robinson's argument "a canny bit of sophistry" for shifting responsibility from actual people onto an abstract system when — in his own words — "teachers are not beating a love of learning out of students but are working themselves to near-exhaustion to furnish kids with knowledge and skills."

There is an irony worth saying out loud here: what the critics attacked was the nineteen minutes, and on the point that creativity depends on knowledge, Robinson's report is on their side. Paragraph 163 says exactly that creative achievement draws from skills, knowledge and understanding. What came under attack was never the argument he wrote down; it was the one he performed. I did not find a public response from Robinson to either of these specific critiques, so I report only that, and won't supply an argument on his behalf.

2021: the "creativity crisis" statistic failed on recount. Anyone reaching for data to prove that schools kill creativity usually reaches for Kyung Hee Kim's 2011 analysis in the Creativity Research Journal: using TTCT historical norming data (roughly 300,000 tests, 1968–2008), she reported that creative thinking scores had declined significantly since 1990, and were static or falling from sixth grade onward. In 2021 Baptiste Barbot and Sameh Said-Metwaly redid the exercise in the Journal of Creative Behavior (vol. 55, no. 3, pp. 696–709). They criticised Kim's "problematic empirical decisions, statistical approaches, data representation, and interpretation of findings," then ran cross-temporal meta-analyses on over 330,000 participants across TTCT norming editions. Their finding: "no evidence for the notion of a generational decline of creativity, and even less so, for a 'creativity crisis'" — which they call "a counter-productive societal and scientific myth." They also concluded there is no general decline in creativity as individuals get older.

To be clear about what this does and does not do: it does not refute Robinson. He cited no creativity-test data on stage; his argument was institutional and moral, not quantitative. But it does mean the number most often produced in his support will not bear weight — and that the empirical version closest to his actual words, that schooling makes children less creative as they age, currently has no support either.

Robinson died in London of cancer on 21 August 2020, aged 70. The Element (2009) is the book he told the audience he was writing under the title Epiphany — he later confirmed himself that Epiphany had been the working title. The talk keeps growing: TED's counter now reads 80,571,996.

So the verdict lands on misused rather than refuted, because three things are true at once. His central plea — that a system measuring every child with one ruler exacts a real cost — has not been refuted by any evidence, and picked up small but real support inside Creative Partnerships. His complete argument, all 243 pages of it, is considerably more rigorous than his critics assumed. And the version actually circulating in the world has had the qualifiers, the country, the citations and the words "knowledge and skills" stripped out of it, leaving one sentence fit for a poster. What made this talk succeed is the same thing that got it misused.
